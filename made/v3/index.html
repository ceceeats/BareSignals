From recipe to vialV3

Every vial starts as a recipe and ends as a white cake of powder. In between: the letters, the build, the cleanup, the drying, the test. Know how a peptide is made and a COA stops being a stamp. It becomes a list of what was checked, and of what was not.

Part one · The recipe

The recipe comes first

A peptide's recipe is its sequence: which amino acids, in which order. It is always written front to back, from the N end to the C end. Change one letter, or one end, and it is a different molecule with a different weight. This is Selank, seven letters, in the shapes from the Aminos page.

T K P R P G P · N end left, C end right

NA and Amidate: caps on the two ends

The names with NA and Amidate are the same letters with both ends capped. Nothing in the middle changes.

NA · N-acetylAn acetyl group (a small two-carbon piece) is stuck onto the front end. Adds 42 to the weight and removes the front's plus charge.
Amidate · C-amideThe back end's acid group is swapped for an amide (NH₂). Takes 1 off the weight and removes the back's minus charge.

Why bother. Enzymes in blood and tissue called exopeptidases chew peptides from the loose ends, one letter at a time. Caps hide the ends, so the chain should last longer. An uncharged end may also slip through membranes more easily. That is the reasoning behind NA Selank Amidate and NA Semax Amidate; human data comparing capped and uncapped versions is thin (reported, not proven).

On a bead, the amide end comes from the bead itself: a bead type called Rink amide releases the chain with NH₂ on the end.16 The acetyl cap is added as the very last step. A cell's ribosome cannot do either directly, another reason these come off beads.

Look-alikes: same letters, different molecule

Some names look like twins on a sticker. Pick a pair; the orange marks are the only differences.

Capped ends seen elsewhere in the Library: TB-500 frag 17–23 starts with Ac. Ipamorelin, SS-31, Oxytocin, Kisspeptin-10, Sermorelin and CJC-1295 end in NH₂. A COA's mass line has to match the capped weight, not the plain one.

Part two · The letters

Where each amino acid comes from

Two routes, two answers

Grown in cells: nobody adds amino acids. The bacteria or yeast make their own from the sugar they are fed, and their ribosome links them.

Stacked on beads: nobody makes the letters on site either. They arrive as ready-made white powders, already capped, bought by the kilo from chemical suppliers. The lab only links them.

So where do those powders come from? Mostly from the same trick as the first route: bacteria fed sugar. The world makes millions of tonnes of amino acids a year, most of it for food and animal feed. Peptide makers buy a tiny, extra-clean slice.9

Sugar in, bacteria grow, the broth is filtered, the amino acid is pulled out by ion exchange and crystallised.

Then the caps go on

A plain amino acid would link to anything, including itself. So before it reaches a peptide lab, each one is given guards:

  1. A front cap, Fmoc. Blocks the front so the letter can only link once. It comes off with a mild base (piperidine), once per round, at the lab.16
  2. Side-chain guards. Letters with reactive side groups get extra guards: tBu, Boc, Trt, Pbf. These survive every round and come off only with strong acid (TFA) at the very end.
  3. Two caps, two removers. Base takes the front cap off every round, acid takes the guards off once. That split is the whole trick of the stack.

Special letters cost more. Mirror forms (D-amino acids) and letters the body never makes (Aib, Nle, Nal, Dmt) are made by chemistry or enzymes in small batches. A peptide holding them is pricier per round, before anything else.

Short and still pricey: SS-31

SS-31 is only four letters: D-Arg, Dmt, Lys, Phe, with an amide end. Short should mean cheap. Three things push it up:

  • Two of the four letters are special. D-Arg is a mirror form and Dmt (2′,6′-dimethyltyrosine) is a lab-made tyrosine the body never builds; Dmt is reported as costly to make cleanly.18 Arg also needs one of the bulkiest guards (Pbf).
  • People use a lot of it. Reported doses run in milligrams a day (5 to 10 mg in the Dosage page), against micrograms for BPC-157. A month of SS-31 uses many times more powder, so the price per protocol feels high even when the price per mg is not.
  • Demand and attention. Elamipretide, the medicine name, received FDA accelerated approval in September 2025 for Barth syndrome.18

Rule: length drives cost, but special letters, caps, dose size and demand can override it.

Part three · The build

Two ways to make a chain

Let a living cell build it, or stack it chemically on a tiny bead.

Recombinant · bacteria or yeast

Drawing, not a photo. E. coli is a rod about 2 µm long: 500 in a row would span 1 mm.

  1. Write the gene. The DNA code for the peptide is put into a small ring of DNA, a plasmid (the orange stretch in the drawing).
  2. Give it to a cell. Usually the gut bacterium E. coli, or a yeast (Saccharomyces or Pichia). Big proteins like antibodies use hamster ovary cells (CHO).
  3. Let them multiply. Billions of cells grow in a steel tank (a fermenter), fed sugar and air. Each copy of the cell carries the ring.
  4. The ribosome builds it. Same machine as in your body: it reads the copied code three letters at a time and links the chain from the N end to the C end.
  5. Harvest. Bacteria are broken open to get the peptide out. Yeast can be made to push it out into the broth.
  6. Finish it. Some medicines get a chemical add-on after harvest. Brand semaglutide's chain is grown in yeast, then a fatty side chain is attached so it lasts a week.1

What comes along: bits of the cell itself. Host cell proteins, host DNA and, from bacteria, endotoxin (Part eleven). All of it has to be washed out.

Best for long chains made only of natural letters. Most peptides under about 30 letters are cheaper and simpler to stack chemically.11

Solid phase synthesis (SPPS) · the stacking

Not a beaker. The beads sit in a glass or plastic tube with a filter at the bottom. Liquid goes in, the beads soak, liquid drains out through the filter, the beads stay. The chain is a solid stuck to a solid, bathed again and again in liquid. Watch it run:

Same steps, more rounds

Every letter is one round: uncap, wash, link, wash. Nothing is skipped for a long chain. At 99% per link:

Simplified: about nine liquid changes a round and 30 to 60 min a round on a standard machine; fast machines and big plants differ. Across 40 real peptide processes, making 1 kg of peptide used about 13,000 kg of solvents, reagents and water, against a few hundred for a typical pill drug.12

Why longer usually costs more: more rounds, more solvent, more failed chains, and near twins that sit closer to the right one, so the cleanup throws more away. Not always: SS-31 (Part two) is short and pricey.

Why every round counts

No link is perfect. If each round works 99% of the time, the misses add up with every letter. Pick a link rate (the peptide follows the one picked above):

Of 38 recently approved small peptide medicines, 31 are made fully by chemistry; the other 7 use cells for some part.11 Research vials lean even harder to beads.

Brand medicines are listed by their label route. A research vial of the same name can be made a different way: research semaglutide, for example, is usually reported as stacked chemically, the route regulators expect for copies of synthetic peptides.11 Ask the seller.

Part four · The crude

What falls off the bead

At the end, strong acid (TFA) cuts every chain off its bead and strips the guards. The liquid is poured into ice-cold ether and the peptide crashes out as a solid: the crude. It is the right chain mixed with its mistakes. Here they are on Semax (M E H F P G P). Tap one.

Which mistakes happen most

No one publishes an average across all peptides, and it depends on the letters. What reviews and tests agree on:

    • 10 of 13 impurities found in one 15-letter test peptide were single missing letters.13 BPC-157 is also 15 letters.
    • 44% of research peptides ordered at 95% purity or better actually met it, when a university lab retested them. Most impurities were missing letters, and one sample's main molecule was not the ordered peptide at all.14
    • +16 oxidation has been documented in approved medicines: an oxygen on the tryptophan of leuprolide and on the methionine of eledoisin.13 Semax starts with methionine.
    • D- mirror swaps are most common at histidine, cysteine, aspartic acid and serine.15 Semax and Ipamorelin both carry a histidine.

    The hardest step: the link. Missing letters, extra letters and mirror swaps are all born there, and they are the near twins that are hardest to cut away later. Oxidation and deamidation are different: they keep forming in storage, after the COA was written.

    Part five · Purify

    Keep the peak, cut the rest

    The crude is dissolved in water and acetonitrile and pushed through a steel column packed with greasy silica (HPLC, usually a C18 column). A slow shift from water to solvent pulls each molecule off at its own moment, by how greasy it is. A UV light at about 214 to 220 nm watches the exit and draws a peak each time something passes.

    The right chain makes the tallest peak. Its near twins sit on either side: molecules almost identical to the peptide, usually one letter short, one extra or one guard left on, so they leave the column a few seconds earlier or later. The lab collects a slice around the middle, the cut. Purity is a business choice as much as a science one.

    Part six · Dose, dry, seal

    From a liquid to a white cake

    The kept slices are pooled: the peptide is now dissolved in liquid, and stays liquid until the very last steps. Lyophilized just means freeze dried.

    Vials are pressed shut inside the dryer itself, before air is let back in.17 Many are closed under a partial vacuum, often with nitrogen, which is why a fresh vial can pull water in on its own when you add it.

    The whole trip, solid and liquid

    • Wet solidChain on beads, soaked and drained every round.
    • LiquidCut free in acid (TFA).
    • SolidCrashed out in cold ether: the crude powder.
    • LiquidDissolved again, through the HPLC, slices pooled, salt swapped, diluted to the dose.
    • FrozenFilled into vials and frozen.
    • SolidIce pulled off as vapour: the cake. Then sealed.
    • LiquidBack to liquid only when someone adds water (the Water page).

    What is left in the vial: the peptide, its salt partner (TFA or acetate), some water the powder pulls from air, any filler (mannitol keeps the cake's shape), and whatever impurities survived the cut. "10 mg" on a label rarely means 10 mg of pure peptide. Part nine does the math.

    Part seven · Purity, seen

    What 99.9%, 98% and 90% look like

    HPLC purity is the share of peptide molecules that are the right chain. Slide it and look inside the vial.

    98%

    You will never see these. Impurities in a clear vial are dissolved molecules, the same size as the peptide: about a millionth of a millimetre. No eye or ordinary microscope can see them. The dots are drawn huge so you can count. Specks you can see are a different problem (clumps, particles, contamination).

    How small

      What the purity number cannot see

      HPLC purity only compares things that show up as UV peaks: peptides and their near twins. It is blind to:

      Invisible to purity

      • Water and salt partners (TFA, acetate)
      • Endotoxin
      • Heavy metals
      • Leftover solvents
      • Bacteria or fungi
      • A wrong molecule that exits at the same moment

      So a 99% vial can still

      • hold only 70% peptide by weight
      • carry endotoxin
      • be the wrong peptide, if nobody ran mass spec
      • be non-sterile

      Medicines also limit each single impurity, not only the total. A grey market COA almost always gives one total number.

      Part eight · The COA

      Certificate of Analysis, line by line

      A COA is a lab's report on one batch (the lot). Not on every vial, not on the brand. Tap a line. Colour shows how often each line appears on research peptide COAs.

      Usually thereSometimesRarely
      Certificate of AnalysisExample · not a real lot

      A COA covers

      • That one lot, on the test date
      • Only the tests printed on it
      • The lab's own method and limits

      A COA does not cover

      • The vial in hand, unless the lot number matches
      • Shipping heat, storage, time since testing
      • Any test that is not on the page
      • How you mix, store or draw it

      Read it like a skeptic

      • Lot number on the COA matches the lot on the vial in hand.
      • Mass spec found weight matches the theoretical weight for that sequence (Part nine).
      • A chromatogram picture is attached, not just a number.
      • The picture is not the same one reused across different compounds.
      • Who tested it: the maker (in-house) or an outside lab you can contact.
      • Every lot reading exactly 99.9% is a pattern, not a result.
      • Endotoxin and sterility lines exist if it is meant for injection.
      Part nine · Weight

      Two weights that matter

      Molecular weight is a fingerprint. Every sequence has one exact weight, the sum of its letters minus the water lost at each link. Mass spec (MS) weighs the molecules in the batch. If the found weight matches the theoretical one, the chain is probably the right one.

      Weights in daltons (g/mol). A missing proline drops 97.1, a missing glycine 57.1. MS sees that instantly; HPLC may not.

      The label weight is usually powder weight. Part of that powder is the salt partner and water. The share that is real peptide is the net peptide content, usually 60 to 90%.4 Purity and net content are two different numbers and both shrink what you actually hold.

      Part ten · pH

      Why acid and base matter

      024678101214
      BAC water, 4.5 to 7
      Blood, 7.35 to 7.45

      Dissolving

      Every peptide has a pH where its charges cancel (its isoelectric point). Near that pH it dissolves worst and can turn cloudy or clump. That is why some peptides need a slightly acidic water to go clear.

      Shelf life

      Higher pH speeds some breakdown, like asparagine turning into aspartate (deamidation). Wrong pH plus warmth plus time equals a weaker vial with more impurities than its COA shows.

      Sting

      Leftover TFA makes a solution more acidic. More acid, more sting at the injection site. A COA may list pH or the salt form.

      The water

      Bacteriostatic water is sterile water with 0.9% benzyl alcohol, pH about 5.7 (range 4.5 to 7).5 The alcohol slows bacteria after the vial is opened. It does not sterilise a dirty vial.

      Signal: a mixed vial that stays cloudy, has floating bits, or changes colour is telling you something changed. Clear and colourless is the baseline for most peptides. GHK-Cu is the known exception: copper makes it blue.

      Part eleven · Toxins

      What purity never measured

      Endotoxin is a piece of dead bacteria. It is a fat-sugar molecule (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) from the outer wall of gram-negative bacteria like E. coli. Bacteria shed a little while alive and a lot when they die.6 So a product can be sterile, zero living bacteria, and still be full of endotoxin.

      Why it matters. The immune system reads LPS as an infection. Small amounts in the blood bring fever, chills, shaking, aches and headache within hours. Large amounts can drop blood pressure into shock.

      Why it is hard to remove. It survives heat. Normal autoclaving does not reliably destroy it; it takes dry heat around 250°C for 30 minutes.6 Sterile filters catch bacteria, not reliably their endotoxin. It has to be kept out from the start: clean water, clean glass, clean hands.

      The limit. Injected medicines are held under 5 endotoxin units (EU) per kg of body weight per hour.3 Tested with LAL, a reagent made from horseshoe crab blood that clots when endotoxin is present.

      Read an endotoxin line

      For understanding a COA line only. "< 1 EU/mg" means under the test's floor, not zero. No endotoxin line at all means nobody measured it.

      Heavy metals come from the process: metal catalysts, reagents, water, steel equipment. They are not counted in purity and need a separate test (ICP-MS). The risk is mostly slow: metals build up with repeated use, so small amounts over months matter more than one shot.

      Limits are ICH Q3D permitted daily exposures for injected (parenteral) medicines, total from every source per day.7 Injected limits are tighter than oral ones because nothing is filtered by the gut first. Research peptide COAs rarely include this test.

      Bead chemistry runs on solvents: DMF and NMP to link, DCM to wash, piperidine to uncap, TFA to cut free, ether to crash the crude out, acetonitrile in the HPLC. Freeze drying removes most of it. "Most" is what a residual solvent test (gas chromatography, GC) puts a number on.

      DMF and NMP are reproductive toxins in high exposure; DCM is a suspected carcinogen. At trace levels in a single vial the concern is low; across months of daily injections it is an unknown when nobody tested.

      Medicines follow ICH Q3C solvent limits. Research COAs almost never list residual solvents.

      Powder is not automatically sterile. Sterility comes from the process: filtering the solution through a 0.22 µm membrane before filling, in a clean room, then sealing. A sterility test (USP <71>) checks that nothing grows from samples of the lot.

      Bacteria or fungi in a vial cause local infection: a hot, swollen, painful lump that gets worse over days, sometimes an abscess. Each puncture of the stopper is also a chance to bring something in, which is why bacteriostatic water exists.

      Filtration removes living bacteria. It does not reliably remove endotoxin or metals. Three different problems, three different tests.

      Part twelve · Reactions

      When the body answers back

      Timing tells you a lot. A reaction can come from the peptide itself, an impurity, clumped aggregates, the preservative or contamination. Tolerating last month's vial says nothing about a new lot.

      Minutes to hours

      Injection site

      Redness, itch, a small bump or sting. Common, local, fades in a day or two.

      30 min to a few hours

      Fever-type

      Chills, shaking, fever, aches, headache, nausea. Classic picture of endotoxin. Needs medical attention if strong, or if blood pressure feels low (dizzy, faint).

      Seconds to 30 min

      Allergic

      Hives spreading past the site, swelling of lips, tongue or throat, hoarse voice, wheeze, dizziness, pale clammy skin. This can become anaphylaxis.

      1 to 3 days

      Infection

      The spot gets hotter, bigger and more painful instead of better. Red streaks, pus or fever. Needs a doctor.

      Anaphylaxis is an emergency

      Throat or tongue swelling, trouble breathing or swallowing, wheeze, sudden dizziness, collapse. Can start within minutes.8

      1. Use an adrenaline auto-injector straight away if one is prescribed.
      2. Call emergency services: 112 (Indonesia, EU), 911 (US), 999 (UK). Say "anaphylaxis".
      3. Lie down with legs raised. Sit up if breathing is hard. Do not stand or walk, even if it eases.
      4. Second adrenaline dose after 5 minutes if no better.

      Educational, not medical advice. If something feels wrong after an injection, get help early. Bring the vial and its lot number.
