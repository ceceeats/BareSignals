How peptides are madeV9

A peptide is a recipe built from tiny bricks. This page follows one from the recipe card to the sealed vial, and shows what can go wrong at every step.

The whole path, tap a step

Part one · The recipe

A peptide is a recipe of bricks

LikeLego. There are 20 kinds of brick. Every brick has the same plug and the same socket; only the piece on top differs. Snap them in one exact order and you have a peptide.

The next brick's plug (front, N end) snaps into the socket (back, C end) of the one before. The plug and socket are used up and become a peptide bond; one water drops out.

Bricks, bonds, ends

Brick is this page's word for an amino acid. Inside a chain, chemists call each one a residue.

Every link is a peptide bond. That is how all peptides are joined. So every chain has exactly one free front (N end) and one free back (C end). A few peptides also loop back on themselves: MT-2 closes a ring, oxytocin has a bridge between its two cysteines (C).

Why left to right? Chemists always write a peptide front (N) on the left and back (C) on the right, like reading. That is normal. On a bead it is built the other way round, back first, so pictures of the bead look flipped. That is real, not a drawing mistake.

      Part three · The build

      Which peptide, which way

      Almost every research peptide is stacked by machine. Cells only win for long chains of natural bricks. Each tag wears the peptide’s main Library colour; tap one to open it.

      Brand medicines are listed by their label route. The same molecule can be made two ways: brand semaglutide is grown in yeast, while research-vial semaglutide is usually reported as stacked on beads, the route regulators expect for copies of synthetic peptides.11 Of 38 recent small peptide medicines, 31 are made fully by chemistry.11 Khavinson bioregulators are the short peptides (2 to 4 bricks) developed by Vladimir Khavinson’s institute in St Petersburg: Cardiogen, Vilon, Epitalon’s cousins.

      Stacked on beads

      Likebuilding a Lego tower one brick at a time on a grain of sand, then washing the sand between every brick. Not in a beaker: in a tube with a filter at the bottom. Liquid in, soak, drain, repeat. The beads never leave.

      The trick: two temporary caps

      Likethe cap on a marker. A bare brick would snap onto anything, even itself. Caps make it snap once, in the right place.

      Front cap (Fmoc): off every roundSide guard: off once, at the endPermanent end caps (Ac, NH₂, Part one): stay for good

      Two caps, two removers: base takes the front cap off every round, acid takes the guards off once. That split is the whole trick of the stack.16

      Same steps, more rounds

      About nine liquid changes and 30 to 60 minutes a round on a standard machine. Across 40 real peptide processes, 1 kg of peptide used about 13,000 kg of solvents, reagents and water, against a few hundred for a pill drug.12

      Why longer usually costs more: more rounds, more solvent, more broken chains, and twins closer to the right one, so the cleanup throws more away. Not always: SS-31 is short and pricey.

      Grown in cells

      Likea brewery. Give a cell the gene, feed it sugar, and billions of copies build the chain for you with their own ribosomes. Three kinds of cell do most of the work.

      What comes along: bits of the cell itself. Host proteins, host DNA and, from bacteria, endotoxin (Part twelve). All of it has to be washed out. Brand semaglutide's chain is grown in yeast, then the fatty tail is added by chemistry.1

      Part four · The crude

      What falls off the bead

      Acid cuts every chain off its bead, then ice-cold ether turns the liquid into a powder: the crude. It is the right chain mixed with its mistakes. Here they all are on one peptide.

      Read every chain front (N, left) to back (C, right). Chains grow from the back, so when growth stops early it is the front that is missing.

      Count them: one batch, before cleanup

      Every snap has a small miss rate. Here is a pretend 10 mg batch straight off the bead, before any cleanup. Part eight shows the same idea after the cleanup, in the vial you would buy.

      Each snap works

        Illustrative model: each snap fails at the rate picked, plus a small fixed share of guard, mirror and oxygen mistakes. Real crude depends on the bricks. The peptide follows the one picked in Part three.

        Meet each mistake

        Weights are in daltons, the unit for molecules (one hydrogen atom is about 1). A COA's mass line compares these weights, which is how most mistakes get caught.

        Mirror swap: left hand, right hand

        Every brick except glycine comes in two mirror shapes, like your hands: same parts, mirror arrangement. The body builds only with left-hand bricks, called L. A brick that flips to the right-hand form, D, weighs exactly the same, which is why it hides from mass spec, but it can sit in the receptor the wrong way.

        Some peptides use D bricks on purpose (Ipamorelin, MT-2) to slow enzymes down. A swap is a D where the recipe says L.

        Which mistakes happen most

          • 10 of 13 impurities found in one 15-brick test peptide were single missing bricks.13 BPC-157 is also 15 bricks.
          • 44% of research peptides ordered at 95% purity or better actually met it when a university lab retested them. One sample's main molecule was not the ordered peptide at all.14
          • +16 oxidation is documented in approved medicines: leuprolide and eledoisin.13
          • D swaps are most common at histidine, cysteine, aspartic acid and serine.15

          The hardest step: the snap. Missing, extra and mirror-swapped bricks are all born there, and they are the twins hardest to cut away. Oxidation and deamidation keep forming in storage, after the COA was written.

          Part five · Purify

          Keep the peak, cut the rest

          Likea race where every molecule runs the same track. Greasier runners are slower. The lab keeps the runners who cross the line in the middle of the pack.

          The crude is dissolved and pushed through a steel column packed with greasy silica (HPLC). A UV light at the exit draws a peak each time something passes.

          The right chain makes the tallest peak. Its near twins (one brick missing, one extra, a guard left on) leave a few seconds before or after it. The lab keeps a slice around the middle: the cut. Purity is a business choice as much as a science one.

          Part six · Dose, dry, seal

          From a liquid to a white cake

          Likeinstant coffee. Make the drink, freeze it, then pull the ice off as vapour. What stays is a dry cake that turns back into the drink when water is added. Lyophilized just means freeze dried.

          Drawn as a 3 mL vial. Tap a vial for its step.

          Vials are pressed shut inside the dryer itself, before air is let back in.17 Many are closed under a partial vacuum, often with nitrogen, which is why a fresh vial can pull water in on its own.

          The whole trip, solid and liquid

          • Wet solidChain on beads, soaked and drained every round.
          • LiquidCut free in acid (TFA).
          • SolidCrashed out in cold ether: the crude powder.
          • LiquidDissolved again, through the HPLC, slices pooled, salt swapped, diluted to the dose.
          • FrozenFilled into vials and frozen.
          • SolidIce pulled off as vapour: the cake. Then sealed.
          • LiquidBack to liquid only when someone adds water (the Water page).

          What is left in the vial: the peptide, its salt partner (TFA or acetate), a little water, maybe a filler, and whatever impurities survived the cut. Part seven covers the helpers and what wears it down; Part ten does the weight math.

          Part seven · Excipients and stability

          What else is in the vial, and what wears it down

          Likethe box, the foam and the little silica packet that ship a Lego set. None of it is the toy. All of it keeps the toy whole until it is opened.

          Excipients are everything in a vial that is not the peptide: helpers added on purpose to hold its shape, its pH and its cleanliness. Medicines list every one on the label. Research vials usually list none.

                  Part nine · The COA

                  Certificate of Analysis, line by line

                  A COA is a lab's report on one batch (the lot). Not on every vial, not on the brand. Tap a line. Colour shows how often each line appears on research peptide COAs.

                  Usually thereSometimesRarely
                  Certificate of AnalysisExample · not a real lot

                  A COA covers

                  • That one lot, on the test date
                  • Only the tests printed on it
                  • The lab's own method and limits

                  A COA does not cover

                  • The vial in hand, unless the lot number matches
                  • Shipping heat, storage, time since testing
                  • Any test that is not on the page
                  • How you mix, store or draw it

                  Read it like a skeptic

                  • Lot number on the COA matches the lot on the vial in hand.
                  • Mass spec found weight matches the theoretical weight for that sequence (Part ten).
                  • A chromatogram picture is attached, not just a number.
                  • The picture is not the same one reused across different compounds.
                  • Who tested it: the maker (in-house) or an outside lab you can contact.
                  • Every lot reading exactly 99.9% is a pattern, not a result.
                  • Endotoxin and sterility lines exist if it is meant for injection.
                  Part ten · Weight

                  Two weights that matter

                  Molecular weight is a fingerprint. Every sequence has one exact weight, the sum of its letters minus the water lost at each link. Mass spec (MS) weighs the molecules in the batch. If the found weight matches the theoretical one, the chain is probably the right one.

                  This is BPC-157, 15 letters. Tap any letter to drop it, the way a missed link would.

                  Weights in daltons (g/mol). A missing proline drops 97.1, a missing glycine 57.1. MS sees that instantly; HPLC may not.

                  The label weight is usually powder weight. Part of that powder is the salt partner and water. The share that is real peptide is the net peptide content, usually 60 to 90%.4 Purity and net content are two different numbers and both shrink what you actually hold.

                  Dissolving

                  Every peptide has a pH where its charges cancel (its isoelectric point). Near that pH it dissolves worst and can turn cloudy or clump. That is why some peptides need a slightly acidic water to go clear.

                  Shelf life

                  Higher pH speeds breakdown such as deamidation. The full list of threats, and which peptides have soft spots, is in Part seven.

                  Sting

                  Leftover TFA makes a solution more acidic. More acid, more sting at the injection site. A COA may list pH or the salt form.

                  Part twelve · Toxins

                  What purity never measured

                  Endotoxin is a piece of dead bacteria. It is a fat-sugar molecule (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) from the outer wall of gram-negative bacteria like E. coli. Bacteria shed a little while alive and a lot when they die.6 So a product can be sterile, zero living bacteria, and still be full of endotoxin.

                  Why it matters. The immune system reads LPS as an infection. Small amounts in the blood bring fever, chills, shaking, aches and headache within hours. Large amounts can drop blood pressure into shock.

                  Why it is hard to remove. It survives heat. Normal autoclaving does not reliably destroy it; it takes dry heat around 250°C for 30 minutes.6 Sterile filters catch bacteria, not reliably their endotoxin. It has to be kept out from the start: clean water, clean glass, clean hands.

                  The limit. Injected medicines are held under 5 endotoxin units (EU) per kg of body weight per hour.3 Tested with LAL, a reagent made from horseshoe crab blood that clots when endotoxin is present.

                  Read an endotoxin line

                  For understanding a COA line only. "< 1 EU/mg" means under the test's floor, not zero. No endotoxin line at all means nobody measured it.

                  Heavy metals come from the process: metal catalysts, reagents, water, steel equipment. They are not counted in purity and need a separate test (ICP-MS). The risk is mostly slow: metals build up with repeated use, so small amounts over months matter more than one shot.

                  Limits are ICH Q3D permitted daily exposures for injected (parenteral) medicines, total from every source per day.7 Injected limits are tighter than oral ones because nothing is filtered by the gut first. Research peptide COAs rarely include this test.

                  Bead chemistry runs on solvents: DMF and NMP to link, DCM to wash, piperidine to uncap, TFA to cut free, ether to crash the crude out, acetonitrile in the HPLC. Freeze drying removes most of it. "Most" is what a residual solvent test (gas chromatography, GC) puts a number on.

                  DMF and NMP are reproductive toxins in high exposure; DCM is a suspected carcinogen. At trace levels in a single vial the concern is low; across months of daily injections it is an unknown when nobody tested.

                  Medicines follow ICH Q3C solvent limits. Research COAs almost never list residual solvents.

                  Powder is not automatically sterile. Sterility comes from the process: filtering the solution through a 0.22 µm membrane before filling, in a clean room, then sealing. A sterility test (USP <71>) checks that nothing grows from samples of the lot.

                  Bacteria or fungi in a vial cause local infection: a hot, swollen, painful lump that gets worse over days, sometimes an abscess. Each puncture of the stopper is also a chance to bring something in, which is why bacteriostatic water exists.

                  Filtration removes living bacteria. It does not reliably remove endotoxin or metals. Three different problems, three different tests.

                  Part thirteen · Reactions

                  When the body answers back

                  Timing tells you a lot. A reaction can come from the peptide itself, an impurity, clumped aggregates, the preservative or contamination. Tolerating last month's vial says nothing about a new lot.

                  Minutes to hours

                  Injection site

                  Redness, itch, a small bump or sting. Common, local, fades in a day or two.

                  30 min to a few hours

                  Fever-type

                  Chills, shaking, fever, aches, headache, nausea. Classic picture of endotoxin. Needs medical attention if strong, or if blood pressure feels low (dizzy, faint).

                  Seconds to 30 min

                  Allergic

                  Hives spreading past the site, swelling of lips, tongue or throat, hoarse voice, wheeze, dizziness, pale clammy skin. This can become anaphylaxis.

                  1 to 3 days

                  Infection

                  The spot gets hotter, bigger and more painful instead of better. Red streaks, pus or fever. Needs a doctor.

                  Anaphylaxis is an emergency

                  Throat or tongue swelling, trouble breathing or swallowing, wheeze, sudden dizziness, collapse. Can start within minutes.8

                  1. Use an adrenaline auto-injector straight away if one is prescribed.
                  2. Call emergency services: 112 (Indonesia, EU), 911 (US), 999 (UK). Say "anaphylaxis".
                  3. Lie down with legs raised. Sit up if breathing is hard. Do not stand or walk, even if it eases.
                  4. Second adrenaline dose after 5 minutes if no better.

                  Educational, not medical advice. If something feels wrong after an injection, get help early. Bring the vial and its lot number.
