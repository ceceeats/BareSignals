Antibiotics & your gut

The post-antibiotic microbiome · what is lost, what comes back, what helps

Draft Version 122 Nov 202653 sources
Gut community Gut lining Immune training C. diff risk

What it is. About 38 trillion bacteria live in you, nearly all in the colon, and a course of antibiotics clears part of that community along with the infection. The gap, not the loss, is what causes the trouble.

What studies found. Most healthy adults are back near baseline in 2 to 8 weeks with nothing done. But 9 species everyone carried were still missing in most people at 6 months, and clindamycin left a trace at 2 years. Which drug you took matters far more than what you take afterwards.

What people report. Nothing yet. This first pass collected studies only; first-hand reports are the next pass, and the page says so rather than filling the gap with something weaker.

Watch out. Watery diarrhoea three or more times a day, fever, or blood, during a course or up to three months after, needs a doctor the same day, not a probiotic.

What that community was doing for you

Guarding the door. The residents hold every scrap of food and wall space, so an invader has nowhere to settle. This is the job you only notice when it stops.

Feeding your gut wall. Fibre gets fermented into butyrate, which is the main fuel of your colon lining cells, helps keep the wall sealed, and calms inflammation. The makers are Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale, Coprococcus, and antibiotics hit them hard.7,4

Rewriting bile acids. Gut bacteria convert the liver's bile acids into secondary forms, and those secondary forms are what stop C. difficile spores from sprouting. After broad antibiotics they fell about a thousandfold.5

Training your immune system. In the same study gut bacterial load dropped ten-thousandfold, inflammatory signals rose, and in people with low starting antibody levels the response to a flu vaccine was weaker.5

Making vitamins. Vitamin K2 and some B vitamins. How much of that you actually absorb is still argued over.

2 What the studies found

The recovery studies, with numbers

Human

12 healthy men, the worst case on purpose. Four days of meropenem, gentamicin and vancomycin together, then shotgun sequencing out to 180 days. Early blooms of Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus faecalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum; Bifidobacterium and butyrate makers lost. Composition was near baseline within about 1.5 months. Nine species that every single man carried before were still undetectable in most of them at six months, and species carrying beta-lactam resistance genes were favoured.4

Human

3 people, two courses, 10 months, sampled 52 to 56 times each. Ciprofloxacin. Diversity fell within 3 to 4 days. Recovery began about a week after each course but was, in the authors' word, often incomplete. The second course did not play out like the first. By the end, each person had settled into a stable state different from their starting one, and all three said their gut felt normal throughout.3,2

RCT

66 healthy adults, randomised to one of four drugs or placebo, followed 12 months. The gut was severely affected by most of them, butyrate makers were strongly underrepresented for months, and resistance genes were enriched. Saliva bounced back fast; the gut did not.7

Human

4 people on clindamycin, 4 controls, 2 years. Bacteroides diversity fell sharply and never returned to its original composition in two years. Resistance genes stayed high the whole time. The controls barely changed. This is the longest individual follow-up in the field and the reason clindamycin has the reputation it does.28

Review

31 studies of the antibiotics actually prescribed in primary care. Most people recover to baseline within a few weeks; some studies show effects lasting 2 to 6 months. Penicillin V, nitrofurantoin and amoxicillin had very little effect. Clarithromycin lowered Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus for up to 5 weeks.32

Human

18 adults on cefprozil: who gets hit depends on where you start. The reshaping was predictable from each person's starting microbiome, and the subgroup that began with lower diversity got blooms of Enterobacter cloacae, an opportunist. Resistance genes appeared that had been undetectable before.35

Cohort

About 15,000 Swedish adults, prescriptions linked to one stool sample. Use 4 to 8 years earlier still showed up in today's microbiome, strongest for clindamycin, fluoroquinolones and flucloxacillin, and present even after a single course. Diversity fell with each extra course, most steeply for the first two. Read it carefully: this is observational, one sample per person, it cannot show the drug caused the difference, and this pass read the press release and preprint rather than the final paper.36

Worth saying plainly: nearly every recovery study is small, in healthy young volunteers, and measures stool rather than the gut wall. "Recovered" means diversity and overall composition look like baseline again. It does not mean every species came back.

Who recovers slower

Repeat courses. The second course recovers differently and less completely than the first,3 and population data shows diversity dropping with each extra course.36

Starting with low diversity. It predicted which people got opportunist blooms after a cephalosporin.35

Long courses. Cumulative days, not just number of courses, is what the dose-response curves track.39

Age over 65, hospital stays, weakened immunity, a previous episode. These are the standard C. diff risk factors.48

Not intravenous versus oral. Many IV drugs reach the gut through bile anyway, and the damage tracks how much drug ends up in stool.

The part that lasts: resistance genes

Every study that measured it found more antibiotic resistance genes in the gut afterwards, and often for drugs the person never took: azithromycin and cefpodoxime raised genes for other classes entirely.6 Clindamycin's stayed up for two years.28

The twist worth knowing: amoxicillin barely touches diversity but raised the most resistance genes of the four drugs in a randomised comparison.7 Gentle on the census is not the same as gentle on the genes. This matters for treating your future infections, and for what you pass on, rather than for how you feel now.

The long-term links, honestly

All of these are observational. People who take antibiotics are people who had infections, and the infection itself may be doing the work. None of them prove cause.

Inflammatory bowel disease: up 28 to 48% depending on age, in 6.1 million Danes with 52,898 new cases, dose-dependent, peaking 1 to 2 years after. The strongest links were with drugs used for gut infections, which may mean early undiagnosed IBD was being treated as infection.39

Bowel cancer: colon cancer up about 9 to 17%, and rectal cancer slightly down, in 28,980 UK cases. An effect that runs in opposite directions in two neighbouring tissues deserves scepticism.40

Childhood weight: a single course was not significant; more than one, or exposure before 6 months, was, at about 20 to 24% higher odds.41

Mood and cognition: repeated courses linked to 23 to 56% higher odds of depression, and two or more months of midlife use to slightly lower cognitive scores seven years later. Confounding here is severe.53,52

The one claim that does not survive

"Take probiotics to restore your microbiome after antibiotics." This is the single most repeated piece of advice on the subject, and it is the one with evidence against it rather than for it.

A meta-analysis of randomised trials found probiotics taken during antibiotics made no difference to diversity, by any of three measures. Both groups drifted back to baseline in 3 to 8 weeks regardless.45

A study that went further and took actual gut-wall biopsies rather than stool found something worse: after antibiotics, an 11-strain probiotic colonised the gut wall better than usual, and that came with a markedly delayed and persistently incomplete return of the person's own community, compared with people who did nothing. The people who banked their own stool beforehand and had it returned recovered within days.17

The same group then found that probiotics after antibiotics were linked to more antibiotic resistance genes in the gut wall, which did not show up in stool samples at all.46

Hold the strength of this honestly: it is one small study from one centre, invasive, not replicated. It is not proof that probiotics harm recovery. But there is nothing on the other side of the scale, and "restores your gut flora" is a marketing line, not a finding. What probiotics do have good evidence for is preventing diarrhoea, which is a different and useful thing.

5 Pairings, and the same problem elsewhere

The same problem, other places

The vagina, where it has actually been tested. This is the closest thing to a controlled experiment in replanting a community after antibiotics, and it is worth reading because the design is everything. Women treated for bacterial vaginosis got a course of metronidazole first, then a live L. crispatus product for 11 weeks. Recurrence at 12 weeks: 30% against 45% on placebo, and the strain was still detectable in 79% of them.51

Three things made that work, and all three are missing from a capsule swallowed by a healthy adult: the antibiotic cleared the space first, the dosing continued for weeks rather than days, and the organism was matched to the niche. That is the whole pattern of when seeding works.

The gut after a gut infection is a different problem with its own page, and some of what is weak here has better evidence there.

Peptides, and why they are a footnote here

This page is about the microbiome, so the honest answer about peptides is short. A PubMed search for BPC-157 and gut microbiota returns zero results, run on 22 November 2026.24 Not a weak result. None.

BPC-157 does have a long rodent record on gut lining damage: ulcers, painkiller injury, fistulas, almost all of it from one research group. Its three published human papers all come from a single clinician at a private US clinic that sells it, two are uncontrolled and the third has two participants, and none of them are about the gut.25 KPV calmed chemically induced colitis in mice and has no human data.26 Larazotide, designed to tighten the gut lining, showed no change in permeability across four trials in 626 coeliac patients and its phase 3 was stopped in 2022.27

So: there is a plausible story about the gut wall, and nothing at all about the community living on it. If that changes, it goes here.

6 Warnings

When this needs a doctor, not a supplement

Same day, not next week: watery diarrhoea three or more times a day for more than a day or two, during a course or up to about three months after it; fever; blood or pus in stool; belly pain or swelling that is getting worse; signs of dehydration; or diarrhoea that comes back after it had settled.48

Higher urgency if you are over 65, recently in hospital, immunocompromised, or have had C. difficile before.

Do not take loperamide or another anti-diarrhoea drug for suspected C. diff without medical advice.

The useful test after antibiotics is a C. diff test, which your doctor orders. Not a consumer microbiome kit.

Risks in the things on this page

Probiotics are not risk-free for everyone. The Cochrane reviews found no serious attributable events in healthy people, and flagged case reports of serious infections in debilitated or immunocompromised patients and those with central lines.9 Live yeast has the same caveat. If your immune system is suppressed, this is a conversation with your doctor.

FMT has a hard safety floor. Two patients developed drug-resistant E. coli bloodstream infections from the same screened donor, and one of them died. Both were immunocompromised.50 Screening was and is imperfect. This is why FMT belongs in medicine and not in a kitchen.

One strain was flagged going the wrong way. The AGA guideline notes L. rhamnosus GG was linked to higher C. diff recurrence than placebo in one treatment trial.44 Common does not mean neutral.

Nothing here is a reason to refuse antibiotics you need. When an infection needs treating, treating it wins. The useful questions are whether it is needed, whether something narrower works, and whether the course can be shorter.

7 Usual questions

What people ask

How long until my gut recovers?

Most healthy adults are back near baseline within 2 to 8 weeks: by about day 19 for several common drugs, and about 1.5 months even after a four-day cocktail of three last-resort antibiotics.6,4 Some individual species, and the resistance genes you carry, can stay different for 6 to 12 months.

Does one course ruin it forever?

No. Nothing in the literature supports that. But an exact return to the old state is uncommon: in the cocktail study 9 species everyone started with were still missing in most people at 6 months,4 and a Swedish cohort of about 15,000 people links even a single course of clindamycin, a fluoroquinolone or flucloxacillin to measurable differences 4 to 8 years later.36 That last one is observational, with one stool sample per person, so treat it as a signal and not a measurement of you.

Which antibiotics are hardest on the gut?

Clindamycin first by a distance, then ciprofloxacin, broad cephalosporins, oral vancomycin, and azithromycin for slowness rather than depth. Gentlest: penicillin V, amoxicillin, nitrofurantoin, the tetracyclines, rifaximin.

Should I take probiotics at the same time?

For preventing diarrhoea, the trials say yes, with the size of the benefit depending on who you are. Children: 19% down to 8%.9 High-risk hospital patients: a real C. diff reduction.10 A healthy adult on a short outpatient course: small, and the largest well-run trial in older inpatients found nothing.12 Starting within the first 2 days is where the evidence sits.11

Do probiotics help my microbiome recover afterwards?

That is a different question from the one above, and the answer is no. A meta-analysis found no effect on diversity.45 One small study using actual gut-wall biopsies found probiotics delayed the return of the person's own community compared with doing nothing, while their own banked stool restored it in days.17 The same group later found probiotics after antibiotics were linked to more resistance genes in the gut wall.46 One small study is not a verdict, but there is nothing on the other side.

What is the single best thing I can do?

Not take antibiotics you do not need, and when you do need them, take the narrowest drug for the shortest effective course. That is the only intervention here with proven effect sizes. After that: time does most of the work, and eating a wide range of plants and fermented foods is the best-supported help.

Is diarrhoea on antibiotics normal?

Common: 5 to 35% depending on the drug.47 Loose stools that settle within days of finishing are expected. Watery diarrhoea three or more times a day, fever, or blood is not, and needs a doctor.

Is two courses worse than one?

On average yes. The second ciprofloxacin course produced a different and still incomplete recovery,3 and in the Swedish cohort diversity fell with each extra course, most steeply for the first two.36

Are IV antibiotics gentler on the gut?

Not necessarily. Many intravenous drugs reach the gut through bile, and the damage tracks how much of the drug ends up in stool rather than how it went in.

Can I get C. diff weeks after finishing?

Yes. Risk is highest during the course and the month after, and it can appear up to about three months later.48

Will I become antibiotic resistant?

You will not; your gut bacteria will carry more resistance genes for months, which every study that measured it found, often for drugs you never took.7,6 That matters for treating future infections and for spread, not for your body developing resistance.

Do antibiotics cause weight gain?

There is a small signal in infants given repeated courses or exposed before 6 months. A single course was not significant.41 Adult evidence is weak.

Do they raise the risk of IBD or bowel cancer?

Observational links exist: inflammatory bowel disease up about 28 to 48%,39 colon cancer up about 9 to 17% while rectal cancer was slightly down.40 The absolute changes are small, and people who get antibiotics are people who had infections, so the infection may be doing the work. Not proven either way.

Does an acid blocker make C. diff more likely?

The observational data says a little (odds 1.26),42 but a 2025 meta-analysis of 8 randomised trials in 29,880 people found no significant increase.43 A good example of a widely repeated risk that may be confounding: sicker people take acid blockers.

Should I get a microbiome test before and after?

No guideline supports it, and the practical problems are large: one person's day-to-day swings can be bigger than the differences between people, and different labs disagree with each other. The one test worth having after antibiotics is a C. diff test if you develop diarrhoea. There is a full page on this under Goals.

Words on this page

Microbiome
All the microbes living in a place, and their genes. Yours is mostly in the colon.
Diversity
How many different kinds there are and how evenly spread. Higher is linked with health on average; it is not a diagnosis.
Butyrate
A short-chain fatty acid that bacteria make from fibre. The main fuel of your colon lining cells.
Colonization resistance
A full gut keeping invaders out, simply by leaving them nowhere to live and nothing to eat.
Secondary bile acids
Bile acids that gut bacteria have converted. They stop C. difficile spores from sprouting.
Resistome
The collection of antibiotic resistance genes your gut bacteria carry.
AAD
Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. Most of it is not C. diff.
NNT
Number needed to treat: how many people take something for one of them to benefit. NNT 9 means 8 of 9 get nothing.
Odds ratio
How much more likely an outcome is. A big ratio on a rare event is still a rare event.
FMT
Faecal microbiota transplant: moving a whole stool community from a donor, or from your own banked sample.

How strong the evidence is

RCT
Randomised trial in people, the strongest single design here
Human
Measured in people, but without randomisation
Review
A meta-analysis or systematic review pooling trials
Cohort
Observational: a population followed or looked back at. Shows links, not causes
Animal
Mice or rats only
