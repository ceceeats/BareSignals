Testing: methods, accuracy, what it can guide

Sources mainly via PubMed abstracts. Blocked this pass: full text of Porcari consensus on PMC (PMC12343204; read instead via the WUR open repository copy, edepot.wur.nl/685103) and the final Communications Biology paper (nature.com/articles/s42003-025-09301-3; read instead via its bioRxiv preprint plus NIST, UMaryland and AGA news write-ups).

1. Methods explained for non-experts

The one-line truth: almost every consumer test measures DNA (or RNA) in one stool sample. That tells you which microbes were passing through the end of your gut that day, at a resolution that depends on the method. None reliably tells you which strains you carry.

Species vs strain (why "strain" is the wall)

  • Genus → species → strain. Lactobacillus is a genus (old naming; many split into new genera in 2020) reported; L. rhamnosus is a species; L. rhamnosus GG is a strain. Strains of one species can differ by hundreds of genes: one makes a toxin, another doesn't (e.g. E. coli O157:H7 vs harmless E. coli) reported.
  • Probiotic evidence is strain-specific, so a test that says "you have Bifidobacterium longum" does not say you have the strain from a product or study inferred.
  • Strains are real and stable: Faith 2013 (n=37 US adults, up to 5 years, 16S plus whole-genome sequencing of >500 cultured isolates) found strain stability followed a power law suggesting most strains stay for decades; shared strains were found in family members, not unrelated people verified (Science, doi:10.1126/science.1237439, PMID 23828941). But this needed culturing hundreds of isolates and research-grade sequencing, not a consumer kit inferred.

3. Accuracy and reproducibility

Same sample, seven companies (NIST / University of Maryland, Communications Biology, 2026; preprint bioRxiv 2024)

  • Design: NIST homogenized one donor's stool into a reference material; identical aliquots went to 7 direct-to-consumer companies, 3 kits each = 21 tests; companies were not told until after reporting verified bioRxiv doi:10.1101/2024.06.05.596628; final paper nature.com/articles/s42003-025-09301-3, not opened.
  • Methods: 3 companies used 16S (one -4, two ; one also ITS for fungi), the rest (implied 4) shotgun. No shipments used ice verified preprint.
  • Read depth ranged from <20,000 to >10 million. More reads did not mean more taxa: one company reported >600 species at 20-30k reads, another 95-99 species at >10 million verified preprint.
  • 1,208 unique taxa across all reports; only 3 genera found in every sample; 17 genera shared after dropping one outlier replicate (<2% of taxa) reported AGA GI & Hepatology News Apr 2026; matches Scientific American.
  • For 17 of 18 common genera, the variation caused by the testing method was equal to or bigger than real person-to-person biological variation reported AGA News.
  • Within-company repeat agreement: genera shared between a company's own 3 replicates ranged from 6% (worst) to 98% verified preprint. One company rated 2 replicates "healthy" and the third "unhealthy" from the identical sample verified preprint; NIST blog.
  • One replicate had >50% unidentified reads and still got a consumer report reported AGA News.
  • C. difficile reported present by 3 companies, absent by 4 reported AGA News.
  • Reference "healthy ranges" differed between companies and generally disagreed (except Bifidobacterium) verified preprint.
  • Some companies recommended their own probiotics with little evidence verified preprint; no count given.

Research-lab reproducibility (the baseline even without consumer shortcuts)

  • MBQC, Sinha 2017: blinded stool, chemostat and artificial community samples sequenced by 15 labs, analyzed by 9 bioinformatics pipelines. Variability came most from specimen type/origin, then DNA extraction, sample handling, and bioinformatics; artificial communities exposed extraction-efficiency and classification differences verified (Nat Biotechnol, doi:10.1038/nbt.3981, PMID 28967885).
  • Porcari consensus on handling: collection must avoid contamination and preserve DNA (98%); ship within set time and store at −80 °C on arrival (97.5%); preservative tubes preferred verified.

Day-to-day variation (one sample is a snapshot)

  • Vandeputte 2021: daily quantitative profiling, 713 samples, 20 Belgian women, 6 weeks. For 78% of genera, day-to-day variation within one person was larger than variation between people, with up to 100-fold shifts. Diversity also fluctuated. Stool moisture (and less so diet) drove it; menstrual cycle did not verified (Nat Commun, doi:10.1038/s41467-021-27098-7, PMID 34795283). Authors recommend repeated sampling.
  • Johnson 2019: 34 people, daily food records + shotgun stool, 17 days: composition depended on several days of diet history; responses to food were highly personal verified (Cell Host Microbe, doi:10.1016/j.chom.2019.05.005).
  • Dethlefsen & Relman 2011: day-to-day variability exists but around a stable average over months verified (PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.1000087107).

Stool vs gut wall (mucosa)

  • Zmora 2018: mucosal microbiome "only partially" correlates with stool; probiotic colonization of the gut wall was person-, region- and strain-specific and could not be judged from probiotic presence in stool verified (Cell, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.041, PMID 30193112). Meaning: finding a probiotic species in stool does not prove it has settled in.
  • Stool reflects the distal colon; small-intestine microbes (relevant to SIBO) are largely invisible to stool tests reported.

Negative mirror

  • No study found showing a consumer test is analytically valid at strain level inferred from searches.
  • Requested "Hornung" and "Johns Hopkins/Nature Comms 2024" multi-company studies: not found this pass. The 2024 Northwestern study the brief mentions was not found either; the main real multi-company study is the NIST/UMaryland one above flag.

4. Clinical consensus

International consensus statement (Porcari et al., Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol, Feb 2025; online Dec 2024)

doi:10.1016/S2468-1253(24)00311-X, PMID 39647502 verified abstract + repository full text

  • 69 experts, 18 countries, Delphi, pass = ≥80% agreement.
  • "Insufficient evidence to widely recommend the routine use of microbiome testing" (90%).
  • Patients ordering tests themselves without a clinical reason is discouraged (80.4%).
  • Testing over time may help in specific clinical situations (80%).
  • 16S and shotgun are both fine for profiling (98%); culture and multiplex PCR are not microbiome tests (90%).
  • Reports should include alpha and beta diversity (90%), the deepest feasible resolution (96%), and comparison with matched healthy controls (88%).
  • Do not report the Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio (86%); dysbiosis indices not yet supported (90%); strict "healthy" reference ranges not supported (90%).
  • Testing providers should not give treatment advice (98%); management changes only by the referring doctor (100%).
  • Patients shouldn't stop medicines or change diet before testing unless their doctor advises (100%).

AGA / gastroenterologist position

  • No formal AGA guideline on consumer microbiome tests found this pass flag. AGA-linked experts: 2018 primer (Khoruts, AGA Center for Gut Microbiome Research advisory board): tests are not validated for any diagnosis, not suited to infection diagnosis, one stool sample is a snapshot that may not reflect the gut wall reported MDedge Dec 2018, summarizing Staley/Kaiser/Khoruts Dig Dis Sci 2018.
  • 2024 MDedge interviews: Colleen Kelly MD (AGAF) calls the tests "not validated" and "clinically meaningless" and advises patients against them; others say no microbiome test can confirm or rule out IBS reported.
  • Where testing IS useful (standard care, not "microbiome tests"): C. difficile testing (toxin/PCR) in diarrhea after antibiotics, stool culture or FDA-cleared multiplex PCR for infectious diarrhea, ova & parasites, calprotectin to rule out IBD, breath tests for SIBO/lactose/fructose malabsorption, and donor screening for fecal transplant reported standard guidance; consensus confirms PCR/culture are separate from microbiome testing.

What is a "healthy microbiome"?

  • No agreed definition. Van Hul, Cani, de Vos, Tilg, El-Omar 2024: "dysbiosis" is used without precision or definition; it's hard to even define who the healthy reference population is verified (Gut, doi:10.1136/gutjnl-2024-333378, PMID 39322314).
  • Diversity (alpha = how many/even; beta = how different from others) is the most used marker; higher diversity is linked with health on average but is not a diagnosis reported.
  • Gut Microbiome Health Index (GMHI), Gupta 2020: 50 species from 4,347 metagenomes in 34 studies; 73.7% balanced accuracy telling healthy from non-healthy groups in validation verified (Nat Commun, doi:10.1038/s41467-020-18476-8). GMWI2 (Chang 2024): 8,069 metagenomes, 54 studies, 26 countries; 80% cross-validated, ~75% in external cohorts verified (Nat Commun, doi:10.1038/s41467-024-51651-9). Meaning: about 1 in 4 people misclassified, it detects "some disease vs none" not which disease, and it needs shotgun data. Group-level tool, not personal diagnosis inferred.

5. Can a test guide what to take?

Test-guided diets (strongest evidence; microbiome is one input among many)

  • Zeevi 2015 (Weizmann): 800 people, CGM, 46,898 meals: big person-to-person differences in glucose response to identical meals; algorithm (blood, diet, body, activity, microbiome) predicted responses, validated in 100 people; a blinded RCT diet lowered post-meal glucose verified (Cell, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2015.11.001). RCT was small (n≈26) reported.
  • Ben-Yacov 2021: prediabetes, n=225, 6 months, algorithm diet (clinical + microbiome) vs Mediterranean. Time >140 mg/dL: −1.3 vs −0.3 h/day; HbA1c −0.16% vs −0.08% (between-group diff ~0.08 percentage points, small); held at 12 months; CGM oral glucose test no different verified (Diabetes Care, doi:10.2337/dc21-0162). Follow-up analysis in Gut 2023 found 9 species partly mediating effects (doi:10.1136/gutjnl-2022-329201) verified.
  • Popp 2022 (NYU, null): 204 randomized adults with obesity + prediabetes/T2D, 6 months: personalized glucose-response diet −3.26% weight vs low-fat −4.31% (difference 1.05%, P=.16, not significant) verified (JAMA Netw Open, doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.33760).
  • PREDICT 1 (Berry 2020): 1,002 UK twins/adults. Gut microbiome explained 7.1% of variance in post-meal fat response and 6.0% in glucose response; meal macronutrients 3.6% and 15.4%; genes 9.5% for glucose verified (Nat Med, doi:10.1038/s41591-020-0934-0). Meaning: microbiome is a minor predictor.
  • ZOE METHOD trial (Bermingham 2024): n=347 US adults 41-70, 18 weeks, app program (food data, glucose/fat responses, microbiome, history) vs USDA advice. Primary: triglycerides −0.13 mmol/L vs control (significant); LDL not significant. Secondary gains in weight, waist, HbA1c, diet quality, beta-diversity; no difference in BP, insulin, glucose, ApoB verified (Nat Med, doi:10.1038/s41591-024-02951-6, NCT05273268). Caveat: control got less contact/engagement; can't separate the microbiome's contribution from the app/coaching effect inferred. Company-funded reported.
  • IBS microbiome-guided diet vs low-FODMAP (Tunali 2026, Gut Microbes, 12-month follow-up): open-label, blinded outcome assessment, 16S-guided diet for 6 weeks. 12-month responders 62.5% vs 34.5% (absolute +28%, 95% CI 4.2-47.7), low-FODMAP benefit faded; authors call it hypothesis-generating verified (doi:10.1080/19490976.2026.2719125). Small (responder % implies ~25-30 per arm) inferred; likely company-linked algorithm speculated check COI.

Test-guided probiotics

  • No randomized trial found showing that choosing probiotics from a stool test beats no probiotic or a standard choice inferred from PubMed searches; absence of evidence.
  • Zmora 2018: colonization of an 11-strain probiotic was person-specific and predicted by baseline features, but stool tests couldn't show it verified. Suggests personalization might matter in principle, but no consumer test implements a validated version inferred.
  • Consensus: testing labs should not give therapy advice (98%) verified. NIST study: some companies sold their own probiotics off the report verified.

6. Testing before and after antibiotics

  • What happens (no-treatment baseline): Palleja 2018: 12 healthy men, 4 days of meropenem + gentamicin + vancomycin, shotgun over 6 months: blooms of Enterobacteria, E. faecalis, F. nucleatum; loss of bifidobacteria and butyrate producers; near-baseline by ~1.5 months, but 9 common species still undetectable in most people at 180 days; resistance-gene carriers favored verified (Nat Microbiol, doi:10.1038/s41564-018-0257-9). Dethlefsen 2011: ciprofloxacin, 3 people, 10 months: diversity loss within 3-4 days, recovery starting ~1 week after, often incomplete, a new stable state verified.
  • Probiotics after antibiotics may slow recovery: Suez 2018: an 11-strain probiotic delayed and kept incomplete the return of the person's own stool/mucosal microbiome vs spontaneous recovery; autologous FMT (own banked stool) restored it within days verified (Cell, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.047). Small human arms reported.
  • Is a before/after test useful for an individual? No guideline recommends it outside research inferred. Problems: day-to-day noise (up to 100-fold genus shifts) can swamp the signal; between-company noise matches biological variation; no "target" to recover to; result doesn't change management inferred from Sections 3-4. Consensus allows longitudinal testing only "in specific clinical scenarios" (80%) verified.
  • What a real change would look like (if using the SAME lab, same kit, ≥2 baseline samples): drop in diversity, rise of Enterobacteriaceae/Enterococcus, loss of Bifidobacterium and butyrate producers (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia), more resistance genes (shotgun only), then gradual return over 1-3 months inferred from Palleja/Dethlefsen.
  • Clinically relevant test after antibiotics: C. difficile testing if new diarrhea (≥3 loose stools/24 h) reported standard IDSA criteria.
  • Banking own stool before antibiotics for autologous FMT is research-only reported.

7. Fifteen usual questions (short answers)

  1. Can a test tell me which strains I have? Not reliably. 16S stops at genus; shotgun gives species and sometimes strain for abundant microbes; no consumer test is validated for strains.
  2. Will it show if my probiotic "took"? Not reliably. Stool presence doesn't prove gut-wall colonization (Zmora 2018), and 16S can't name the strain.
  3. Which method is best? For profiling: shotgun > 16S. For infection: culture/PCR via a doctor. For SIBO: breath test. For inflammation: calprotectin.
  4. Would two companies give me the same result? Probably not. NIST: only 3 genera agreed across 7 companies; method noise ≥ real biological difference for 17/18 genera.
  5. Would the same company give the same result twice? Depends: replicate agreement ranged 6-98% of genera.
  6. Is there a "normal range"? No agreed one. The consensus says strict healthy reference ranges are not supported.
  7. What does a "diversity score" mean? Average link with health in populations; not a diagnosis, and it moves day to day.
  8. Is the Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio meaningful? Consensus says don't report it (86%).
  9. Can it diagnose IBS, leaky gut, candida? No test diagnoses IBS; zonulin kits don't measure zonulin; "candida overgrowth" from stool sequencing has no validated cutoff reported.
  10. Should I test before and after antibiotics? Not useful for most people; recovery mostly happens over ~1.5 months anyway; use C. diff testing if diarrhea.
  11. Can the results tell me what to eat? Diet programs that combine glucose/fat responses + microbiome show small benefits (triglycerides −0.13 mmol/L; modest glycemic gains); a weight-loss trial was null. The microbiome adds ~6-7% of predictive power.
  12. Should I buy the probiotics the report recommends? No trial shows test-picked probiotics work; the consensus says labs shouldn't give therapy advice; some sell their own products (conflict of interest).
  13. How should I collect the sample? Follow the kit; preservative tube; don't change diet or stop medicines first; note recent antibiotics, illness, stool consistency. One sample = one day.
  14. Is a SIBO breath test reliable? Moderately; standardized cutoffs exist (H2 ≥20 ppm by 90 min; CH4 ≥10 ppm), but false positives/negatives are common, especially with lactulose.
  15. When is stool testing actually medically useful? Infection (culture/PCR, C. diff), parasites, calprotectin for possible IBD, donor screening, and red flags (blood, weight loss, night symptoms, age 45+ change in bowel habit) which need a doctor, not a kit reported.

Adding strains: does anything stick

Can you add new bacterial strains to your gut, and how often do they stick?

All sources traced via PubMed (article metadata and abstracts retrieved from PubMed) plus FDA primary pages. Every claim tagged verified (I read the primary abstract/source myself), reported (secondary source), inferred, speculated. All checked 22 Nov 2026.

Short answer up front: yes, but rarely, and it is not random. Whether a strain sticks is mostly decided by the gut you already have, not by the dose on the label.

1. Colonization resistance, in plain terms

The one-line version. A healthy adult gut is already full. Every nutrient, every bit of mucus surface, every bile-acid niche is already being used by a resident strain that got there first and has been tuned to that body for years. A swallowed probiotic arrives as an immigrant into a fully employed economy. It can pass through the town, it can trade for a few days, but there is no vacant job for it.

Niche theory, applied. Two conditions have to be met for a newcomer to establish: (a) the function it performs must not already be covered by residents (phylogenetic limiting), and (b) the food it eats must be in surplus (resource availability). Maldonado-Gómez 2016 showed both operating in humans at once: engraftment of B. longum AH1206 was associated with low abundance of resident B. longum and underrepresentation of specific carbohydrate utilization genes in the pre-treatment microbiome, and the authors named these two factors explicitly — "phylogenetic limiting and resource availability are two factors that control the niche opportunity for AH1206 colonization". verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2016.09.001" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Priority effects. Whoever arrives first and establishes gets to shape the environment against later arrivals. The cleanest human evidence is the mirror image: when residents are knocked back by antibiotics, newcomers suddenly stick. In Suez/Zmora 2018, antibiotic perturbation enhanced probiotics colonization in the human mucosa. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.047" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Same pattern in FMT: Podlesny 2022 found donor strain engraftment is strongly positively correlated with pre-FMT recipient microbiota dysbiosis, and was enhanced through antibiotic pretreatment and bowel lavage. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100711" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Why "it showed up in my stool" is not colonization. Stool is the exit pipe, not the tissue. Zmora 2018 found the mucosal-associated gastrointestinal microbiome only partially correlates with stool microbiome, and critically that mucosal colonization patterns were "indistinguishable by probiotics presence in stool". verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.041" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) So a stool PCR test showing your probiotic strain tells you almost nothing about whether it colonized the gut wall.

All-or-nothing, not partial. Smillie 2018 found donor strains within a species engraft in an all-or-nothing manner verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2018.01.003" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI), and Podlesny 2022 found that conspecific coexistence of donor and recipient strains after FMT is uncommon verified. Translation: for a given species you tend to end up with the donor's strain or your own, not a blend. inferred from those two findings

Baseline (no treatment). The honest comparator: resident adult gut strains are stable for years without any intervention, and an ingested strain's default fate is transit. Maldonado-Gómez state the default plainly: live bacteria such as probiotics have long been used to modulate gut microbiota and human physiology, but their colonization is mostly transient verified; Frese 2017 open with the same: attempts to alter intestinal dysbiosis via administration of probiotics have consistently shown that colonization with the administered microbes is transient verified (https://doi.org/10.1128/mSphere.00501-17" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI).

2. Human studies, with numbers

Zmora 2018, Cell — the mucosal biopsy study

Design: healthy volunteers, 11-strain probiotic vs placebo, with invasive sampling (upper and lower endoscopy, multiple gut regions) at baseline and during supplementation, plus metagenomics and host transcriptome. Probiotics remained viable upon gastrointestinal passage; in colonized but not germ-free mice, probiotics met a marked mucosal colonization resistance; in humans, person-, region- and strain-specific mucosal colonization patterns, with predictive baseline host and microbiome features; the effect on the mucosal community and gut transcriptome was transient and individualized. Conclusion in their words: empiric probiotics supplementation may be limited in universally and persistently impacting the gut mucosa. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.041" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

  • The popular "permissive vs resistant" two-group split (roughly, some people colonize and some do not, predictable from baseline) comes from this paper's body and figures, not the abstract. I did not open the full text; the per-group counts are reported, not verified here. Flagged in Gaps.

Suez/Zmora 2018, Cell — the companion antibiotic paper (and a harm signal)

Same group, same issue. After antibiotics, compared with spontaneous recovery, probiotics induced a markedly delayed and persistently incomplete indigenous stool/mucosal microbiome reconstitution and host transcriptome recovery, while autologous FMT induced a rapid and near-complete recovery within days; in vitro, Lactobacillus-secreted soluble factors contributed to probiotics-induced microbiome inhibition. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.047" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) This is the main negative finding in the field: better colonization after antibiotics came with worse recovery of the person's own community. n is small and it is a single centre.

Maldonado-Gómez 2016, Cell Host Microbe — B. longum AH1206, the ~30% number

Orally administered to humans, B. longum AH1206 stably persisted in the gut of 30% of individuals for at least 6 months without causing gastrointestinal symptoms or impacting the composition of the resident gut microbiota. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2016.09.001" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Read that carefully: it stuck in a minority, it was predictable from baseline (low resident B. longum, missing carbohydrate-utilisation genes), and it did not reshape the rest of the community. So "a strain can engraft" and "engrafting changes your microbiome" are two different claims; this study supports the first, not the second.

Frese 2017, mSphere — B. infantis EVC001 in breastfed infants, the clearest success

Design: n = 34 infants fed EVC001 day 7–28 of life, n = 32 no probiotic; stool over first 60 days. B. infantis-fed infants had significantly higher fecal Bifidobacteriaceae, in particular B. infantis, while EVC001 was fed, and this difference persisted more than 30 days after supplementation ceased; fecal milk oligosaccharides were significantly lower, demonstrating higher consumption of human milk oligosaccharides; acetate and lactate higher, fecal pH lower; infants with high Bifidobacteriaceae had 4-fold-lower fecal endotoxin. The authors' own explanation is the mechanism of this whole page: it underscores the importance of pairing a probiotic organism with a specific substrate. verified (https://doi.org/10.1128/mSphere.00501-17" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Why this one works and adult probiotics don't: an infant gut is an open niche, and the newcomer arrived with its own exclusive food supply (HMOs in breast milk) that residents could not use. Not generalisable to adults. inferred Caveat worth stating on a consumer page: all Frese authors were Evolve Biosystems (the manufacturer) or collaborating UC Davis groups. verified from affiliations Follow-up: persistence beyond ~1 year and clinical outcomes were not in this paper.

Lactobacillus reuteri

Could not retrieve a human persistence trial inside the search cap; my targeted queries returned nothing usable. No numbers given here on purpose. The general field statement (Lactobacillus strains are transient in adults, clearing within ~1–2 weeks of stopping) is reported and untraced in this pass. Flagged in Gaps.

Akkermansia muciniphila — Depommier 2019, Nat Med

Design: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot, overweight/obese insulin-resistant volunteers; 40 enrolled, 32 completed; 10^10 A. muciniphila daily, live or pasteurized, for three months. Results, pasteurized vs placebo: insulin sensitivity +28.62 ± 7.02% (P = 0.002), insulinemia −34.08 ± 7.12% (P = 0.006), total cholesterol −8.68 ± 2.38% (P = 0.02); body weight −2.27 ± 0.92 kg (P = 0.091), fat mass −1.37 ± 0.82 kg (P = 0.092), hip circumference −2.63 ± 1.14 cm (P = 0.091) — note those three did not reach significance. Liver dysfunction and inflammation markers fell while the overall gut microbiome structure was unaffected. verified (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-019-0495-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Two things a consumer page must say. (1) It was pasteurized (dead) Akkermansia that produced the metabolic signal — the live arm did not. So this is not an engraftment story at all; it is a dead-bacterium-as-drug story, and the pasteurized cells cannot colonize anything. verified from the abstract: significant results are reported only for the pasteurized arm (2) it is explicitly a proof-of-concept study, n = 32 completers, primary endpoints were safety/tolerability. Not an efficacy result.

Spore probiotics (Bacillus)

The strongest human data is survival-and-germination, not colonization. Colom 2021: 11 ileostomy participants, randomized crossover, double-blind, placebo-controlled; after B. subtilis DE111, spores and vegetative cells began appearing in ileum effluent at 3 h; by 6 h spore concentration rose to 9.7 × 10^7 ± 8.1 × 10^7 CFU/g and stayed constant to 8 h; vegetative cells reached 7.3 × 10^7 ± 1.4 × 10^8 CFU/g at 7 h, showing spores remain viable through the stomach and germinate in the human small intestine within 3 h. verified (https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2021.715863" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Read the limits: n = 11, 8-hour window, ileostomy (no colon), funded and authored by the ingredient manufacturer (Deerland). verified from affiliations It proves spores survive transit and wake up. It says nothing about long-term colonic residence. Note the huge standard deviations — person-to-person variation exceeded the mean. verified

Next-generation strains — Faecalibacterium prausnitzii

EXL01, first-in-human, 2026: open-label, single-arm, 8 adults with mild-to-moderate Crohn's after steroid-induced response/remission. EXL01 was well tolerated with no treatment-related adverse events; six participants completed, two discontinued due to disease flare; and the engraftment-relevant finding: gut microbiota composition remained largely stable, while transcriptomic analyses revealed distinct changes in ileal gene expression. verified (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72375-y" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) So even for a "missing keystone" species deliberately chosen because it is depleted, the community did not visibly change. n = 8, open-label, no control arm, safety primary. Not evidence of benefit.

Synbiotics (strain + its own fibre) — Krumbeck 2018

This is the direct test of "feed the strain its own food and it will stick," and it mostly failed. Design: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-arm, obese adults, 3 weeks; arms = GOS alone, B. adolescentis IVS-1 (autochthonous, human-derived, selected by in vivo selection), B. lactis BB-12 (commercial, allochthonous), and the synbiotic combinations.

  • IVS-1 reached significantly higher cell numbers in feces than BB-12 (P < 0.01), and its administration increased total bifidobacteria comparably to GOS verified
  • although GOS showed a clear bifidogenic effect, both probiotic strains showed only a non-significant trend of higher fecal cell numbers when administered with GOS — i.e. no synergy verified
  • Barrier function: post-aspirin sucralose:lactulose ratios were reduced with IVS-1 (P = 0.050), IVS-1 + GOS (P = 0.022) and GOS (P = 0.010); sucralose excretion reduced with BB-12 (P = 0.002) and GOS (P = 0.020) — improvements in colonic permeability but no synergistic effects; no changes in endotoxemia markers verified
  • Author conclusion, which is the headline for this whole section: "autochthony of the probiotic strain has a larger effect on ecological performance than the provision of a prebiotic substrate" verified (https://doi.org/10.1186/s40168-018-0494-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Where the strain came from matters more than what you feed it. One borderline p = 0.050 in a multi-arm 3-week trial should not be oversold.

3. FMT: how much donor strain actually engrafts

The engraftment numbers

Ianiro 2022, Nat Med — shotgun metagenomic systematic meta-analysis. Correction to the brief: this paper is 226 donor/pre-FMT/post-FMT triads across 8 disease types, not 316 FMTs. Integrated analysis of new and public stool metagenomes from 226 triads of donors, pre-FMT and post-FMT recipients across eight different disease types. verified Findings:

  • Recipients with higher donor strain engraftment were more likely to experience clinical success after FMT (P = 0.017) — engraftment is not just a biomarker, it tracks outcome. verified
  • Increased engraftment with multiple routes in one treatment (e.g. capsules plus colonoscopy) and in antibiotic-treated recipients with infectious diseases versus antibiotic-naïve patients with noncommunicable diseases verified
  • Taxon asymmetry: Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria (including Bifidobacteria) displayed higher engraftment than Firmicutes, except for six under-characterized Firmicutes species verified
  • Cross-dataset machine learning predicted presence/absence of species post-FMT at 0.77 average AUROC verified (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-01964-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Smillie 2018, Cell Host Microbe — rCDI as the model system. Engraftment can be predicted largely from the abundance and phylogeny of bacteria in the donor and the pre-FMT patient; donor strains within a species engraft all-or-nothing, and previously undetected strains frequently colonize patients receiving FMT; validated in metabolic syndrome, suggesting the same principles extend to other indications. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2018.01.003" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Podlesny 2022, Cell Rep Med — 14 FMT trials, 5 pathologies, >250 individuals. Independently of the underlying clinical condition, conspecific coexistence of donor and recipient strains is uncommon; donor strain engraftment is strongly positively correlated with pre-FMT recipient microbiota dysbiosis; enhanced by antibiotic pretreatment and bowel lavage; dependent on donor and recipient α-diversity; strains from relatively abundant species were more likely, and predicted oral, oxygen-tolerant and gram-positive species less likely, to engraft. verified (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100711" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Synthesis across the three: engraftment is a predictable ecological process driven by (a) how empty the recipient's gut is, (b) how abundant the strain is in the donor, (c) the taxon's oxygen tolerance and lifestyle. Dose and route help; donor "superness" matters less than recipient vacancy. inferred from the three above

Success rates by condition

**Recurrent C. difficile — the only indication where this reliably works.** van Nood 2013, NEJM, the landmark RCT, stopped early at interim analysis: of 16 patients in the infusion group, 13 (81%) had resolution after the first infusion; 2 of the 3 remaining resolved after a second infusion from a different donor (i.e. 15/16, 94% overall) versus 4 of 13 (31%) with vancomycin alone and 3 of 13 (23%) with vancomycin plus bowel lavage (P < 0.001 for both). verified (https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1205037" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

  • Baseline for context: 23–31% resolution on standard antibiotic therapy in that trial. verified
  • Absolute risk difference vs vancomycin alone: 81% − 31% = 50 percentage points; NNT ≈ 2 (first infusion only). Using 94% after retreatment: 63 points, NNT ≈ 1.6. inferred my arithmetic from the published counts
  • Caveats: n = 43 total, open-label, stopped early (which inflates effect estimates), nasoduodenal route. verified

Ulcerative colitis — real but modest, low-certainty. Cochrane (Imdad 2023, 12 studies, 550 participants): FMT may increase rates of induction of clinical remission in UC (RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.13 to 2.84; low-certainty evidence) at 6–12 weeks; endoscopic remission RR 1.45, 95% CI 0.64 to 3.29 — CIs wide and including a possible null effect, low certainty; little to no difference in any adverse events (RR 0.99, 0.85 to 1.16); very uncertain for serious adverse events (RR 1.77, 0.88 to 3.55) and quality of life (MD 15.34, −3.84 to 34.52). Maintenance of remission: very uncertain (clinical RR 2.97, 0.26 to 34.42; endoscopic RR 3.28, 0.73 to 14.74). Crohn's: no included study assessed induction of remission in CD; one study with 21 participants on maintenance, RR 1.21, 0.36 to 4.14, very low certainty. verified (https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012774.pub3" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) RR 1.79 on a low base rate means a real but partial effect; the review's own verdict is "may increase" with low certainty, and maintenance is unknown. Nothing here supports FMT as UC therapy outside trials.

IBS — genuinely mixed, and the positive trial is the outlier. El-Salhy 2020, Gut: n = 165, randomized double-blind, placebo (own faeces) vs 30 g vs 60 g FMT by gastroscope, single well-characterized donor. Response (≥50-point drop in total IBS symptom score) at 3 months: 23.6% placebo, 76.9% with 30 g (p < 0.0001), 89.1% with 60 g (p < 0.0001), with significant improvements in fatigue and quality of life and mild self-limiting GI adverse events. Authors attribute it to donor selection and dose: "utilising a well-defined donor with a normal dysbiosis index and favourable specific microbial signature is essential; the response increases with the dose". verified (https://doi.org/10.1136/gutjnl-2019-319630" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Read this sceptically. Effect sizes of 77–89% response are far outside anything else in IBS, from one centre, one donor, with the paper itself opening by noting that two previous double-blind placebo-controlled studies disagreed — one found improvement, the other found no effect verified. A single "super-donor" result that no one else has reproduced at that magnitude is a hypothesis, not a finding. Also note the 23.6% placebo response — that is the baseline, and in most IBS trials placebo response runs 30–40%. [the 23.6% is verified; the 30–40% general figure is reported, untraced here]

Obesity / metabolic — mostly null, and the cleanest demonstration that engraftment ≠ benefit. Yu 2020 (FMT-TRIM), PLoS Med: n = 24 adults with obesity and HOMA-IR 2.0–8.0, weekly oral lean-donor FMT capsules vs placebo for 6 weeks, 12-week follow-up, primary endpoint insulin sensitivity by hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp. No statistically significant improvement in insulin sensitivity (+5% ± 12% FMT vs −3% ± 32% placebo; mean difference 9%, 95% CI −5% to 28%, p = 0.16); no significant differences in HOMA-IR (MD 0.2, −0.9 to 0.9, p = 0.96), lean mass or fat mass; but variable engraftment of donor bacterial groups persisted throughout the 12-week study, and the conclusion states it directly: gut microbiota engraftment occurred in most recipients for at least 12 weeks; despite engraftment, no clinically significant metabolic effects were observed. verified (https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003051" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Leong 2020 (Gut Bugs), JAMA Netw Open — correction to the brief: the adolescent trial is Leong et al. 2020, not "Wilson 2021"; B. Wilson is a co-author. n = 87 adolescents aged 14–18 with BMI ≥ 30, single course of oral encapsulated FMT from 4 lean same-sex donors vs saline placebo. No effect on BMI standard deviation score at 6 weeks (aMD −0.026; 95% CI −0.074 to 0.022); android-to-gynoid fat ratio fell at 6, 12 and 26 weeks (aMD −0.021, −0.023, −0.029); no effects on insulin sensitivity, liver function, lipids, inflammatory markers, blood pressure, total body fat percentage, gut health or quality of life; microbiome community shift maintained to 12 weeks; post-hoc, among those with baseline metabolic syndrome, greater resolution with FMT (18→4) than placebo (13→10) by 26 weeks (aOR 0.06, 95% CI 0.01–0.45; P = .007); no serious adverse events. verified (https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2020.30415" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) The metabolic-syndrome result is explicitly post-hoc exploratory in a trial that missed its primary endpoint. It should be described as a hypothesis for a future trial, nothing more. verified that the authors label it post-hoc

Autism — open-label only, no control group. Kang 2017, Microbiome: 18 children, "Microbiota Transfer Therapy" = 2-week antibiotic treatment, bowel cleanse, then extended FMT with a high initial dose followed by daily lower maintenance doses for 7–8 weeks; ~80% reduction in GI symptoms at end of treatment with significant improvements in constipation, diarrhea, indigestion and abdominal pain persisting 8 weeks after treatment; behavioral ASD symptoms improved significantly and remained improved at 8 weeks; sequencing revealed successful partial engraftment of donor microbiota, with increased diversity and increased Bifidobacterium, Prevotella and Desulfovibrio persisting after treatment stopped. verified (https://doi.org/10.1186/s40168-016-0225-7" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Hard limits: n = 18, open-label, no placebo arm, parent-reported outcome scales, in a condition with large placebo and expectancy effects, and the authors call it exploratory. The 2-year follow-up (Nirmalkar 2022) found the microbiome did not simply stay fixed: at two-year follow-up the microbiota and microbial genes shifted into a new state, distinct from both their Baseline levels and the typically developing group. verified (https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms232113481" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI)

Safety: the 2019 ESBL death

DeFilipp 2019, NEJM: two patients developed ESBL-producing E. coli bacteremia after FMT in two independent clinical trials; both cases were linked to the same stool donor by genomic sequencing; one of the patients died, prompting the authors to call for enhanced donor screening and continued vigilance to define the benefits and risks of FMT across different patient populations. verified (https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1910437" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Both recipients were immunocompromised (myelodysplastic syndrome; hepatic encephalopathy per the indexing). verified from MeSH terms This is the hard safety floor on the whole field: a transplanted community can carry a resistant organism, screening was and is imperfect, and the people most likely to be offered FMT for non-CDI indications are often the least able to tolerate that. Cochrane's systemic read is consistent: the evidence was very uncertain about the risk of serious adverse events. verified

4. Approved live biotherapeutics, and the failures

Approved (US)

Rebyota (fecal microbiota, live-jslm; Ferring) — approved 30 November 2022 for prevention of recurrence of Clostridioides difficile infection in adults 18 and older following antibiotic treatment for recurrent CDI. verified (FDA: https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/vaccines/rebyota) Phase 3 PUNCH CD3 (Khanna 2022, Drugs): 289 randomized 2:1, 267 treated (180 RBX2660, 87 placebo), single blinded enema, primary endpoint absence of CDI diarrhea within 8 weeks; model-estimated treatment success 70.6% with RBX2660 vs 57.5% placebo, treatment effect 13.1%, posterior probability of superiority 0.991; more than 90% of 8-week responders sustained response through 6 months in both arms; treatment-emergent adverse events higher than placebo, mostly mild GI events. verified (https://doi.org/10.1007/s40265-022-01797-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Read the fine print honestly: 13.1 percentage points over placebo (NNT ≈ 8) inferred my arithmetic, a Bayesian primary analysis integrating the earlier phase 2b, and the paper itself discloses that original model estimates were 70.4% vs 58.1%, and the data were "aligned to improve the exchangeability and interpretability of the Bayesian analysis" verified. And the honest negative: the phase 2b (PUNCH CD2) missed its primary endpoint — 56.8% (25/45) one dose, 55.6% (25/45) two doses, 43.2% (19/44) placebo, both p = 0.2 vs placebo in the final ITT population, with significance only in the per-protocol population (87.5% vs 58.1%, p = 0.017). verified (https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-022-00744-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) An ITT-negative phase 2b followed by a Bayesian phase 3 that borrowed from it is a real weakness to state out loud.

Vowst (fecal microbiota spores, live-brpk; Seres/Nestlé) — approved 26 April 2023 for prevention of recurrence of CDI in adults 18 and older after antibacterial treatment for recurrent CDI; four capsules once daily for three consecutive days; pivotal trial 89 Vowst vs 93 placebo, CDI recurrence through 8 weeks 12.4% vs 39.8%; warnings: it is made from donated human fecal matter and may carry a risk of transmitting infectious agents despite donor and stool testing, and may contain food allergens of unknown allergenic potential. verified (FDA press announcement, 26 Apr 2023) Underlying trial, ECOSPOR III (Feuerstadt 2022, NEJM): 182 enrolled of 281 screened; recurrence 12% SER-109 vs 40% placebo (relative risk 0.32, 95% CI 0.18–0.58, P < 0.001); subgroups RR 0.24 (0.07–0.78) under 65, 0.36 (0.18–0.72) 65+, 0.41 (0.22–0.79) after vancomycin, 0.09 (0.01–0.63) after fidaxomicin; and the engraftment point: SER-109 dose species were detected as early as week 1 and were associated with bile-acid profiles known to inhibit C. difficile spore germination. verified (https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2106516" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Absolute risk reduction 28 points, NNT ≈ 4 inferred my arithmetic. Note the mechanism FDA/Seres propose is not "repopulating the gut" but restoring bile-acid chemistry that stops spores germinating — a functional, not a census, effect. verified from the abstract

Pipeline failures — the part the category does not advertise

SER-287 in ulcerative colitis. Phase 1b looked genuinely promising and showed engraftment is tunable: 58 adults with active mild-to-moderate UC; clinical remission at week 8 was 40% with vancomycin/SER-287 daily vs 0% placebo/placebo (P = .024), 13.3% placebo/SER-287 weekly, 17.7% vancomycin/SER-287 weekly, and engraftment of dose species was facilitated by vancomycin preconditioning and daily dosing. verified (https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2020.07.048" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Then the phase 2b (ECO-RESET) failed. Secondary reporting: the trial missed its primary endpoint, with about 10% remission on the treatment and "about the same proportion" on placebo, and Seres shut down the associated open-label and maintenance studies. reported — STAT News, 22 Jul 2021; I could not read past the paywall and found no peer-reviewed publication of ECO-RESET in this pass (https://www.statnews.com/2021/07/22/seres-therapeutics-ulcerative-colitis-therapy-falls-short/) Why this matters more than the headline: a 40%-vs-0% phase 1b result in n = 58, with 0% in a tiny placebo cell, collapsed to ~10% vs ~10% when properly powered. That is the single best cautionary example in this field of small-trial effects evaporating. inferred

SER-109 succeeded in rCDI but note the pattern: the same platform worked for an infection with an empty, antibiotic-cleared niche and failed for chronic inflammatory disease with an intact community. That is colonization resistance again. inferred

Beyond the gut: Lactin-V (vaginal L. crispatus CTV-05)

Cohen 2020, NEJM: phase 2b, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 228 women aged 18–45 randomized 2:1 to vaginal Lactin-V or placebo for 11 weeks after a course of vaginal metronidazole gel, followed to week 24. Results: recurrence by week 12 in 46/152 (30%) with Lactin-V vs 34/76 (45%) placebo (risk ratio 0.66, 95% CI 0.44–0.87, P = 0.01); RR by week 24 0.73 (0.54–0.92); colonization check: at the 12-week visit L. crispatus CTV-05 was detected in 79% of participants in the Lactin-V group; adverse events did not differ significantly between groups. verified (https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1915254" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI) Absolute risk reduction 15 points, NNT ≈ 7 inferred my arithmetic. Baseline to keep in view: 45% recurrence on placebo despite antibiotic treatment first — this is a hard condition, and the drug cuts recurrence by about a third, not to zero. The template is identical to every success above: clear the niche with an antibiotic first, then seed, then keep dosing for 11 weeks. inferred

What the whole file actually says

Three conditions keep recurring in every success and are absent in every failure. (1) A vacancy — antibiotics, bowel lavage, infancy, or a genuinely missing species. (2) An exclusive food supply or function the residents cannot provide (HMOs for B. infantis; bile-acid transformation for SER-109). (3) Repeated dosing over weeks, not a capsule a day indefinitely hoping for permanence. Where all three hold, strains stick and sometimes help. Where none hold — a healthy adult taking a shelf probiotic — the honest expected outcome is transit, a transient and individual mucosal blip, and nothing detectable a month later.

And the hardest finding to sell but the most important: engraftment and benefit come apart. Yu 2020 got engraftment and no metabolic effect. Depommier got metabolic effects from dead bacteria. Maldonado-Gómez got 6-month engraftment with no change to the resident community. "Did it colonize?" and "did it do anything?" are two separate questions, and the industry routinely answers the first and implies the second.
