What a course does, and how recovery runs

What antibiotics do to the gut microbiome, and how it recovers on its own. Checked 22 Nov 2026. Tags: verified = abstract or primary record read this pass. reported = secondary source (press release, review, news summary). inferred = my reasoning from the data. speculated = plausible, unproven. Main caveat for the whole page: almost every human recovery study is small (3 to 66 healthy adults), uses healthy young volunteers, and measures stool, not the gut wall. "Recovered" usually means diversity and overall composition look like baseline again. It does not mean every species came back.

1. Plain biology

What it is

  • About 38 trillion bacteria (3.8 x 10^13) live in a 70 kg adult. That is roughly the same as the number of human cells (3.0 x 10^13), not the old "10 to 1" claim. Total bacterial mass is about 0.2 kg. Almost all of them live in the colon. verified Sender 2016, PLoS Biol, PMID 27541692, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1002533
  • Species: about 1,000 or more species across people, with a typical person carrying a few hundred. reported from Qin 2010 Nature; not re-opened this pass
  • Main phyla: Firmicutes (now Bacillota) and Bacteroidetes (Bacteroidota) make up most of it. Smaller groups are Actinobacteria (includes Bifidobacterium), Proteobacteria (includes E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae) and Verrucomicrobia (Akkermansia). reported
  • Each person's mix is unique. Differences between people are bigger than changes inside one person over months. verified Dethlefsen 2011, PMID 20847294

What it does, in plain words

  • Colonization resistance (guarding the door): resident microbes take up the space and food, and make chemicals that stop invaders such as C. difficile, Salmonella and resistant Enterobacteriaceae from settling. When antibiotics clear the residents, invaders get an opening. reported mechanism well established
  • Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and butyrate: bacteria ferment fibre into acetate, propionate and butyrate. Butyrate is the main fuel for colon lining cells, helps keep the lining sealed, and calms inflammation. Butyrate makers include Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia, Eubacterium rectale and Coprococcus. Antibiotics hit these hard. verified for depletion: Zaura 2015 PMID 26556275, Palleja 2018 PMID 30349083
  • Mucus layer: a gel coat over the colon lining. Microbes help keep it at the right thickness. Some (Akkermansia) live in it. reported
  • Bile acids: the liver makes "primary" bile acids. Gut bacteria (mainly Firmicutes/Clostridia) convert them to "secondary" bile acids. Secondary bile acids block C. difficile spores from growing, and primary ones help them sprout. After broad antibiotics in healthy adults, serum secondary bile acids fell about 1,000-fold. verified Hagan 2019, Cell, PMID 31491384, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2019.08.010. Oral vancomycin cut fecal secondary bile acids and lowered insulin sensitivity in obese men in 7 days. Amoxicillin did not. verified Vrieze 2014, PMID 24316517, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhep.2013.11.034
  • Immune training: microbes teach the immune system what to tolerate. In Hagan 2019, broad antibiotics cut gut bacterial load 10,000-fold and raised inflammatory signals. In people with low starting antibody levels, flu vaccine responses were weaker. verified PMID 31491384
  • Vitamins: gut bacteria make vitamin K2 and some B vitamins (B12, folate, biotin). The amount people actually absorb from this is debated. reported

Systematic review baseline

  • Elvers 2020, BMJ Open, PMID 32958481, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2019-035677: 31 studies of common UK primary-care antibiotics. Most people recover to baseline within a few weeks; some studies suggest effects lasting 2 to 6 months. Penicillin V, nitrofurantoin and amoxicillin had very little effect. Doxycycline cut Bifidobacterium short-term. Clarithromycin cut Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus for up to 5 weeks. verified

Typical recovery timeline (synthesis) inferred from the studies above

  • Days 1 to 4 on the drug: diversity drops fast (cipro within 3 to 4 days).
  • During and 1 week after: lowest point. Blooms of Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter), Enterococcus, sometimes yeast (Candida). [Candida bloom: reported, not verified this pass]
  • 1 to 4 weeks after: most of the overall picture returns for narrow or gentle drugs (amoxicillin, nitrofurantoin, penicillin V, levofloxacin/cefpodoxime by about day 19).
  • 1 to 2 months: most healthy adults near baseline richness, even after the harsh 3-drug cocktail (1.5 months).
  • 2 to 12 months: some taxa still missing, resistome still different. Longer for clindamycin, ciprofloxacin, azithromycin.
  • Years: a lasting "fingerprint" is reported for clindamycin (Jernberg, 2 years) and at population level for clindamycin, fluoroquinolones and flucloxacillin (Baldanzi, 4 to 8 years).

What fraction fully recover

  • No study gives a clean percentage. inferred Pattern: most healthy adults return to a similar but not identical state. Dethlefsen: 3 of 3 ended in a new stable state. Palleja: most of 12 still missing several of 9 core species at 6 months. Anthony: "a subset" kept low diversity. Treat "full return to the exact pre-antibiotic state" as uncommon, and "functionally fine" as the usual outcome.

What is lost and what blooms

  • Lost: Bifidobacterium (Palleja, Stewardson, Elvers, Sewunet); butyrate producers including Faecalibacterium, Coprococcus, Ruminococcus (Zaura, Palleja, Stewardson); Bacteroides diversity (Jernberg); Clostridiales that make secondary bile acids (Vrieze, Ayele); archaea Methanobrevibacter (Palleja). verified
  • Bloom: Enterobacteriaceae and Proteobacteria (Palleja, Vrieze, Ayele), Enterococcus faecalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum (Palleja), Enterobacter cloacae (Raymond, low-diversity starters), Lachnoclostridium bolteae (Raymond). verified
  • Resistome: every study that looked found resistance genes enriched after the course, often for genes unrelated to the drug given (Anthony: cfxA, tetO, tet40 after azithromycin/cefpodoxime). Clindamycin-linked resistance genes persisted up to 2 years (Jernberg). verified

4. Who recovers slower or gets hit harder

  • Repeat courses: second cipro course gave a different and still incomplete recovery (Dethlefsen 2011) verified. Diversity fell with each extra course, most for the first two (Baldanzi preprint) reported. Kids with frequent courses had lower species and strain diversity and more resistance genes (Yassour 2016, via Genome Med review table) reported.
  • Low starting diversity: predicted Enterobacter blooms after cefprozil (Raymond 2016) verified.
  • Drug choice: clindamycin, fluoroquinolones (cipro), azithromycin, 3rd-gen cephalosporins and broad combos slowest; amoxicillin, penicillin V, nitrofurantoin fastest verified across section 2 and 3.
  • Long courses: CDC says longer courses may double C. diff risk reported. Cumulative days linked to IBD and other outcomes in dose-response fashion (Faye 2023) verified.
  • Age: 65 and over is a CDC C. diff risk factor reported. Infants under 6 months and repeated courses carry the clearest childhood weight signal (Rasmussen 2018) verified. IBD link strongest at 40+ (Faye 2023) verified.
  • Hospital and IV: hospital stays, nursing homes and weakened immunity are CDC risk factors reported. IV drugs with heavy bile excretion (ceftriaxone, piperacillin-tazobactam) still reach the gut; DAV132 trial showed fecal drug levels drive the damage (Messaoudene 2024, PMID 39278946) verified. IV is not automatically gentler inferred.
  • PPIs (acid blockers): observational meta-analysis of 50 studies: CDI OR 1.26 (Cao 2018, PMID 28842261) verified. Negative mirror: RCT meta-analysis of 8 trials, 29,880 people: no significant increase (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.89) (Floria 2025, PMID 41047657, https://doi.org/10.1080/19490976.2025.2562341) verified. So the PPI link may be confounding (sicker people take PPIs).
  • Previous C. diff is a CDC risk factor reported.

5. Long-term links reported (all observational)

Common caveat: people who get antibiotics are sicker, see doctors more, and have infections. Infection itself, not the drug, may drive these links ("confounding by indication"). None of these prove causation.

  • IBD: Denmark, 6.1 million people, 52,898 new IBD cases. Any antibiotic vs none: IRR 1.28 (age 10 to 40), 1.48 (40 to 60), 1.47 (60+). Dose-response; peak 1 to 2 years after; highest with drugs used for gut infections (which could mean early undiagnosed IBD was being treated as infection). Faye 2023, Gut, PMID 36623926, https://doi.org/10.1136/gutjnl-2022-327845 verified
  • Colorectal cancer: UK, 28,980 cases vs 137,077 controls. Antibiotics linked to more colon cancer (dose-dependent; ampicillin/amoxicillin aOR 1.09) but less rectal cancer (>60 days, aOR 0.85). Link seen for exposure more than 10 years before diagnosis (aOR 1.17). Zhang 2019, Gut, PMID 31427405, https://doi.org/10.1136/gutjnl-2019-318593 verified. Small effect sizes (about 9 to 17% relative).
  • Childhood weight: infancy antibiotics OR 1.11 overall; one course not significant; more than one course OR 1.24; first 6 months OR 1.20 (Rasmussen 2018, 527,504 children, PMID 29359849) verified. Larger 2023 meta: OR 1.07 for one course up to 1.31 for 4+ courses, very low certainty (Chagas 2023, PMID 37573229) verified.
  • Allergy/asthma in kids: widely reported link; my PubMed search returned nothing this pass. gap
  • Mood: UK nested case-control: one course linked to depression AOR 1.23 (penicillins) and 1.25 (quinolones); 2 to 5 penicillin courses 1.40; more than 5 courses 1.56. Anxiety similar; no link to psychosis. Lurie 2015, J Clin Psychiatry, PMID 26580313, https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.15m09961 verified. Strong confounding risk (infection and inflammation alone affect mood).
  • Cognition: women with 2+ months of midlife antibiotics had slightly lower scores 7 years later (about equal to 3 to 4 years of aging). Mehta 2022, PMID 35320274 verified.
  • Heart disease: Korea, 2.16 million adults; 365+ cumulative days aHR 1.10; 1 to 14 days no increase (0.98). Park 2025, PMID 41120818 verified. Notable: short courses showed no link.
  • Pregnancy: maternal antibiotics linked to more childhood infections to age 13 (IRR 1.75), but no clear microbiome signal in the babies (Hauerslev 2026, PMID 42372831) verified. A negative mirror for the "microbiome explains it" story.

6. Normal struggle vs see a doctor

Common and usually self-limiting reported: looser or more frequent stools during or shortly after the course, bloating, gas, mild cramps, change in stool smell or colour, mild nausea, vaginal yeast infection. Usually settles within days to a couple of weeks of stopping.

Warning signs (C. diff or other problem) reported CDC lists diarrhea, fever, stomach tenderness or pain, loss of appetite, nausea:

  • Watery diarrhea 3 or more times a day for more than a day or two, especially during or within weeks (up to about 3 months) after antibiotics
  • Fever
  • Blood or pus in stool
  • Belly pain, tenderness or swelling that is getting worse
  • Signs of dehydration (very little urine, dizziness, fast heartbeat)
  • Diarrhea that returns after it had settled

Higher urgency if 65+, recently in hospital, immunocompromised, or had C. diff before. Anti-diarrhea drugs (loperamide) should not be taken for suspected C. diff without medical advice. reported (CDC signs-and-symptoms page was blocked this pass; used CDC "About C. diff" page.)

7. The 15 usual questions

  1. How long until my gut recovers? Most healthy adults are back near baseline within 2 to 8 weeks (diversity recovered by about day 19 for several drugs, 1.5 months even after a harsh 3-drug cocktail). Some species and the resistance gene profile can stay different for 6 to 12 months or longer.
  2. Does one course ruin it forever? No evidence it "ruins" it. Most people end up functionally normal. But exact return is uncommon: some species may not come back, and a Swedish study links single courses of clindamycin, fluoroquinolones or flucloxacillin to measurable differences 4 to 8 years later (observational).
  3. Which antibiotics are hardest on the gut? Clindamycin, ciprofloxacin, broad cephalosporins, azithromycin (slower recovery), and oral vancomycin. Gentlest: penicillin V, amoxicillin, nitrofurantoin, rifaximin.
  4. Should I take probiotics at the same time? For preventing diarrhea, trials say they help: in children, AAD 8% vs 19% (NNT 9; NNT 6 for 5+ billion CFU/day), mainly L. rhamnosus GG or S. boulardii (Cochrane, Guo 2019). For C. diff, they reduce risk mainly when baseline risk is over 5% (3.1% vs 11.6%; NNT 12), not in low-risk people (Cochrane, Goldenberg 2017, PMID 29257353, https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006095.pub4). AGA 2020 guideline gives a conditional recommendation for some strains in people on antibiotics (PMID 32531291; full text not opened). Not advised for severely ill or immunocompromised people.
  5. Do probiotics help my microbiome recover afterwards? Probably not, and possibly the opposite: in Suez 2018 an 11-strain probiotic delayed native recovery vs doing nothing. Small study (n=21), one product.
  6. What is the best thing for recovery? No trial-proven method beyond time. Fibre-rich diet is plausible because butyrate producers feed on fibre inferred. Own-stool FMT worked in Suez but is not a consumer option.
  7. Is diarrhea on antibiotics normal? Common (5 to 35%). Mild loose stools are expected. Watery diarrhea 3+ times a day, fever, or blood needs a doctor.
  8. Is two courses worse than one? Yes on average: second cipro course produced a different, still incomplete recovery; diversity drops with each course, most for the first two.
  9. Are IV antibiotics safer for the gut? Not necessarily. Many IV drugs reach the gut through bile; damage tracks drug levels in stool.
  10. Can I get C. diff weeks later? Yes. Risk is highest during the course and the month after, and C. diff can appear up to about 3 months later reported.
  11. Will I become resistant? Your gut bacteria will carry more resistance genes for months (seen in every study that looked); this matters for future infections and spread, not for "you" becoming resistant.
  12. Do antibiotics cause weight gain? Small link in infants with repeated courses or exposure under 6 months; one course not significant. Adult evidence weak.
  13. Can antibiotics cause depression or anxiety? Observational link (about 20 to 50% higher odds with repeated courses), but infection and illness are likely confounders. Not proven.
  14. Do they raise IBD or cancer risk? Observational links exist (IBD up about 28 to 48%; colon cancer up about 9 to 17%, rectal cancer slightly down). Absolute risk changes are small and causation unproven.
  15. Should I avoid antibiotics then? No. When an infection needs one, the benefit wins. The useful moves are asking whether it is needed, whether a narrower drug works, and whether the shortest effective course is used inferred consistent with stewardship guidance.

Negative mirrors and contradictions found

  • Amoxicillin: little diversity change (Elvers, Vrieze, Zaura) yet most resistance genes (Zaura summary). "Gentle" on diversity is not gentle on resistome.
  • Levofloxacin/moxifloxacin: minimal disruption in healthy volunteers (Anthony, Ayele), contradicting cipro's harsh record and the high CDI odds for the class. Suggests class labels hide drug-level differences.
  • Amox-clav: back to baseline by follow-up (Sewunet, sponsor-funded) despite high AAD reputation.
  • Low-dose minocycline 16 weeks: no microbiome change (Gallo 2025, industry-linked).
  • PPIs: observational yes, RCTs no.
  • Rifaximin: "minimal effect" evidence mostly from manufacturer-authored review and tiny cohorts.
  • Maternal antibiotics: health link, no microbiome link.
  • Heart disease cohort: short courses (1 to 14 days) showed no risk.

What helps during and after

What actually helps the gut microbiome during and after antibiotics. Checked 22 Nov 2026. Tags: verified = abstract or primary page read this pass; reported = secondary source; inferred; speculated. Doses and strains are given only as used in studies.

0. Baseline: what happens with no help at all

  • Healthy men given a 4-day triple last-resort antibiotic cocktail (meropenem, gentamicin, vancomycin), n=12, shotgun metagenomics: near-baseline composition back within ~1.5 months, but 9 common species that every subject had before were still undetectable in most subjects at day 180. Early blooms of Enterobacteria, E. faecalis, F. nucleatum; Bifidobacterium and butyrate producers lost. Palleja 2018, Nat Microbiol. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-018-0257-9 verified
  • Two ciprofloxacin courses in 3 people over 10 months: diversity loss within 3–4 days; return began ~1 week after each course but was often incomplete; communities settled into an altered stable state. Dethlefsen & Relman 2011, PNAS. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1000087107 verified
  • Children: meta-analysis of 12 studies; macrolides reduced richness about twice as long as penicillin; azithromycin RCTs pooled Shannon diversity MD −0.86. McDonnell 2021, Gut Microbes. https://doi.org/10.1080/19490976.2020.1870402 verified
  • AAD baseline rate in placebo arms: ~19% children (Guo 2019), ~18.7% mixed (Szajewska 2015), 10.4% in hospitalised over-65s (PLACIDE). C. diff diarrhoea in placebo arms: 4.0% (Goldenberg 2017). verified sources below
  • Plain takeaway: most healthy guts largely bounce back by themselves in weeks, with some species lost for 6+ months. Any "helper" has to beat this natural rebound, and some do not. inferred

1. Probiotics for antibiotic-associated diarrhoea (AAD) and C. diff

How it works (plain words): live microbes pass through while the antibiotic has cleared space; they may crowd out pathogens, calm inflammation and support the lining. They mostly do not move in permanently. inferred

Best human evidence

  • Cochrane, Goldenberg 2017 (C. diff prevention): 31 RCTs, 8,672 people. C. diff diarrhoea 1.5% (70/4,525) on probiotics vs 4.0% (164/4,147) control; RR 0.40 (0.30–0.52), moderate certainty; NNT 42. Effect concentrated where baseline risk >5%: 3.1% vs 11.6%, RR 0.30, NNT 12. No benefit where baseline risk ≤5%. Did not reduce C. diff detected in stool (15.5% vs 17.0%, RR 0.86 ns), meaning it seems to block disease rather than colonisation. Adverse events lower on probiotics (RR 0.83, very low certainty). https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006095.pub4 verified
  • Cochrane, Guo 2019 (children AAD): 33 RCTs, 6,352 children. AAD 8% vs 19%; RR 0.45 (0.36–0.56); NNT 9, moderate certainty. Dose mattered: ≥5 billion CFU/day RR 0.37, NNT 6; <5 billion RR 0.68 (borderline). Diarrhoea shortened by ~0.9 day. No serious adverse events attributable in 24 trials; the authors note case reports of serious events in debilitated or immunocompromised children with central lines. 20 of 33 trials high risk of bias. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004827.pub5 verified
  • Hempel 2012, JAMA: 82 RCTs; pooled 63 RCTs, 11,811 people: RR 0.58 (0.50–0.68), risk difference −7%, NNT 13. Strains poorly documented; heterogeneity substantial. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.3507 verified
  • Timing, Shen 2017 Gastroenterology (meta-regression, 19 studies, 6,261 hospitalised adults): CDI 1.6% vs 3.9%, RR 0.42. Starting within 2 days of the first antibiotic dose: RR 0.32; starting later: RR 0.70 (ns). Efficacy fell with each day of delay. https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2017.02.003 verified
  • "Take 2 hours apart from the antibiotic": no trial evidence found in this pass; it is common label and pharmacist advice for bacterial probiotics. Not relevant to S. boulardii (a yeast, untouched by antibacterials). inferred

What failed or argues against

  • PLACIDE, Allen 2013 Lancet: largest trial; 2,941 inpatients ≥65 analysed; multistrain lactobacilli + bifidobacteria, 6×10^10 organisms/day for 21 days. AAD 10.8% vs 10.4% (RR 1.04, 0.84–1.28); C. diff diarrhoea 0.8% vs 1.2% (RR 0.71, ns). Null. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(13)61218-0 verified
  • The positive meta-analyses lean on many small, high or unclear risk-of-bias trials (Goldenberg: 27/39 high or unclear; Guo: 20/33 high). The biggest well-run trial is null. verified
  • AGA 2020 guideline (Su et al., Gastroenterology): for adults and children on antibiotics, suggests (conditional, low-quality evidence) one of: S. boulardii; 2-strain L. acidophilus CL1285 + L. casei LBC80R; 3-strain L. acidophilus + L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus + B. bifidum; 4-strain same + S. salivarius subsp. thermophilus. States people who value avoiding cost or harm, especially outpatients at low C. diff risk, may reasonably choose none. For C. diff treatment: probiotics only within trials; L. rhamnosus ATCC 53103 (= LGG) was linked to higher recurrence vs placebo in one trial. https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2020.05.059 (full text read via mirrored PDF at spg.pt) verified
  • IDSA/SHEA 2021 C. diff guideline position on probiotics not checked this pass. gap

Verdict: Likely for reducing AAD (strongest in children and at high dose) and C. diff in high-risk inpatients; Unproven for low-risk healthy outpatients and for "restoring" the microbiome (see section 4).

2. Saccharomyces boulardii

How it works: a probiotic yeast. Antibacterial drugs do not kill it, so it survives the course; proposed actions include a protease that degrades C. diff toxins, and immune and barrier effects. reported mechanism not re-checked this pass

Best evidence: Szajewska 2015, Aliment Pharmacol Ther: 21 RCTs, 4,780 people. AAD 18.7% → 8.5%; RR 0.47 (0.38–0.57); NNT 10. Children 20.9% → 8.8% (RR 0.43); adults 17.4% → 8.2% (RR 0.49). C. diff diarrhoea reduced in children only (2 RCTs, n=579, RR 0.25); not in adults (9 RCTs, n=1,441, RR 0.80, 0.47–1.34). https://doi.org/10.1111/apt.13344 verified

  • Listed first in AGA 2020 options (above). verified

What argues against: no significant C. diff effect in adults in the meta-analysis; live yeast fungaemia case reports in critically ill or central-line patients reported Guo 2019 notes serious events in debilitated patients generally. Not a microbiome "rebuilder": it does not replace lost bacteria. inferred

Verdict: Likely for preventing AAD (both ages). Plausible for C. diff in adults. Avoid framing it as restoring diversity.

3. LGG, multi-strain products, kefir and yogurt

  • LGG, Szajewska 2015 APT: 12 RCTs, 1,499 people. AAD 22.4% → 12.3%, RR 0.49 (low quality). Children significant (RR 0.48, moderate quality); adults not significant (RR 0.48, 0.20–1.15) except H. pylori therapy subgroup (RR 0.26). https://doi.org/10.1111/apt.13404 verified AGA flagged LGG with higher C. diff recurrence in a treatment trial. verified
  • Multi-strain: AGA picks only specific 2-, 3- and 4-strain combos (above). PLACIDE's multi-strain product failed. "More strains = better" has no support. verified /inferred
  • Kefir, MILK trial, Merenstein 2009: 125 children 1–5 y on antibiotics; diarrhoea 18% kefir vs 21.9% placebo (RR 0.82, 0.54–1.43), null. https://doi.org/10.1001/archpediatrics.2009.119 verified
  • Yogurt for AAD: not searched in depth this pass; commercial yogurts usually carry far below the ≥5 billion CFU/day that worked in Guo 2019. inferred

Verdict: LGG Likely in children, Plausible in adults. Kefir/yogurt for AAD Unproven (one null RCT for kefir). Generic multi-strain blends Unproven unless the exact AGA combos.

4. Counter-evidence: do probiotics slow microbiome recovery?

  • Suez 2018, Cell (mice + humans, invasive colonoscopic sampling; human arm small, ~21 people across spontaneous, probiotic and aFMT arms reported exact n not in abstract): after broad antibiotics, an 11-strain probiotic colonised the human mucosa better than in non-antibiotic gut, but caused "markedly delayed and persistently incomplete" return of native stool and mucosal microbiome and host gene expression vs spontaneous recovery. Autologous FMT restored near-baseline within days. Lactobacillus-secreted factors inhibited regrowth in vitro. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.047 verified. The "up to 5 months" figure is widely quoted from the paper's follow-up reported not visible in abstract.
  • Montassier 2021, Nat Microbiol (same group, reanalysis): after antibiotics, probiotics worsened expansion of antibiotic-resistance genes in the gut mucosa by supporting strains carrying vancomycin resistance genes; aFMT or spontaneous recovery mitigated it. Not seen in stool samples. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-021-00920-0 verified
  • Éliás 2023, BMC Med (meta-analysis of RCTs, 15 in review, 5 pooled): probiotics during antibiotics made no difference to Shannon (MD 0.23, ns), Chao1 or OTUs. Both groups tended back to baseline in 3–8 weeks. Small samples. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-023-02961-0 verified
  • Balance: no good evidence probiotics reduce diversity on standard stool measures; the delay signal comes from one small, invasive study (with mucosal biopsies). Probiotics prevent a symptom (diarrhoea); they do not rebuild the native community. inferred

Verdict: "Probiotics restore your microbiome after antibiotics" = Myth (no support; one signal of harm to recovery). "Probiotics delay recovery" = Plausible (one small study, not replicated).

5. Fibre and prebiotics

How it works: fibre is food for native fermenters that survived; they make short-chain fatty acids (butyrate) that feed the lining and favour regrowth of anaerobes. inferred

Evidence

  • Ng 2019, Cell Host Microbe (mice carrying human microbiota): a fibre-deficient diet worsened collapse during antibiotics and delayed recovery; single housing (no reseeding from others) badly disrupted recovery. Mouse only. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2019.10.011 verified
  • GOS during amoxicillin, Br J Nutr 2014: 12 healthy adults, 2.5 g GOS three times daily during and 7 days after amoxicillin vs maltodextrin. Bifidobacteria trended higher (ns), butyrate significantly higher after antibiotics. Tiny pilot. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114514001135 verified
  • Synbiotic in toddlers after amoxicillin (TOBBI): protocol only, results not yet published as of this check. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-025-05405-1 verified
  • American Gut, McDonald 2018 mSystems: >10,000 citizen samples; eating more distinct plants associated with higher diversity. The "30+ plants per week" cut-off is from this project's comparison reported not in the abstract; it is cross-sectional, self-selected, and not about antibiotics. https://doi.org/10.1128/mSystems.00031-18 verified for abstract
  • Inulin, resistant starch, PHGG specifically after antibiotics: no human RCT found this pass. Wastyk 2021 high-fibre arm did not raise diversity over 10 weeks in healthy adults (below). verified for Wastyk

Verdict: whole-food fibre after antibiotics Plausible (strong mouse data, tiny human pilot). Isolated prebiotic supplements post-antibiotic Unproven. "30 plants a week" as a specific threshold Plausible (association only).

6. Fermented foods

  • Wastyk 2021, Cell: 17-week randomised study, n=18 per arm (36 healthy adults). High-fermented-food diet steadily raised microbiome diversity and lowered inflammatory markers; 19 blood inflammatory proteins fell, including IL-6, and 4 immune cell types showed less activation. High-fibre arm: diversity stable, primary outcome (cytokine response score) unchanged in both. Larger servings gave stronger effects. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2021.06.019 verified abstract; Stanford release https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation verified
  • Not done in people after antibiotics; small n; healthy volunteers only. Kefir null for AAD (section 3). verified

Verdict: Plausible for rebuilding diversity in the weeks after a course (good RCT in healthy people, untested post-antibiotic). Unproven for preventing AAD.

7. FMT: autologous banking, donor FMT, approved microbiome drugs

  • Autologous FMT (aFMT): Suez 2018 (near-complete recovery in days, above). Taur 2018, Sci Transl Med: RCT in allo stem-cell transplant patients, 25 analysed (14 aFMT vs 11 none); aFMT restored diversity and the pre-antibiotic composition. https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.aap9489 verified. Not an available consumer option; stool banking is done only in research or specialist settings. inferred
  • Donor FMT for recurrent C. diff, Cochrane Minkoff 2023: 6 RCTs, 320 adults; resolution RR 1.92 (1.36–2.71), NNT 3, moderate certainty; serious adverse events RR 0.73 (wide CI). https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013871.pub2 verified
  • Rebyota (RBX2660), rectal single dose, FDA approval 30 Nov 2022 reported via ACP Gastroenterology; PUNCH CD3, 267 treated: Bayesian treatment success 70.6% vs 57.5% placebo (difference 13.1%, posterior probability of superiority 0.991); >90% of 8-week responders sustained to 6 months; more mild GI adverse events. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40265-022-01797-x verified
  • Vowst (SER-109), oral purified Firmicutes spores, 4 capsules daily × 3 days, FDA approval 26 Apr 2023 for preventing recurrence after treatment for recurrent CDI, adults. ECOSPOR III, 182 patients with ≥3 episodes: recurrence 12% vs 40% at 8 weeks (RR 0.32); NNT ≈4 inferred from figures. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2106516 verified; FDA release https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-orally-administered-fecal-microbiota-product-prevention-recurrence-clostridioides verified
  • Note the placebo baseline: 40–58% did fine without the product (Rebyota placebo 57.5% success).

Verdict: donor FMT and Vowst/Rebyota for recurrent C. diff Proven. aFMT to restore microbiome after antibiotics Likely (two small RCT-type studies) but not consumer-available. Any FMT for ordinary courses in healthy people Unproven.

9. Peptides tie-in (light)

  • BPC-157: 15-amino-acid fragment described as derived from a gastric juice protein; decades of rodent work from one Zagreb group on GI lesions, NSAID injury and fistulas reported not re-read this pass. No study on BPC-157 and microbiome composition exists in PubMed (search "BPC 157 gut microbiota" = 0 results, 22 Nov 2026) verified. All three human publications are by Edwin Lee at a private Florida clinic (Institute for Hormonal Balance, Orlando, per the 2021 paper), all in Altern Ther Health Med: (1) retrospective phone survey, 16 knee-pain patients, 14/16 reported relief, no controls (2021) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34324435/; (2) 12 women, interstitial cystitis, bladder injection, uncontrolled (2024) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39325560/; (3) IV safety "pilot", n=2, both prior users (2025) https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40131143/ verified. None are GI. That the clinic sells it is per the brief reported not independently checked. Verdict for gut repair after antibiotics: Unproven (animal only).
  • KPV (Lys-Pro-Val, fragment of α-MSH): enters gut cells via PepT1 transporter, damps NF-κB; oral KPV reduced DSS and TNBS colitis in mice. Dalmasso 2008 Gastroenterology https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2007.10.026; nanoparticle delivery in mice, Xiao 2017 https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymthe.2016.11.020 verified. No human data. Unproven.
  • LL-37: human cathelicidin, the body's own antimicrobial peptide. No human supplementation trial on the gut microbiome checked this pass; as an antimicrobial, adding it could in theory hit commensals too speculated. Unproven.
  • Larazotide (AT-1001): tight-junction (zonulin) blocker. Meta-analysis of 4 RCTs, 626 celiac patients: no difference in lactulose:mannitol permeability; some symptom benefit only under gluten challenge. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinre.2021.101782 verified. Phase 3 (CedLara) discontinued June 2022 after interim analysis reported Celiac Disease Foundation, BioSpace. Unproven / failed for its main indication; never tested after antibiotics.
  • GLP-2 / teduglutide: approved drug that grows small-bowel absorptive surface in short bowel syndrome (STEPS programme; post hoc citrulline analysis https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clnu.2019.11.001 verified). Not studied for antibiotic recovery; no microbiome RCT found. Irrelevant to healthy people. Unproven for this use.
  • Peptides and microbiome composition in humans: no study found for any of these. verified by negative search, BPC-157 only; others inferred

10. Lifestyle

  • Avoid unnecessary courses: each course causes a fast diversity drop and, with repeats, incomplete return to baseline (Dethlefsen 2011); some species gone at 6 months even after one 4-day course (Palleja 2018). The strongest "intervention" is not taking antibiotics that are not needed. verified data; inferred advice Proven that courses disrupt; avoiding unneeded ones is the most reliable protection.
  • Exercise: Allen 2018 MSSE, 32 previously sedentary adults, 6 weeks endurance training: microbiota shifts depended on obesity status; faecal SCFAs rose in lean only; changes largely reversed after 6 weeks of stopping. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001495 verified. Not tested post-antibiotic. Plausible.
  • Artificial sweeteners: Suez 2022 Cell, RCT 120 healthy adults, 2 weeks of saccharin, sucralose, aspartame or stevia sachets below ADI: each altered stool and oral microbiome; saccharin and sucralose impaired glucose responses; effect transferable to germ-free mice, person-specific. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.07.016 verified. Not studied during antibiotics. Plausible that limiting them helps a disturbed microbiome inferred.
  • Sleep, alcohol: not researched this pass. Unproven pending evidence. gap
  • Environmental reseeding (contact with other people, pets, outdoors): mouse data show isolation badly delays recovery (Ng 2019). Plausible, human data absent. verified mouse
