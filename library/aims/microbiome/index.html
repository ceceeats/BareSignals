Microbiome testing & adding strains

What a gut test can tell you, what it cannot, and whether a new strain ever stays

Draft Version 122 Nov 202638 sources
Testing Strains Probiotics FMT

What it is. Two questions people ask together: how do I find out what is living in my gut, and can I put something new in there. They have different answers, and both are more interesting than the kits suggest.

What studies found. One stool sample split between 7 companies produced 1,208 organisms, with only 3 genera agreeing across all of them. No consumer test is validated at strain level. Adding a strain does work, in about 30% of people for the best-studied one, and only where there is a vacancy, an exclusive food, and weeks of dosing.

What people report. Nothing yet. This first pass collected studies only; first-hand reports come next, and the page says so rather than filling the gap.

Watch out. Engrafting and helping are separate things. The clearest trials got bacteria to settle in and measured no benefit at all, and the one famous metabolic result came from dead bacteria.

1 What a test is looking at

Genus, species, strain

Almost every argument about these tests comes down to one thing: how far down this ladder the method can actually see. Most stop two rungs above where the interesting answer lives.

GenusLactobacillusA family of related species. This is where a 16S test reliably stops.
SpeciesLactobacillus rhamnosusShotgun sequencing reaches here routinely.
StrainL. rhamnosus GGThe level every probiotic study is actually about. Shotgun can sometimes reach it for abundant organisms at high read depth. No consumer test is validated here.

Why it matters: two strains of one species can differ by hundreds of genes. One strain of E. coli lives in you harmlessly and another causes outbreaks. So a report saying you have Bifidobacterium longum does not tell you whether you have the strain from any study or any product.

Strains themselves are stable and real. Tracking people for up to five years with cultured isolates and whole-genome sequencing found most strains persist for what looks like decades, and that family members share strains while unrelated people do not.33 But that took culturing hundreds of isolates in a research lab, not a kit through the post.

Every method, and what it can see

16S sequencingGenusEntry kits

Reads one short stretch of a single bacterial barcode gene. Cheap, everywhere, decades of reference data behind it. The limit is hard: short reads of one or two variable regions cannot separate close species, and even full-length reads need special handling to approach strain level.1 Bacteria only, so fungi and viruses are invisible. Any “function” in the report is predicted from the names, not measured.

Shotgun sequencingSpecies, sometimes strainMid and premium kits

Reads random fragments of everything in the sample. Gets species routinely, picks up fungi and viruses, and can read actual genes including antibiotic resistance genes. Strain-level calls are possible but only for abundant species at high read depth with specialist tools. Depth varies enormously between companies: in the comparison below it ran from under 20,000 reads to over 10 million, and more reads did not mean more organisms found.2

RNA (activity) testsSpecies plus activityActivity kits

Reads which genes are switched on rather than which are present, which is genuinely the more interesting question. RNA degrades fast, and the main method paper behind the consumer version is written entirely by employees of the company selling it, describing stability only in several small studies.3 No independent multi-lab validation was found.

PCR panelsOnly what is on the listPractitioner panels

Counts copies of a fixed list of DNA targets. Fast and quantitative for the things on the list, and blind to everything else. Hospital versions for infectious diarrhoea are properly cleared and useful. The international consensus is explicit that multiplex PCR is not microbiome testing and is not a stand-in for it.4 A PubMed search for validation of the commercial functional-medicine panels returned nothing.

Stool cultureSpecies, strain if typedHospital

Grows organisms on plates. Still the gold standard for named pathogens like Salmonella, Shigella and Campylobacter, and the only method that gives you antibiotic sensitivity. Most gut bacteria are anaerobes that will not grow this way, so it cannot profile a community, and the consensus says the same thing it says about PCR.4

Breath testsNo organisms namedSIBO kits

Measures hydrogen and methane after a sugar drink: gas made by fermentation. Clinically real, with agreed cutoffs (hydrogen up 20 ppm by 90 minutes, methane 10 ppm).5 It names no organism. Lactulose can reach the colon fast and read as a false positive; glucose is absorbed early and can miss the far end of the small intestine.

CalprotectinNot a microbe testVia a doctor

A protein from white blood cells, so it measures inflammation rather than bacteria. Genuinely useful as a rule-out: at 40 µg/g or below in someone with IBS-type symptoms, the chance of inflammatory bowel disease is 1% or less.6 It does not tell IBS apart from healthy.

Zonulin ("leaky gut")Does not workAdd-on

This one is worth naming plainly. When researchers put the commercial kits through mass spectrometry, they found the kits were not detecting zonulin at all: they were picking up haptoglobin and complement C3, different proteins entirely.7 A result from one of these is not a measurement of your gut barrier.

The one-line truth under all of it: nearly every consumer test reads DNA or RNA from one stool sample. That tells you what was passing through the end of your gut on one day, at whatever resolution the method allows.

2 How much to trust a result

One sample, seven companies

This is the study to know about. A single donor's stool was homogenised into a reference material so that every aliquot was genuinely identical, then sent out as 21 kits to 7 direct-to-consumer companies, who were not told until afterwards.2

1,208different organisms reported between them, from the same stool
3genera that appeared in every single result
17 of 18common genera where the method mattered as much as the person
6 to 98%agreement between one company's own three repeats

Read the third number again, because it is the one that undermines the product. For 17 of 18 common genera, the variation caused by the testing method was equal to or bigger than real biological variation between people. If the instrument moves the needle as much as the thing being measured, the reading is not telling you about you.

Then the details. C. difficile was reported present by 3 companies and absent by 4, from the same stool. One company rated two of its own replicates healthy and the third unhealthy. One replicate had more than half its reads unidentified and still produced a consumer report. Read depth ran from under 20,000 to over 10 million, and more reads did not mean more organisms found: one company reported over 600 species from 20 to 30 thousand reads, another 95 to 99 species from over 10 million. The companies' "healthy ranges" generally disagreed with each other.

Even done perfectly, one sample is one day

Set the consumer kits aside, because research labs have the same problem in milder form. Blinded samples sent to 15 labs and run through 9 analysis pipelines showed most of the variation came from the sample type, then from how the DNA was extracted, then from handling and software.25 Which step you used changes the answer.

And your own gut moves. Across 713 daily samples from 20 women over 6 weeks, for 78% of genera the day-to-day swing within one person was larger than the differences between people, with shifts up to a hundredfold.24 Stool moisture drove most of it. The authors' recommendation was repeated sampling, which is the opposite of how these kits are sold.

There is also a sampling problem nobody can solve by spending more: stool is the exit pipe. It reflects the far end of the colon, correlates only partly with the gut wall,10 and barely sees the small intestine, which is where SIBO happens.

What counts as a healthy microbiome

Nobody has agreed. Researchers in the field wrote in 2024 that "dysbiosis" is used without precision or definition, and that it is hard even to define who the healthy reference population would be.26

The international consensus, 69 experts across 18 countries, went further and said what reports should stop doing: do not report the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio (86% agreement), dysbiosis indices are not yet supported (90%), and strict healthy reference ranges are not supported (90%).4 Those three things are the backbone of most consumer reports.

The best research scores do better than nothing and not by much: around 74% and 80% balanced accuracy at separating people with some disease from people with none.27,28 About 1 in 4 people land in the wrong group, and the score does not say which disease. That is a group-level research tool, not a verdict on a person.

3 Can you add a strain, and does it stay

Twelve findings that answer it

Tap through these in order: they build into one answer. Short version up front, because it deserves saying plainly: yes, sometimes, rarely, and it is not random. Whether a strain sticks is decided mostly by the gut you already have, not by the dose on the label.

The pattern, in one place. Every success in this field has three things, and every failure is missing at least one.

  • A vacancy. Antibiotics, bowel lavage, infancy, or a species you genuinely lack. Engraftment after a faecal transplant tracks how disturbed the recipient's gut was beforehand, and is boosted by clearing it first.14
  • A food or a job the residents cannot cover. The infant strain came with milk sugars only it could digest.9 The approved C. diff product works by restoring bile-acid chemistry, a job the missing bacteria used to do.22
  • Weeks of repeated dosing. The vaginal product that worked dosed for 11 weeks after an antibiotic cleared the field, and was still detectable in 79% of women at 12 weeks.36

A healthy adult swallowing a shelf capsule has none of the three. The honest expected outcome is transit: it passes through, there may be a brief individual blip at the gut wall, and a month later nothing is detectable.

The finding that matters most

Engraftment and benefit come apart. If you take one thing from this page, take this, because almost all marketing in the category quietly assumes the opposite.

In obesity, donor bacteria engrafted and persisted for 12 weeks and nothing metabolic happened.19 In 87 adolescents, the community shift held to 12 weeks and the trial missed its primary endpoint.20 The famous Akkermansia result came from dead bacteria, which cannot colonise anything.12 And the best-documented adult engraftment, 6 months in 30% of people, changed the resident community not at all.8

So there are two questions, and they need answering separately: did it move in, and did it do anything. A product that can show the first has not shown the second.

Can a test tell you what to eat

This is where the evidence is strongest, and it is still modest. In 1,002 adults, the gut microbiome explained 7.1% of the variation in blood fat responses to meals and 6.0% for glucose. The meal's own makeup explained 3.6% and 15.4%, and genes 9.5% for glucose.29 The microbiome is a real but minor term in that equation.

The best trial: 347 adults, 18 weeks, a programme using food data, glucose and fat responses, microbiome and history, against standard dietary advice. Triglycerides fell 0.13 mmol/L more than control. LDL did not move. Weight, waist, HbA1c and diet quality improved as secondary measures; blood pressure, insulin, glucose and ApoB did not.30 The control group also got less contact, so the app and coaching cannot be separated from the test.

Against it: a 204-person randomised trial of a personalised glucose-response diet for weight loss was null, 3.26% against 4.31% for a plain low-fat diet.31 An earlier prediabetes trial of an algorithm diet beat a Mediterranean diet on time above target glucose, with an HbA1c difference of about 0.08 percentage points.32

For test-guided probiotics specifically, searched for and not found: no randomised trial shows that picking a probiotic from a stool test beats not doing so.

4 When testing is actually worth it

The tests that answer a real question

These are ordinary medicine, ordered by a doctor, and the consensus is clear that they are a different thing from "microbiome testing".4

  • C. difficile test for diarrhoea during or after antibiotics. The one that matters most on the antibiotics page.
  • Stool culture or a cleared PCR panel for infectious diarrhoea, which also gives antibiotic sensitivity.
  • Ova and parasites where the history suggests it.
  • Calprotectin to rule out inflammatory bowel disease: at 40 µg/g or below, the chance of IBD is 1% or less.6
  • Breath testing for SIBO or lactose and fructose problems, with the standard cutoffs.5
  • Donor screening before a faecal transplant, and the death below is why.

Red flags that need a doctor rather than a kit: blood in stool, unexplained weight loss, symptoms that wake you at night, or a change in bowel habit over the age of 45.

What the experts actually agreed

69 experts from 18 countries, Delphi method, agreement counted at 80% or above.4

  • Not enough evidence to recommend routine microbiome testing (90%).
  • Patients ordering tests themselves without a clinical reason is discouraged (80%).
  • Testing over time may help in specific clinical situations (80%).
  • 16S and shotgun are both acceptable for profiling (98%); culture and multiplex PCR are not microbiome tests (90%).
  • Do not report the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio (86%). Dysbiosis indices not supported (90%). Strict healthy ranges not supported (90%).
  • Testing providers should not give treatment advice (98%), and management changes only through the referring doctor (100%).
  • Do not stop medicines or change your diet before testing unless your doctor says so (100%).
  • Labs should be accredited and regulated (100%), and should be transparent about how scarce the evidence is (100%).

Warnings

Transplanting a community has killed someone. Two patients developed drug-resistant E. coli bloodstream infections traced by genomic sequencing to the same screened donor, and one of them died. Both were immunocompromised.23 Screening was and remains imperfect. Nothing resembling this belongs outside medical care.

Do not act on a consumer report alone. Given that the same stool can come back healthy and unhealthy from one company, treating a report as a reason to start a restrictive diet, a supplement stack, or to stop a medicine is acting on noise. The consensus says explicitly not to change diet or medicines around testing without your doctor.4

Live products are not risk-free for everyone. If your immune system is suppressed, or you have a central line, live bacteria and live yeast are a conversation with your doctor rather than a purchase.

A normal-looking report does not rule anything out. No microbiome test can confirm or exclude IBS, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease or cancer.

5 Usual questions

What people ask

Can a test tell me which strains I have?

Not reliably, and this is the single most misunderstood thing about these kits. 16S tests stop at genus, which is the level of “Lactobacillus” rather than “L. rhamnosus GG”.1 Shotgun tests reach species, and strain only for abundant organisms at high depth. No consumer test is validated at strain level. This matters because probiotic evidence is strain-specific: knowing you carry a species tells you nothing about whether you carry the strain that was studied.

Would two companies give me the same answer?

Mostly not. One stool sample was homogenised and sent as 21 kits to 7 companies. They reported 1,208 different organisms between them, and only 3 genera appeared in every single result. For 17 of 18 common genera, the variation caused by the testing method was as large as or larger than real differences between people. C. difficile was reported present by 3 companies and absent by 4.2

Would the same company give the same answer twice?

Sometimes. In that study, agreement between a company's own three replicates of the identical sample ranged from 6% to 98% of genera. One company called the same sample healthy twice and unhealthy once.2

Is one sample enough?

It is one day, and your gut moves more than you would think. Across 713 daily samples from 20 women, for 78% of genera the day-to-day swing within one person was bigger than the differences between people, with shifts up to a hundredfold.24 Stool moisture drove most of it.

Is there a normal range?

No agreed one. The international consensus says strict healthy reference ranges are not supported (90% agreement), that dysbiosis indices are not yet supported (90%), and that the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio should not be reported at all (86%).4 Researchers who work on this say the word “dysbiosis” is used without a definition, and that it is hard even to agree who the healthy reference population is.26

What does a diversity score actually mean?

On average, across populations, higher diversity goes with better health. It is not a diagnosis, it moves day to day, and it cannot tell you what to do. The best research indices get about 74 to 80% accuracy at separating “some disease” from “none”,27,28 which means roughly 1 in 4 people are put in the wrong group, and they do not say which disease.

Can a test tell me what to eat?

A little, and less than the marketing implies. In 1,002 adults, the microbiome explained 7.1% of the variation in how people's blood fats responded to food and 6.0% for glucose: a real but minor input next to the meal itself and your genes.29 The best trial of a programme combining microbiome, glucose and blood fat data in 347 people cut triglycerides by 0.13 mmol/L, with LDL unchanged.30 A weight-loss trial of a similar personalised approach in 204 people was null.31 And in all of these, the coaching and app cannot be separated from the test.

Should I buy the probiotics the report recommends?

No trial has shown that picking probiotics from a stool test beats no probiotic or a standard choice: searched for, not found. The consensus says testing labs should not give treatment advice (98% agreement),4 and the seven-company study noted some companies recommending their own products off the back of the report.2

Will a test show whether my probiotic took?

No, and this is a genuine finding rather than a limitation of cheap kits. When colonization was measured properly with gut-wall biopsies, it could not be told apart by whether the probiotic appeared in stool.10

Should I test before and after antibiotics?

No guideline supports it, and the practical problems stack up: your day-to-day noise can be bigger than the effect, labs disagree with each other, there is no agreed target to recover to, and the result would not change what anyone does. Recovery mostly happens over about 1.5 months regardless.34 The test that is worth having after antibiotics is a C. diff test if you develop diarrhoea.

So can I add a strain at all?

Yes, sometimes, and it is predictable rather than random. One strain persisted 6 months in 30% of people, and who it stuck in could be read off their baseline.8 It needs a vacancy, a food or job the residents cannot cover, and weeks of repeated dosing. A healthy adult taking a shelf capsule has none of those.

Do spore probiotics survive better?

They survive, which is a different claim from colonising. In 11 people with an ileostomy, spores appeared in the small intestine within 3 hours and had germinated by 6 to 7 hours.37 That is an 8-hour window in a gut with no colon, in a manufacturer-funded study. No data was found on whether they persist afterwards.

Is my microbiome permanent?

Largely. Tracking people for up to five years found most strains stay put for what looks like decades, and family members share strains while unrelated people do not.33 That stability is why adding to it is hard, and also why it recovers from disturbance.

When is stool testing genuinely useful?

When a doctor orders it for a question it can answer: a C. diff test for diarrhoea after antibiotics, culture or a cleared PCR panel for infectious diarrhoea, ova and parasites, calprotectin to rule out inflammatory bowel disease, breath testing for SIBO or lactose problems, and donor screening before a transplant. Red flags (blood, weight loss, night symptoms, a change in bowel habit over 45) need a doctor, not a kit.

Is any of this regulated?

Not as diagnostics. No microbiome test is approved as a clinical diagnostic in the US, and these products sit between medical and wellness categories. The consensus called for lab accreditation and regulation with 100% agreement, and for transparency about how scarce the evidence is, also 100%.4

Words on this page

Genus / species / strain
Three zoom levels. Lactobacillus is a genus, L. rhamnosus a species, L. rhamnosus GG a strain. Two strains of one species can differ by hundreds of genes: one harmless, one a toxin maker.
16S
Sequencing one short barcode gene shared by all bacteria. Cheap, reaches genus.
Shotgun
Sequencing random fragments of all the DNA present. Reaches species and reads actual genes.
Engraftment
A newcomer actually settling in and staying, rather than passing through.
Colonization resistance
A full gut keeping newcomers out by leaving them nowhere to live and nothing to eat.
Alpha / beta diversity
Alpha: how varied your own sample is. Beta: how different yours is from someone else’s.
Dysbiosis
A disturbed microbiome. Widely used, with no agreed definition or cutoff.
Autochthonous
Native to the host. A strain originally taken from a human gut, rather than a dairy or lab strain.
FMT
Faecal microbiota transplant: moving a whole stool community from a donor, or from your own banked sample.
Post-hoc
An analysis decided after the results were seen. Much weaker than the question the trial set out to answer.
