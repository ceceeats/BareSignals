# GHK-Cu
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/ghk-cu
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: copper tripeptide-1, prezatide copper, prezatide copper acetate, copper peptide, tripeptide-copper complex
2. Origin: Fragment of a natural protein
3. Sequence: GHK (3 amino acids). Bound to one copper ion; weight shown is GHK alone
4. Legal and approval status: Not an approved drug anywhere. In the US the non-injectable form sits in FDA 503A Category 1 'under evaluation' (except injectable routes); the injectable nomination was withdrawn in April 2026. A compounding advisory meeting is due before end of February 2027. WADA does not name it.

## Summary
5. GHK-Cu is the copper complex of the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, carried naturally in human blood; the copper turns it blue. It is used for skin repair, collagen, wound healing and hair, with plasma levels falling with age as the anti-aging hook. Controlled studies show faster diabetic ulcer closure and thicker skin with topical forms, while injected use has never been studied in humans.
6. Punch line: Blue copper peptide that fades with age. Applied or injected for collagen, firmer skin, wound healing and hair.

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)

## How it works
14. Carries copper to tissue that is rebuilding · Cu(II) delivery · role Tissue repair
   GHK, a tripeptide found in human plasma, binds copper (Cu(II)) tightly and delivers it to tissue. Copper-dependent enzymes such as lysyl oxidase then cross-link new collagen and elastin into strong fibres. Plasma GHK falls with age, though the commonly quoted figures lack a clear primary source.
15. Turns up collagen and elastin making · Fibroblast signalling · role Skin & aesthetics
   In fibroblasts, the cells that make skin and connective tissue, GHK-Cu raises collagen, elastin and glycosaminoglycan synthesis. Small topical human studies report thicker skin and shallower wrinkles, including 40 women over 8 weeks. Injected use has not been studied in people.
16. Remodels scar and wound matrix · MMP and TIMP balance · role Tissue repair
   GHK-Cu shifts the balance between MMPs, enzymes that break down matrix, and TIMPs, their inhibitors. This lets old scar tissue be cleared while new matrix is laid down. A randomised topical trial in diabetic ulcers showed faster closure than vehicle.
17. Enlarges hair follicles in lab studies · Dermal papilla cells · role Hair
   In lab studies GHK-Cu enlarges dermal papilla cells, the signalling cells at the base of each hair follicle. Larger papillae are linked to thicker hair growth. This is cell data only; hair regrowth in people comes from user reports.
18. Lowers inflammatory signals in cell studies · TNF-α, IL-6 down · role Inflammatory signaling
   In cell studies GHK-Cu lowers TNF-α and IL-6, two key inflammatory messengers. Less inflammatory signalling fits calmer healing and less redness. User reports of less redness match this, but it has not been tested as an anti-inflammatory in human trials.
19. Shifts many genes toward repair, in cell data · Gene expression · role Cellular protection
   Gene expression data suggest GHK-Cu shifts many genes toward repair and away from damage. These are broad cell data, mainly from one group; about 35% of the primary literature carries Pickart or Margolina as an author. Treat the scope as claimed, not confirmed.
20. Mechanism overview: GHK is a tripeptide in human plasma that binds copper tightly and declines with age. As GHK-Cu it delivers copper to tissue, where copper-dependent enzymes such as lysyl oxidase cross-link collagen and elastin. In fibroblasts it raises collagen, elastin and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, and shifts the balance between MMPs and their inhibitors, the TIMPs, which remodels scar and wound matrix. Cell work shows lower TNF-α and IL-6 and enlarged hair follicle papilla cells. Broad gene expression shifts toward repair are claimed, mainly by one group.

## What has been tested
46. Tissue repair: Diabetic foot ulcers, gel (trial)
47. Skin & aesthetics: Facial skin, 40 women, 8 weeks (trial)
48. Skin & aesthetics: Skin thickness and wrinkles (human)
49. Tissue repair: Wounds in rabbits and rats (animal)
50. Organ function: Lung fibrosis (mouse)
51. Cognition: Memory in old mice (mouse)
52. Hair: Hair regrowth (users)
53. Human evidence is topical only. A randomised diabetic-ulcer trial (Mulder 1994) showed far faster closure than vehicle. Small facial studies (Leyden 2002; Badenhorst 2016, 40 women, 8 weeks double-blind) reported thicker skin and reduced wrinkle depth, beating vehicle and Matrixyl. Animal work covers rabbit and rat wounds, lung fibrosis and mouse cognition. No human study of injected GHK-Cu exists, and no therapeutic trial is registered; a 2026 review noted about 35% of the primary literature carries Pickart or his colleague Margolina as an author.
54. What it helps: Controlled studies show faster diabetic foot ulcer closure with a gel, and thicker skin with shallower wrinkles in small trials, including 40 women over 8 weeks. Injected use has never been studied in humans. In forums, 71% of clear outcomes were better, mostly smoother skin, less redness and some hair regrowth. Topical users report steadier results; injectors report louder effects with burning at the site.
55. Trial: unclear · Phase 2 · skin wound healing gel · NCT07437586 · Hudson Biotech. Recruiting, but the sponsor is an obscure firm that also lists gray market peptide trials, one titled a mock study. Treat with caution until another sponsor or a result appears. (checked 2026-10-23) [S6] (reported)

## Who shouldn't take it
56. Wilson's disease or copper overload: Each dose delivers copper; people who cannot clear copper accumulate it in liver and brain. (inferred)
57. People with cholestatic liver disease: Copper leaves the body through bile; PBC, PSC and bile duct blockage raise body copper. (inferred)
58. High-dose zinc users or copper-allergic people: Zinc and copper compete; injected copper can cause local reactions in metal-sensitive skin. (inferred)

## Where it comes from
65. The copper(II) complex of the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, isolated from human plasma by Loren Pickart in 1973 and sequenced in 1977. The copper turns it blue. Plasma levels fall from about 200 ng/mL at age 20 to about 80 ng/mL at 60, which is the hook for the anti-aging claims.
66. First studied for: Pickart noticed young blood plasma made old liver tissue behave younger, and traced it to this copper carrier. First developed as a wound-healing agent (ProCyte's Iamin gel), later as a skincare and hair ingredient (copper tripeptide-1).
67. Name story: Gly-His-Lys holding a copper ion. Cosmetic labels call it "copper tripeptide-1"; the official drug name is prezatide copper. [S7] (verified)
68. 1973: Isolated from human plasma by Loren Pickart; sequenced 1977
69. 1980s to 1990s: Wound gel developed; diabetic ulcer trial in 1994
70. 2000s: Becomes copper tripeptide-1 in skincare and hair products
71. 2026: Injectable FDA nomination withdrawn; topical still under review
72. Today: Skin and hair peptide, topical and injected

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### The experiment underneath the claim [cells]
73. The origin story is usually told as "young blood made old tissue act young", which is true but skips the actual design, and the design is what sets the limits of the claim. The work grew out of studies of fibrinogen, a clotting protein that rises with age. Liver tissue from patients aged 60 to 80 showed elevated fibrinogen production. When liver cells from those older patients were incubated in blood from younger people, they behaved almost like younger tissue [1] (verified).
74. So the original observation was about a liver cell's protein output in culture, not about skin, not about collagen, and not about an organism getting younger. The peptide was isolated from human plasma albumin and reported in Nature in 1973 (Pickart and Thaler, 243:85-87), and the sequence was identified four years later in Experientia (Schlesinger, Pickart and Thaler, 1977, 33:324-325) [1] (verified). Everything since has been an extension from that starting point to tissues it was not originally studied in.
75. The widely quoted figures for plasma levels declining with age, roughly 200 nanograms per millilitre at 20 against 80 at 60, circulate without a clear primary citation attached to them in the reference literature [1] (reported). They may well be right. They are also the load-bearing premise of the entire replacement rationale, and that premise is less well sourced than its popularity suggests.

### Why bound copper behaves differently from loose copper [lab]
76. This is the most chemically solid part of the record and the part that gets least attention. Free copper ions are reactive in a way that damages tissue: they cycle between oxidation states and generate reactive species. A 1981 study in the Biochemical Journal (Lau and Sarkar) showed that when copper is held by this tripeptide, that redox activity is effectively silenced [1] (verified, in vitro).
77. That is the real argument for the complex over a copper salt. It is not that the peptide delivers more copper, it is that it delivers copper in a form that does not do damage on the way. The binding geometry has been determined independently by X-ray crystallography, electron paramagnetic resonance and nuclear magnetic resonance (Hureau et al., Chemistry, 2011) [1] (verified), so this is structural chemistry rather than inference.
78. The practical reading: the copper chemistry is well understood, and it is the best-established thing about this molecule. The biology built on top of it is considerably softer.

### What the cell studies measured, and the direction nobody quotes [cells]
79. The fibroblast work is real and reasonably old. Collagen synthesis in cultured skin fibroblasts was reported in FEBS Letters in 1988 (Maquart et al.); sulfated glycosaminoglycan synthesis in Life Sciences in 1992 (Wegrowski et al.); matrix metalloproteinase-2 expression in fibroblast cultures in Life Sciences in 2000 (Siméon et al.), with decorin reported by the same group [1] (reported, cells). These are the primary sources for almost every claim about collagen and matrix.
80. Here is the part that rarely survives the trip into marketing copy. The molecule stimulates both the matrix-degrading enzymes and their inhibitors. The reference literature draws the obvious conclusion from that and says it should therefore be used with caution [1] (verified). Simultaneously pushing breakdown and the brake on breakdown is coherent as a remodelling signal, where old matrix has to come out before new matrix goes in. It is also not the same thing as "builds collagen", and in a tissue that is not actively repairing, net remodelling is not obviously a benefit.
81. A note on how to read these: cell-culture concentrations in this literature are often in the nanomolar range, applied directly to fibroblasts in a dish. Nothing in that design speaks to what concentration reaches a fibroblast in living skin through any route.

### The gene-expression claim, traced to its source [cells]
82. The "resets thousands of genes toward repair" framing has one main scientific parent, and it is worth reading accurately because what it found is more interesting and less flattering than the summary.
83. A 2012 study in Genome Medicine profiled gene expression in lung tissue from eight regions each of six patients with severe emphysema plus two donor lungs, 64 regions in total, and matched the expression patterns to emphysema severity measured histologically. The resulting signature was then queried against a drug-expression database to find compounds whose effects ran opposite to it. This tripeptide came out of that screen [2] (verified).
84. Then they tested it. In distal lung fibroblasts from former smokers with emphysema, the peptide at 10 nanomolar did three things: it changed where integrin-beta-1 sat in the cell and reorganised actin into contractile filaments; it restored collagen gel contraction, which is impaired in these cells, back toward the level seen in former smokers without the disease; and under second-harmonic imaging the treated cells remodelled collagen into fibrils more effectively [2] (verified, human cells).
85. The detail that reframes everything: the paper reports that these effects resembled activation of the TGF-beta pathway, and that the peptide acted like TGF-beta-1 in these assays [2] (verified). TGF-beta is the central pro-fibrotic signal in the body. So the mechanism behind the lung finding is not a gentle rejuvenation programme, it is engagement of a fibrosis-adjacent pathway that happens to be useful when the problem is tissue that has lost its ability to contract and remodel. That is a genuinely promising finding for a specific repair deficit. It is a poor argument for continuous use in healthy tissue (inferred).

### The animal wound work [animals]
86. Wound healing has been reported across rats, rabbits, mice and pigs, much of it in veterinary journals: Veterinary Dermatology in 2006 and 2008 (Cangul et al.; Gul et al.), Veterinary Surgery in 2003 (Canapp et al.), and Life Sciences in 2007 in diabetic rats (Arul et al.) [1] (reported, animals).
87. The most quotable single number comes from a rat ischaemic skin flap model, where wound area fell 64.5% against 28.2% in controls [1] (reported). Ischaemic flaps and diabetic rats share a feature worth noticing: both are models of impaired healing. The pattern across this literature is that effects show up most clearly where repair is already failing, which is consistent with the lung finding above and inconsistent with a general-purpose enhancement story.

### The human record, including the trial that failed [people]
88. The published positive human evidence is topical and small: controlled facial studies reporting firming and reduced wrinkle depth, which the reference literature itself characterises as having relatively small sample sizes and short durations [1] (verified). That is a fair description and not a dismissal; small short trials are how cosmetic ingredients are normally assessed.
89. What is almost entirely missing from the popular account is that this molecule had a proper pivotal programme, and it did not succeed. Under the name Iamin Gel, a Washington-state company ran two Phase III trials in diabetic plantar foot ulcers, enrolling 511 patients across 30 medical centres, testing 0.5% and 2% formulations against placebo [3,4]. The result, announced in October, was that the drug was no better than placebo [4] (verified). The company's share price fell 68% on the day [4].
90. That matters for how the earlier positive ulcer trial should be read. A smaller randomised trial reporting faster closure, followed by a 511-patient multi-centre pivotal programme finding no difference against placebo, is the classic pattern of an early signal that did not replicate at scale. The pivotal result is the more informative of the two, because it was larger, controlled, multi-centre and designed to be decisive. No approved product followed.
91. For the injected route, there is still no human study of any kind, and no therapeutic trial registered. That has not changed, and it is the route with the thinnest evidence and the loudest claims.

### The route with no regulatory file [lab]
92. There is an asymmetry worth knowing when comparing this compound to its stack-mates. For BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV, the US agency published detailed assessments in 2026 covering identity, certificates of analysis, impurities, endotoxin and aggregate testing, toxicology and immunogenicity. For injectable copper tripeptide, no such document exists yet, because its advisory review was deferred to a later meeting [5,6] (verified).
93. So the honest statement is not that injected use has been assessed and cleared, nor that it has been assessed and faulted. It has not been assessed. The questions the agency asked of the other three, what exactly is in the vial, what the unidentified impurities are, whether aggregates are present, whether endotoxin is tested, whether an injected peptide-metal complex raises immunogenicity concerns, have not yet been asked in public of this one.
94. The additional variable here, absent from the other three, is the metal. An injected copper complex raises questions a plain peptide does not: how much copper is delivered, what happens to it after the peptide is degraded, and what repeated dosing does to copper handling. Nothing in the available record addresses this (verified as absent).

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
95. The sequence in 2026 was messier than the summaries suggest, and the detail matters because it changes what is permitted. On 22 April 2026, non-injectable copper tripeptide was removed from Category 1 as part of a bulk withdrawal action affecting twelve peptide substances, which happened because nominators withdrew rather than because of any safety finding. On 5 May 2026 the nominator clarified that it had intended to withdraw only the injectable-route nomination, and the topical form was restored to Category 1, where it sits under temporary compounding enforcement discretion [5] (verified).
96. The injectable route was left unclassified: not in Category 1, not in Category 2, not on the approved bulks list [5] (verified). Legal commentary on that gap advises against compounding it while it persists.
97. Injectable copper tripeptide is scheduled for review at a second advisory committee meeting expected before the end of February 2027, alongside four other peptides, with no date set as of mid-October 2026 [5,6] (reported). That meeting is the next real decision point.
98. Cosmetic topical use is a separate world and continues unaffected, since the ingredient is a long-established cosmetic additive rather than a drug in that context.

### Open questions [mixed]
99. Why did the pivotal ulcer programme fail where the smaller trial succeeded? Dose, formulation, wound selection and trial size are all candidates, and nobody has published a post-mortem. Until someone does, the honest reading is that topical healing benefit did not replicate at scale.
100. Is the TGF-beta-like action a feature or a warning? It explains the lung and wound results and it is also the pathway behind fibrosis. The answer probably depends entirely on whether the tissue is damaged or healthy, which is exactly the distinction continuous cosmetic use erases.
101. What does the plasma-decline figure actually rest on? The replacement rationale depends on it and its primary source is hard to pin down.
102. What happens to the copper after injection, and does repeated dosing shift copper handling? Unstudied, and unique to this compound among the common repair peptides.
103. Does simultaneous stimulation of matrix-degrading enzymes and their inhibitors net out positively in skin that is not injured? The reference literature's own caution suggests this is not settled.
104. Does injected use do anything a topical does not? With no human study of the injected route at all, this remains a claim rather than a finding, despite being the form most discussed.
