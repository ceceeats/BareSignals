# KPV
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/kpv
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: alpha-MSH (11-13), Lys-Pro-Val, tripeptide KPV, L-lysyl-L-prolyl-L-valine
2. Origin: Fragment of a natural protein
3. Sequence: KPV (3 amino acids)
4. Legal and approval status: US: not approved. Removed from FDA Category 2 in April 2026; the July 2026 advisory panel voted 8-6-1 for compounding, no rule yet. Australia, EU, UK: no approved product, sale needs a medicine licence. WADA: not named, falls under S0.

## Summary
5. KPV is the tripeptide Lys-Pro-Val, the last three amino acids of alpha-MSH, keeping its anti-inflammatory action without the tanning. It is studied for gut inflammation such as colitis and for inflamed skin. Evidence is animal and user-reported, with no human trial, but users rate gut benefits highest and report calmer colitis, eczema, psoriasis and rosacea, usually within 3 to 14 days.
6. Punch line: The anti-inflammatory tail of alpha-MSH, minus the tanning. Often blended with BPC-157 and GHK-Cu.

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Inflammatory signaling: Switches off inflammation signals
8. Tissue repair: Helps the gut lining close
9. Digestive & gut: Calms colitis in mice
10. Immunity: Steadies overreacting immune cells
11. Organ function (backed only by animal or user reports): Studied for skin and lung

## How it works
12. Calms inflammation without tanning · Alpha-MSH 11 to 13 · role Inflammatory signaling
   KPV is the last three amino acids (Lys-Pro-Val, positions 11 to 13) of alpha-MSH, a melanocortin hormone. It keeps much of alpha-MSH's anti-inflammatory action but not the skin-darkening effect. This is shown in cell studies and mouse colitis models; no human trial exists.
13. Gets into gut and immune cells · PepT1 transporter · role Digestive & gut
   KPV enters gut lining and immune cells through PepT1, a transporter that normally carries small peptides from digested food. PepT1 becomes more active in the inflamed colon, so uptake is highest where inflammation is. This is why oral use is common, though it is shown only in cells and mice.
14. Blocks a key inflammation switch inside cells · NF-κB down · role Inflammatory signaling
   Inside the cell, KPV blocks activation of NF-κB, the master switch for inflammatory genes. Output of inflammatory cytokines falls as a result. This is shown in human gut, immune and skin cell lines and in mice.
15. Works even without the tanning receptor · MC1R independent · role Immunity
   Its anti-inflammatory effect persists in mice lacking a working MC1R, the receptor alpha-MSH uses for pigment. So KPV acts inside the cell rather than through melanocortin signalling. This is what separates it from tanning peptides.
16. Eases colitis and helps colon lining heal, in mice · Colitis models · role Tissue repair
   In mouse models of colitis (DSS and TNBS), KPV eased inflammation and helped the colon lining heal, including as oral nanoparticles aimed at the colon. These are the core outcome data. No human trial exists or is registered.
17. Mechanism overview: KPV is the C-terminal tripeptide (Lys-Pro-Val) of alpha-MSH, positions 11 to 13. It carries much of alpha-MSH's anti-inflammatory action without the tanning effect. It enters gut epithelial and immune cells through the PepT1 transporter, which is upregulated in the inflamed colon. Inside the cell it blocks NF-κB activation, lowering inflammatory cytokine output. The effect persists without the MC1R receptor, so it does not depend on melanocortin signalling. In mouse colitis models this calms inflammation and helps the colon lining heal.

## What has been tested

48. Skin & aesthetics: Eczema, psoriasis, rosacea (users)

## Who shouldn't take it
53. People with IBD on biologics or immunosuppressants: KPV damps NF-kB signalling; stacking it onto immune drugs is untested. (inferred)
54. People fighting an active infection: Blunting inflammation can blunt the response clearing the infection. (inferred)

## Synergies (paired with)
55. BPC-157: KPV calms inflammation inside gut and immune cells, while BPC-157 protects and repairs the gut lining. People pair them for colitis and gut flares, and they are sold pre-mixed in KLOW and Tβ4 + BPC + KPV. The pair has not been tested together in people.
56. GHK-Cu: KPV damps inflammation through NF-κB, while GHK-Cu rebuilds collagen and remodels skin matrix. People pair them for eczema, rosacea and irritated skin, and they are sold pre-mixed as GHK-Cu + KPV and in KLOW. There is no combination study.
57. TB-500 frag 17–23: KPV calms inflammation, while the TB-500 fragment is used to help repair cells move into injured tissue. People combine them when an injury has a strong inflammatory side. The pair has not been tested together.
58. Thymosin Alpha-1: Thymosin Alpha-1 activates T-cells and antiviral defences, while KPV turns down inflammatory signalling. People pair them to support immune function while calming overactive inflammation. The pairing is user logic, not trial data.
59. LL-37: LL-37 kills bacteria and breaks up biofilms, while KPV calms the inflammation that infection drives. People pair them for gut and skin issues with a microbial side. LL-37 can itself drive skin inflammation, and the pair has not been tested together.
60. Larazotide: Larazotide tightens the seals between gut lining cells through the zonulin pathway, while KPV calms inflammation inside those cells. People pair them for leaky gut and celiac-type symptoms from two angles. The pairing is user logic, not trial data.
61. Thymosin Beta-4: Tβ4 moves repair cells into damaged tissue and grows new vessels, while KPV turns down NF-κB inflammation. They are sold pre-mixed in KLOW and Tβ4 + BPC + KPV for repair with less inflammation. The pair has not been tested together in people.

## Where it comes from
62. The tripeptide Lys-Pro-Val, the last three amino acids (residues 11-13) of alpha-MSH, the hormone that tans skin. KPV keeps the calming, anti-inflammatory part and leaves the tanning behind.
63. First studied for: Studied as the anti-inflammatory core of alpha-MSH, aimed mostly at the gut (colitis) and inflamed skin.
64. Name story: One-letter code for Lys-Pro-Val (lysine got K because L was taken by leucine). [S5] (verified)
65. 1980s to 1990s: Identified as the calming tail, residues 11 to 13, of alpha-MSH
66. 2000s: Colitis work in mice, including mice without MC1R
67. 2010s: Oral nanoparticles aimed at the colon
68. 2026: Removed from FDA Category 2; July panel voted 8 to 6 for compounding
69. Today: Gut and skin calming peptide, oral or injected

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### What was actually nominated, and for what [lab]
70. When a compounding pharmacy asked US regulators to allow this tripeptide onto the 503A list, the request was for a 0.1% cream or gel, applied topically, for wound healing and inflammatory skin conditions such as psoriasis and eczema [1] (verified). The nomination was later withdrawn, and the agency carried on evaluating both the free base and the acetate salt on its own initiative.
71. That detail is worth holding onto, because the official evidence review is about a skin cream. It is not about injection, not about oral capsules and not about the gut, even though the agency noted that online marketing promotes injectable, oral, topical and nasal forms for a long list of conditions including colitis and Crohn's disease [1] (verified). The gap between what was formally assessed and what circulates is wider here than for any other peptide in this group.
72. Both salts share a single CAS number in most public references, which sounds convenient and is actually a problem: it means the paperwork does not reliably distinguish them. The free base is 342.43 g/mol, the acetate 402.5 [1]. In the nomination the agency reviewed, the certificate of analysis was titled for the acetate while listing the free base's molecular formula [1] (verified). Like its stack-mates, KPV is a common name rather than an approved generic name, which the agency flagged as a safety risk in itself.

### The citation audit [lab]
73. This is the most clarifying single fact in the record, and it comes from the agency simply reading the sources it was given. The nomination cited nine references in support of effectiveness. Eight were animal studies of alpha-MSH derivatives. One was a study in human cadaver skin. None of the nine tested this tripeptide in a living human [1] (verified).
74. Reviewing the wider literature itself, the agency found no clinical evidence for either nominated indication, and noted that approved therapies exist for both [1]. It also found no published human studies of compounded products containing it, and no record of any outsourcing facility compounding it between January 2017 and June 2025 [1] (verified). So the claim of established practical use does not hold up either.
75. A fair summary: the evidence for this molecule is a well-constructed argument from a parent hormone's biology, supported by animal and cell work, with nothing human attached to it at any point.

### Mechanism, step by step, and where each step was shown [cells]
76. Step one, getting in. The proposed route into cells is the PepT1 transporter, a di- and tripeptide carrier. This was shown in human intestinal epithelial cell lines, specifically Caco2-BBE and HT29-CL.19A, and in Jurkat T cells [1,2] (verified, cells). The appeal of this mechanism is that the transporter is more active in inflamed intestine, so the molecule would concentrate where it is needed. That logic is sound and it is also why oral use became the common route.
77. Step two, what happens inside. The proposed actions are inhibition of NF-kappa-B activation and interference with interleukin-1-beta signalling [1] (verified as proposed). NF-kappa-B is the master switch for inflammatory gene expression, so blocking its activation is a coherent explanation for broad calming effects across tissues.
78. What is missing from this chain is any quantitative anchor. There is no measured binding affinity to a target, no demonstrated dose-response in a living animal tied to tissue concentration, and no pharmacokinetic data at all, in any species, by any route [1] (verified). The mechanism is a plausible route and a plausible destination with no measurements of the journey.

### The receptor question, answered more sharply than usual [cells]
79. The familiar framing is that this fragment keeps alpha-MSH's calming action without the tanning, and works without the tanning receptor. The agency's review puts it more strongly than that, and the stronger version is more interesting.
80. Tested directly, the tripeptide did not displace alpha-MSH from its binding site, and it did not raise cyclic AMP in macrophages expressing the MC1R receptor. The agency's conclusion was that melanocortin receptors are unlikely to be its targets at all [1] (verified, cells).
81. So this is not a selective melanocortin agonist that skips the pigment effect. It is a fragment of a hormone that appears to work through a different mechanism entirely, probably intracellular, reached by a peptide transporter rather than a surface receptor. That reframes the whole family resemblance: the alpha-MSH lineage explains where the sequence came from, not how it acts (inferred).
82. It also means the usual reassurance, that this is just a well-understood hormone's active core, does not hold. The hormone's receptor pharmacology does not transfer to it.

### The colitis work, in detail [animals]
83. The animal result that built this molecule's reputation is a 2008 study in Inflammatory Bowel Diseases, and it is genuinely striking. It used two independent colitis models: chemical colitis induced by 2.5% dextran sodium sulfate in drinking water for six days, and an immune-driven model created by transferring CD45RBhi T cells [3] (verified).
84. In the chemical model, treated mice recovered earlier and regained more body weight, with fewer inflammatory infiltrates and lower colonic myeloperoxidase activity, which is a direct measure of neutrophil infiltration rather than a subjective score. In the transfer model, treated mice recovered, regained weight and showed less histological inflammation [3].
85. The decisive arm used mice with a non-functional MC1R receptor. In those animals, treatment rescued every treated mouse from death during chemical colitis [3] (verified). That is the experiment behind the receptor-independence claim, and it is a strong design: if the effect survives removal of the proposed receptor, the receptor is not required.
86. Two limits on reading it. The published abstract does not state the dose, schedule or route, so the study cannot be used to derive any dosing logic [3]. And both models are acute chemical or immune injury in mice, which is the standard proxy for inflammatory bowel disease and has a long history of producing results that did not carry into human trials (verified).

### Human data, and what that means for dose [people]
87. There are no human studies of this tripeptide, for any indication, by any route. The agency found no human products, no clinical studies and no human pharmacokinetic data [1] (verified). Its adverse event database and its complaint system together returned no reports through 3 December 2025 [1].
88. That absence of adverse event reports is sometimes read as evidence of safety. It is better read as evidence of absent surveillance: an unapproved substance bought as a research chemical and self-administered does not generate the reports that an approved drug does, and nobody is collecting them.
89. There is no established human dose, and no study from which one could be derived. Every number in circulation originates from community convention or from converting animal doses, and the underlying animal studies frequently do not publish the doses they used in retrievable form. This is a case where the usual practice of citing "reported" ranges has nothing underneath it at the research end.
90. One route-specific finding deserves attention, because it cuts against the nominated form. The tripeptide did not permeate human cadaver skin well. Penetration increased in vitro when iontophoresis or microneedle abrasion was applied [1] (verified). So the one form that was formally nominated, a plain topical cream, faces a delivery problem that the nomination's own supporting science identified.

### Chemistry and product reality [lab]
91. For the free base, no certificate of analysis accompanied the nomination. Publicly available certificates report only purity, with no impurity limits, no aggregate testing and no microbial testing [1]. For the acetate, the certificate reported total impurities of 0.9% against a 2.0% limit and a largest single impurity of 0.24% against a 1.0% limit, which is good, but gave no impurity identities, no aggregate data and no microbial testing [1] (verified).
92. The agency concluded that neither form is well characterised, and that there is insufficient data to conclude that immunogenicity and aggregation risks are absent [1] (verified).
93. Two chemistry details with practical consequences. The salts differ sharply in solubility: the free base dissolves to about 0.70 mg per millilitre in water, the acetate to about 5 mg per millilitre [1]. A product made with one and handled as though it were the other will not behave as expected. And storage differs: the free base is reported stable for up to three years at minus 20 degrees Celsius, while the acetate's stated storage is ordinary refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees [1].
94. Under forced degradation the acetate breaks down to a cyclised product, Lys-Pro diketopiperazine [1] (verified). Cyclisation of a proline-containing peptide is a known and predictable degradation route, and it means a degraded vial does not simply contain less of the peptide, it contains something else.

### Safety: unexamined rather than reassuring [lab]
95. No acute toxicity studies, no repeat-dose studies, no genotoxicity studies, no developmental or reproductive studies and no carcinogenicity studies were submitted or identified for either form [1] (verified). No pharmacokinetic or toxicokinetic studies exist either.
96. So the standard questions have not been asked. What dose causes harm, which organ is affected first, what happens over months, whether it affects DNA or pregnancy: all unexamined.
97. The reported pattern of early reactions in community use, headaches and flu-like feelings sometimes at low exposures, with people who have mast-cell problems reacting at the smallest amounts, has no counterpart anywhere in the formal record, and no mechanism has been investigated for it (reported). Given a proposed mechanism that acts on inflammatory signalling and an entry route through a transporter that immune cells express, an immune-mediated reaction is not implausible, but nothing has tested it (speculated).
98. The agency's overall position was that there is no human safety data and the risks are unknown [1]. That is a precise statement and worth preferring over any confident claim in either direction.

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
99. The agency's own staff proposed not adding either form to the 503A bulks list, for four stated reasons: neither substance is well characterised, the extent of compounding use is unknown, there is no evidence of effectiveness in humans, and there is no human safety data with risks unknown [1] (verified).
100. The advisory committee recommended adding it anyway, voting 8 to 6 with one abstention on 23 July 2026, for wound healing and inflammatory conditions [2,4]. Staff had found that the evidence weighed against it, noting that none of the studies they located involved humans [5].
101. As with the others, that recommendation is advisory and alters nothing by itself. Addition requires formal notice-and-comment rulemaking, which has not started and has no deadline [4] (verified). An "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" went to the White House budget office on 14 October 2026 and was unpublished as of mid-October, with its covered substances and conditions not public [4]. Removal from the do-not-compound category in April 2026 happened because nominations were withdrawn, not because of a revised safety judgement, and left the substance outside any recognised category [6] (verified).
102. In sport, it is not named on the prohibited list, which places it under the catch-all class for non-approved substances rather than outside the rules.

### Open questions [mixed]
103. If melanocortin receptors are not the target, what is? Without an identified target there is no basis for predicting selectivity, no structure-activity work and no rational dose.
104. Does anything reach the intended tissue? With zero pharmacokinetic data in any species and poor permeation through human skin, the delivery question is unanswered for every route in use.
105. Do the mouse colitis results mean anything for human inflammatory bowel disease? This model has a long record of promising results that did not translate, and no human trial has ever been attempted here, let alone registered.
106. What explains the early reactions people report at very low exposures? Nothing in the formal record addresses this, and it is the most common practical complaint.
107. Which salt is in a given product, and was it handled accordingly? Shared CAS numbers, a seven-fold solubility difference and different storage requirements make this genuinely ambiguous.
108. Everything about long-term safety. There is no toxicology of any kind, which makes this unopened rather than open.
