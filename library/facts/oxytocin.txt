# Oxytocin
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/oxytocin
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: Pitocin, Syntocinon, Induxin
2. Origin: Made by the body
3. Sequence: CYIQNCPLG (9 amino acids). Ring closed by a bridge between the two C; amide end
4. Shown with an FDA approved badge
5. Legal and approval status: FDA-approved (Pitocin) for obstetrics. Intranasal/social use is off-label/compounded.

## Summary
6. Oxytocin is a nine-amino-acid hormone made by the body and released from the posterior pituitary, driving labour, milk letdown and social bonding. Its proven, FDA-approved use is obstetric, as Pitocin for inducing labour and controlling bleeding. Users take it nasally or sublingually for mood and closeness, but intranasal trials in autism and social anxiety are largely mixed or negative.
7. Punch line: The bonding hormone. Proven for labour and milk letdown; taken nasally for closeness, with variable effects.
8. Used for: Mood, closeness and libido

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
9. Connection & desire: Closeness and bonding

11. Endocrine & growth: Pituitary hormone
12. Mood: Calm and trust studied
13. Tissue repair (backed only by animal or user reports): Wound healing studied

## How it works
14. Contracts the uterus during labour · Myometrial OXTR, Gq/Ca2+ · role Reproductive
   Oxytocin binds OXTR on uterine muscle, a Gq-coupled receptor that raises calcium inside the cell and makes it contract. This is its proven, long-approved use, given by injection in hospital to start labour and control bleeding after birth.
15. Lets milk down during breastfeeding · Myoepithelial OXTR · role Reproductive
   In the breast, oxytocin binds OXTR on myoepithelial cells that wrap the milk glands and squeezes them, pushing milk out. A licensed nasal spray once existed for this let-down reflex but was discontinued for commercial reasons.
16. Released from the pituitary as a hormone · Posterior pituitary · role Endocrine & growth
   Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released into blood from the posterior pituitary in response to birth, suckling and touch. Released this way it acts on distant tissues. This is textbook endocrine physiology.
17. Shapes bonding and social behaviour · Central OXTR, amygdala · role Mood
   Inside the brain, OXTR in the amygdala and reward circuits shapes trust, bonding and how much attention social cues get. Animal work is strong. Intranasal trials in autism and social anxiety are largely mixed or negative, and how much nasal oxytocin reaches the brain is debated.
18. Surges at orgasm, adds to closeness · Hypothalamic OT neurons · role Connection & desire
   Hypothalamic oxytocin neurons fire around orgasm, and blood oxytocin rises at that moment in people. This is thought to add to feelings of closeness afterwards. Whether taking it nasally boosts libido is not established.
19. Speeds wound healing in animal studies · Anti-inflammatory OXTR · role Tissue repair
   Acting through OXTR on immune and skin cells, oxytocin dampens inflammation. In animal studies this speeds wound healing. There is no human evidence for this use.
20. Mechanism overview: Oxytocin is a nine-amino-acid hormone made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary. It binds OXTR, a Gq-coupled receptor that raises intracellular calcium. In the uterus that contracts smooth muscle during labour. In the breast it squeezes myoepithelial cells to let milk down. Inside the brain, OXTR in the amygdala and reward circuits shapes trust, bonding and social salience, and oxytocin neurons fire at orgasm, adding to closeness. Animal work also shows anti-inflammatory effects that speed wound healing.

## Who shouldn't take it
44. People prone to low sodium (diuretics, SSRIs, marathoners): Oxytocin acts like antidiuretic hormone and can cause water intoxication. (verified)
45. People with borderline personality or attachment anxiety: Intranasal oxytocin reduced trust and cooperation in some of these people. (reported)
46. Anyone who could be pregnant: It contracts the uterus; that is its approved use. (verified)

## Where it comes from
47. The nine-amino-acid 'bonding' hormone of the posterior pituitary, driving uterine contraction, milk letdown, and social attachment.
48. First studied for: Inducing labor and controlling postpartum bleeding (Pitocin).
49. Name story: From Greek oxys (swift) + tokos (childbirth): "quick birth", after Henry Dale found in 1906 that it contracts the uterus. In 1953 du Vigneaud made it the first peptide hormone ever synthesized, winning the 1955 Nobel. [S6] (verified)
50. 1953: First peptide hormone sequenced and synthesized
51. 1950s: Approved as Pitocin for labor and postpartum bleeding
52. 2000s to 2010s: Intranasal trials in autism, social anxiety and bonding
53. Today: An obstetric staple; social use is off-label

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### Two drugs with one name [people]
54. Oxytocin as an approved medicine is an injection given in hospital to start or strengthen labour and control bleeding after birth. Oxytocin as people use it socially is a nasal spray taken for closeness, calm or sex. They share a molecule and almost nothing else: different route, different dose logic, different evidence and a different regulatory status (inferred from the sources below).
55. The licensed injection is standardised in units, not milligrams: 10 USP units per millilitre, with 0.5% chlorobutanol as preservative, buffered to pH 3.5, for intravenous infusion or intramuscular injection [1] (verified). A unit is a measure of biological activity, so units and micrograms do not convert cleanly from one product to another (inferred).

### What happened to the nasal spray [people]
56. A licensed nasal oxytocin did exist in the US. Syntocinon Nasal Spray was approved in 1960 to help milk let-down after birth, and was discontinued in 1997 for commercial reasons [6] (verified). A 2013 licence deal aimed to bring it back for lactation and to trial it in schizophrenia and autism [6] (verified); no relaunch is evident in the current record (inferred). So nasal oxytocin bought in the US today is, in practice, a compounded or imported product, not a licensed one (inferred).

### What the body does with it [mixed]
57. In blood, oxytocin lasts minutes: a plasma half-life of about 1 to 6 minutes, shorter in late pregnancy, cleared by kidney and liver [1] (verified). It also has a built-in antidiuretic effect; with long infusions this has caused water intoxication with seizures, coma and maternal death [1] (verified). That risk belongs to high-dose infusion, not a nasal puff, but it shows the hormone is not only about bonding.
58. The central question for nasal use is whether it reaches the brain. In monkeys, labelled oxytocin given nasally, not intravenously, appeared two hours later in brain regions along the olfactory and trigeminal nerve routes, including the striatum and orbitofrontal cortex [4] (verified). In people, the evidence is thinner: in measured studies, the share of a nasal dose recovered in cerebrospinal fluid is on the order of 0.001% [5] (reported). Some does get in. How much, and whether it is enough to matter, is still argued (inferred).

### The human record on trust and bonding [people]
59. The idea of oxytocin as a trust hormone rests largely on a 2005 study in which 58 investors given nasal oxytocin trusted more in an economic game, at a one-tailed p of 0.029, with no effect on the other side of the exchange or on risk-taking [2] (verified).
60. A 2015 critical review found six conceptual replications, none of which reliably reproduced it; one found the reverse in women. Pooling seven studies and 481 people gave an effect size of 0.077, not significant, and that was with inclusion rules chosen to favour the hypothesis [2] (verified). Detecting even the upper plausible effect would need more than 400 participants, about the total of every trust study combined at the time [2].
61. The newer reading is that oxytocin raises the salience of social cues, good and bad, rather than making people more trusting or warmer across the board [8] (reported). That fits why effects vary so much by person and context (inferred).

### The largest clinical test [people]
62. In autism, a 24-week trial randomised 290 children and adolescents aged 3 to 17 to nasal oxytocin, targeting 48 IU a day, or placebo. Social withdrawal scores and cognitive measures did not differ between groups, and adverse events were similar [3] (verified). It is the largest trial of nasal oxytocin in autism, and it was clearly negative.
63. Sexual and intimacy uses have no comparably sized trial in the record reviewed for this entry (verified as absent in the sources reviewed). There is no established dose for social or sexual use.

### Safety, and what has not been studied [people]
64. The injection label's adverse effects are obstetric and serious: uterine hyperstimulation and rupture, arrhythmia, hypertensive episodes, anaphylaxis and fetal distress [1] (verified). The label also states it is not indicated for elective induction of labour, because benefit against risk was never established [1] (verified).
65. For nasal social use, the large autism trial found adverse events no different from placebo over 24 weeks [3]. Long-term daily nasal use in adults, and use in pregnancy outside obstetric care, are unstudied (verified as absent in the sources reviewed). Because it acts on the uterus, pregnancy is the obvious line not to cross (inferred).

### Who produced the evidence [people]
66. The early social-neuroscience literature was built on small samples and flexible analyses, and later work points to publication bias in the field [2,8] (reported). The autism trial was the first test at a size able to give a clear answer [3]. The pattern, strong small early results followed by weak large ones, is the same one seen with many compounds here (inferred).

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
67. US: approved as an injection for obstetric use; nasal and social use is off-label, typically through compounding [1,6] (verified for the approval; inferred for current supply). Sport: oxytocin is not named on the 2026 Prohibited List and, as an approved medicine, does not fall in the catch-all non-approved class [7] (verified for the list contents; inferred for its application).

### Open questions [mixed]
68. How much of a nasal dose reaches the human brain, and does it act there or through the body? Still unresolved.
69. Is there a group that reliably benefits socially? The autism trial says not in that population; others are untested at scale.
70. What does daily use for months do? No long-term adult data exist.
