# HCG
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/hcg
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: human chorionic gonadotropin, hCG, chorionic gonadotropin, Pregnyl, Novarel, Ovidrel (recombinant), choriogonadotropin alfa, Ovitrelle, Profasi, beta-hCG
2. Origin: Made by the body: placental glycoprotein hormone
3. Shown with an FDA approved badge
4. Legal and approval status: FDA-approved (Novarel, Pregnyl, generic; recombinant Ovidrel); regulated as a biologic since 23 Mar 2020, which cut off most compounding. Reported on the ASHP shortage list into 2026. WADA 2026 S2.2.1: prohibited in males.

## Summary
5. HCG is the pregnancy hormone made by the placenta, which acts on the same receptor as LH. In men it tells the testes to make testosterone and sperm, and it is approved for fertility, undescended testes and low-gonadotropin hypogonadism. Men on testosterone use it to keep testes and fertility intact. The HCG diet has no support, since weight loss came from the calorie cut.
6. Punch line: The pregnancy hormone that mimics LH. Keeps testes making testosterone and sperm while TRT shuts LH off.
7. Used for: TRT fertility and testicle support, male hypogonadism, ovulation trigger

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
8. Reproductive: Keeps sperm production going
9. Endocrine & growth: Drives testosterone via LH receptor
10. Connection & desire: Libido kept up on TRT
11. Muscle & performance: Bridges recovery after a cycle
12. Fat metabolism (backed only by animal or user reports): HCG diet: no proven effect
13. Mood (backed only by animal or user reports): Estrogen-linked mood swings reported
14. Skin & aesthetics (backed only by animal or user reports): Acne reported

## How it works
15. Mimics LH on the testes · LH/choriogonadotropin receptor (LHCGR) · role Endocrine & growth
   hCG binds the LH/choriogonadotropin receptor, LHCGR, the same receptor LH uses on cells of the testes. It acts like LH but lasts much longer because of its beta subunit. This is established clinical pharmacology with decades of use.
16. Makes Leydig cells produce testosterone · Leydig cell steroidogenesis · role Endocrine & growth
   In Leydig cells, LHCGR activation switches on steroidogenesis, the chain of enzymes that turns cholesterol into testosterone. This keeps the testes producing even when the brain's own LH signal is low. It is the basis of its use in male hypogonadism.
17. Keeps testosterone high inside the testes for sperm · Intratesticular testosterone · role Reproductive
   Sperm production needs testosterone levels inside the testes far above blood levels. On testosterone therapy alone that local level fell by 94% in a dose-finding study, and adding hCG kept it near baseline. A small retrospective series kept sperm production at a year, though hCG does not supply FSH.
18. Triggers ovulation in women · LH surge mimic · role Reproductive
   In women hCG mimics the mid-cycle LH surge that releases a mature egg from the ovary. It also supports the corpus luteum, which makes progesterone afterward. This is a long-established fertility use.
19. Some testosterone converts to estrogen · Aromatase, estradiol rise · role Mood
   Aromatase, an enzyme in fat and other tissues, converts part of the extra testosterone into estradiol. At higher doses serum testosterone rose above normal in the dose-finding study, which gives more to convert. This fits the acne, water retention and estrogen symptoms users report.
20. Mechanism overview: hCG is a heterodimeric glycoprotein: an alpha subunit shared with LH, FSH and TSH and a beta subunit that gives it its identity and a long half-life. It binds the LH/choriogonadotropin receptor (LHCGR), a G-protein-coupled receptor on testicular Leydig cells and ovarian theca and luteal cells. In men this switches on steroidogenesis, so the testes make testosterone and keep intratesticular testosterone high, which spermatogenesis needs; it does not supply FSH. In women it mimics the mid-cycle LH surge and supports the corpus luteum. Part of the extra testosterone is aromatised to estradiol.

## Dosage (community reported unless tagged)
21. Typical: 500 IU (TRT); 500–4,000 IU (label) · every other day to 3×/week · cycle Ongoing with TRT; label courses weeks to months · route Sub-Q or IM (verified)
22. Half-life: ~36 h
23. Most people take: TRT add-on 500 IU SC/IM every other day (or ~1,500 IU/week for size only). Label for male hypogonadism 500–1,000 IU 3×/wk, or 4,000 IU 3×/wk for 6–9 months. Ovulation trigger 5,000–10,000 IU once. Pre-PCT blasts of higher doses are community practice.

## What people report
24. Used with TRT to keep testicles full and fertility intact, and as a pre-PCT 'blast'. Users report fuller testes and libido, plus acne, headaches, water retention and estrogen-driven nipple sensitivity.
25. Liked / worked: Keeps intratesticular testosterone up on TRT
26. Liked / worked: No azoospermia at 1 year in a 26-man TRT series
27. Liked / worked: Restores fertility in low-LH/FSH men
28. Liked / worked: Reliable ovulation trigger
29. What worked: 500 IU every other day kept intratesticular testosterone up and, in one retrospective series of 26 men on TRT, none became azoospermic at a year. Restores sperm production in men with low LH/FSH, sometimes with FSH added.
30. Went wrong / did not work: No weight loss beyond the 500 kcal diet
31. Went wrong / did not work: Raises estradiol; gynecomastia risk
32. Went wrong / did not work: Sperm fell in one high-dose study
33. Went wrong / did not work: OHSS and clots in women (label)
34. What went wrong: Label states it has not been shown to help obesity; the HCG diet's weight loss comes from the ~500 kcal/day diet. High doses can raise estradiol and one small study saw sperm counts fall despite hCG.
35. Most reported: Fuller testes and better libido on TRT
36. Most reported: Acne and headaches most reported
37. Most reported: Water retention, nipple sensitivity
38. Most reported: Hard to source since 2020

## What has been tested
39. Reproductive: Sperm preservation on TRT, retrospective (human)
40. Endocrine & growth: Dose-response of intratesticular testosterone (human)
41. Reproductive: Ovulation induction and male hypogonadism (human)
42. Fat metabolism: HCG diet trials: no effect beyond calorie cut (human)
43. Decades of clinical use in fertility; small and mostly retrospective studies of low-dose hCG alongside testosterone therapy.
44. What it helps: Clinically it treats male hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, prepubertal cryptorchidism and triggers ovulation. For men on testosterone, a dose-finding study showed 500 IU every other day raised intratesticular testosterone, and a 26-man retrospective series kept sperm production at a year. People use it to stop testicular shrinkage, protect fertility, and restart the axis after steroid cycles. The HCG diet has no support: the label and the FDA say the weight loss is from the calorie cut. Acne, headache, water retention and estrogen symptoms are the common complaints.
45. Trial: active · Not applicable · IVF egg maturation and stimulation · NCT07771283. Approved for decades; IVF trials of hCG triggers and low dose stimulation are still enrolling, e.g. in Ukraine and Uganda. (checked 2026-10-23) [S1] (reported)

## Who shouldn't take it
46. People with testicular cancer history: hCG is a tumour marker; injecting it blinds follow-up bloodwork. (inferred)
47. People using home pregnancy tests: False positives for days after a dose. (verified)
48. Hormone-sensitive cancer (breast, prostate): It makes the testes or ovaries produce more testosterone and estrogen. (verified)
49. Drug-tested athletes (men): hCG is banned for male athletes because it restarts testosterone production. (verified)

## Synergies (paired with)
50. Gonadorelin: Gonadorelin pulses the pituitary to release its own LH and FSH, while hCG stands in for LH directly at the testes. Men on TRT use one or the other, or both, to keep testes working, but the pair has not been tested together.
51. Kisspeptin-10: Kisspeptin-10 works at the top of the axis, telling the brain to release GnRH, and hCG acts at the testes. People use them together when restarting after steroids, but the pairing is user logic, not trial data.
52. HMG: hCG supplies only LH-like signalling, and HMG adds FSH, which Sertoli cells need for sperm production. Clinicians add FSH or HMG to hCG when sperm does not return on hCG alone, supported by small studies in men.

## Where it comes from
53. The pregnancy hormone made by the placenta; it acts on the same receptor as LH, so in men it tells the testes to make testosterone and sperm. Medical hCG has long been purified from pregnant women's urine; a recombinant version also exists.
54. First studied for: Fertility (ovulation trigger), undescended testes in boys and low-gonadotropin hypogonadism in men.
55. Name story: "Chorionic" comes from Greek khorion, the membrane wrapping the fetus (the afterbirth), because placental tissue makes it. In 1928 it became the basis of the first reliable pregnancy test, run on mice; rabbits followed, hence "the rabbit died". [S2] (verified)
56. 1927: Aschheim and Zondek use it as the first pregnancy test
57. 1954: Simeons pitches hCG plus 500 kcal/day for weight loss
58. 2011: FDA and FTC warn sellers of OTC 'homeopathic' HCG diet drops
59. 2013: TRT plus 500 IU every other day preserves sperm in a 26-man series
60. Mar 2020: Reclassified as a biologic; compounding largely cut off, shortages follow
61. Today: Standard TRT add-on; supply still patchy (reported 2026)

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### The study behind the fertility-preservation use, in detail [people]
62. The practice of adding this hormone to testosterone therapy rests mainly on one small, well-designed study, and its design is unusually direct: it measured hormone concentrations inside the testis rather than in blood.
63. Twenty-nine healthy men aged 18 to 45 all received testosterone enanthate 200 mg weekly, plus one of saline placebo or one of three doses given subcutaneously every other day, for three weeks [1] (verified). Testicular fluid was obtained by fine-needle aspiration of both testes at baseline and on day 21.
64. The placebo arm shows why this matters. Testosterone therapy alone suppressed intratesticular testosterone to about 6% of baseline, a 94% fall [1] (verified). That is the mechanism of testosterone-induced infertility stated numerically, and it is far more dramatic than most discussion conveys.
65. Against that, the three doses preserved intratesticular testosterone at roughly 75%, 93% and 126% of baseline, with a linear relationship between dose and concentration, and none of the three differing significantly from baseline [1] (verified). Pituitary hormones were suppressed to about 5% and 3% of baseline across the study, as expected on exogenous testosterone.
66. The honest limitation is the authors' own: the intratesticular testosterone threshold needed to maintain sperm production is unknown [1] (verified). The study establishes that this hormone preserves the local concentration in a dose-dependent way. It does not establish how much is enough, because nobody knows what the target is.

### What the dose-response actually implies, including a caution [people]
67. The linear dose-response is the useful finding, and it cuts in two directions.
68. At the lowest dose, serum testosterone was similar to placebo, meaning the local effect was achieved without adding appreciably to circulating levels. At the two higher doses, serum testosterone rose above the normal reference range [1] (verified). The top dose also pushed intratesticular testosterone 26% above its own baseline.
69. So more is not simply better. Higher amounts overshoot the local target and add systemic testosterone on top of a replacement dose, which is the plausible route to the oestrogen-related complaints people report, since part of any extra testosterone is converted to oestradiol (inferred, consistent with the measured serum rise).
70. This is the reverse of the intuition that usually governs how this hormone is used alongside testosterone, where the aim is often framed as keeping the testes as stimulated as possible. The data suggest a target range rather than a maximum, and that the lowest effective amount is the one that leaves circulating levels unchanged (inferred).
71. A three-week study in 29 healthy men cannot settle long-term practice, and the supporting evidence beyond it is small and mostly retrospective (verified, as the card records).

### Why this one left the compounding world, and what that did to supply [people]
72. The regulatory history here is different from every other compound in this catalog, and it explains the supply problems better than any market story.
73. On 23 March 2020, a set of products approved as drugs transitioned to licensure as biologics under a statutory provision that deemed their existing approvals to be biologics licences. This hormone was one of four bulk substances that outsourcing facilities had been using and that were affected, alongside hyaluronidase, follicle stimulating hormone and menotropins [2] (verified).
74. The consequence is categorical rather than discretionary: once a product is licensed as a biologic, it is no longer eligible for the compounding exemptions that pharmacies rely on [2] (verified). This was not a safety finding about the hormone. It was a reclassification that removed an entire supply channel overnight.
75. What remains is narrower. A guidance on mixing, diluting or repackaging licensed biological products outside an approved label still applies, under conditions that guidance sets out [2]. That is a different and more limited activity than compounding from bulk substance.
76. The practical result, reported into 2026, is that approved products exist but supply has been patchy, with the hormone appearing on pharmacy shortage listings (reported, as the card records). The useful lesson for reading this catalog: a compound can become hard to obtain for reasons that have nothing to do with its evidence or its safety, and conversely, availability says nothing about either.
77. Two forms exist, one purified from urine and one made recombinantly, which is relevant because they have different manufacturing supply chains and are not affected identically by any single disruption (inferred).

### The weight-loss claim, and why it is the clearest refuted claim in this catalog [people]
78. Most compounds here suffer from absent evidence. This one has the rarer distinction of a claim that was actually tested and rejected, repeatedly, over decades.
79. The weight-loss protocol dates to a 1954 proposal pairing the hormone with a roughly 500 calorie daily diet (verified, as the card's timeline records). The approved labelling states that it has not been shown to be effective adjunctive therapy for obesity, and the explanation for any weight lost is the calorie restriction, not the injection (verified).
80. Regulators and consumer-protection authorities acted against sellers of over-the-counter homeopathic versions of these drops in 2011 (verified, as the card records).
81. The distinction worth preserving: a 500 calorie daily diet produces weight loss in anyone, which is exactly why the protocol appeared to work and why it persisted. Attributing that loss to the injection is the error, and it is an error that was identified, tested and formally rejected rather than merely doubted (verified).
82. This matters as a reference case. When people ask why a given peptide's enthusiastic reports should not settle the question, this is the cleanest available answer: a protocol with decades of satisfied users, a plausible hormonal story, and a controlled answer that the hormone contributed nothing.

### Open questions [mixed]
83. What intratesticular testosterone concentration is actually needed to maintain sperm production? The dose-finding study's authors named this as the central unknown, and it is still unresolved. Without it, dose selection for fertility preservation is empirical.
84. Does preserved local testosterone translate into preserved fertility over years, not months? The supporting evidence is a small retrospective series, and the mechanism is a surrogate for the outcome people care about.
85. Is the lowest dose the right one? It achieved the local effect without raising serum testosterone, which on the available data looks preferable to the higher amounts, but no trial has compared outcomes across doses.
86. What is the long-term oestradiol consequence of adding this to a full replacement dose? Mechanistically predictable, poorly quantified.
87. Does the urine-derived form differ from the recombinant one in practice? They have different manufacturing routes and the comparison is rarely addressed.
88. Will supply stabilise? The constraint is regulatory classification rather than demand or evidence, so it will resolve on a regulatory timeline or not at all.
89. For men using it to restart the axis after suppression: what is the actual recovery curve, and does early use change it? Widely practised, thinly studied.
