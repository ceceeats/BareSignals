# Humanin
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/humanin
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: HN, Alzheimer's survival peptide, MT-RNR2-encoded peptide
2. Origin: Made by the body
3. Sequence: MAPRGFSCLLLLTSEIDLPVKRRA (24 amino acids)
4. Legal and approval status: Research chemical; no clinical program in humans.

## Summary
5. Humanin is a small natural peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA. It is studied for protecting neurons and cells from stress, and for better insulin response and healthy aging, partly by blocking cell-death signals. Evidence is cell and animal work, and no human efficacy trials exist.
6. Punch line: A peptide coded in mitochondrial DNA. Shields neurons and cells from stress; higher in long-lived families.
7. Used for: Cell protection and metabolic aging

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
8. Cellular protection: Protects cells from stress
9. Neural protection: Shields neurons from amyloid
10. Cellular energy: Made by mitochondria
11. Aging & senescence: Higher in long-lived families
12. Glucose & insulin (backed only by animal or user reports): Better insulin response in animals
13. Organ function (backed only by animal or user reports): Organ protection studied
14. Reproductive (backed only by animal or user reports): Fertility studied

## How it works
15. Blocks cell-death signals · Bax binding · role Cellular protection
   Inside cells, humanin binds Bax, a protein that triggers apoptosis, and stops it moving to the mitochondria to punch holes there. It also binds Bid and tBid, which help Bax assemble. Blocking this cascade keeps stressed cells alive, shown in cell studies.
16. Protects neurons from amyloid · FPRL1, gp130 receptors · role Neural protection
   Outside cells, humanin acts on the receptor FPRL1 and on a receptor complex containing gp130 that switches on JAK2 and STAT3. In cell studies this protects neurons from amyloid toxicity, and it helped in Alzheimer's mouse models. Protection persisted without FPRL1, so the receptor story is incomplete.
17. Supports mitochondrial function · Mitochondria-derived peptide · role Cellular energy
   Humanin's reading frame sits in the mitochondrial ribosomal RNA gene, making it a mitochondria-derived peptide, though nuclear copies exist and the true source is unsettled. It is linked to lower oxidative stress in cell and animal work. No effect in people has been tested.
18. Improves insulin sensitivity in rodents · Hypothalamic STAT3 · role Glucose & insulin
   In the hypothalamus, humanin signals through STAT3 and improves insulin sensitivity in the body. This was shown in rats with the peptide infused directly into the brain. Much of the animal work used a far stronger analogue, HNG, rather than natural humanin.
19. Levels fall with age, higher in long-lived · Age-linked decline · role Aging & senescence
   Blood levels of humanin fall with age in humans and mice, and children of centenarians had higher levels than controls in a small cohort. That links it to healthy aging. It is association only, and no one has been given humanin in a published human study.
20. Mechanism overview: Humanin is a 21 to 24 amino acid peptide encoded inside mitochondrial DNA. Inside cells it binds Bax and stops it moving to mitochondria, blocking the apoptosis cascade. Outside cells it acts through FPRL1 and a gp130-containing receptor complex, which protects neurons from amyloid toxicity. In the hypothalamus it signals through STAT3, improving insulin sensitivity in rodents. Blood levels fall with age and run higher in long-lived people.

## Synergies (paired with)
44. MOTS-c: Both are peptides encoded in mitochondrial DNA. Humanin guards cells against death signals, while MOTS-c activates AMPK and mimics exercise on metabolism, so people pair them for metabolic aging. The pair has not been tested together, and humanin has no human data at all.
45. SS-31: SS-31 binds cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane to steady energy production and cut oxidative stress, while humanin blocks cell-death signals at the mitochondria. People pair them for mitochondrial protection from two angles. The pairing is user logic, not trial data.

## Where it comes from
46. Found in 2001 in the one brain region that survived in an Alzheimer's patient, and encoded in the mitochondria's own DNA. Named for its hoped-for protective power.

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### Where it came from, and the part of its address nobody has settled [lab]
52. It was found in 2001 by a cell-survival screen: material from the unaffected occipital cortex of a person who had died with sporadic Alzheimer's disease was tested for genes that kept neurons alive against Alzheimer's-type insults, and one short reading frame kept surviving [1] (verified, as described in a later review). That reading frame sits inside the gene for the large ribosomal RNA of mitochondria, a stretch of DNA not expected to code for a protein at all.
53. That location creates a real puzzle. Mitochondria and the rest of the cell read slightly different genetic codes, so the same sequence gives a 21-residue peptide if translated inside mitochondria and a 24-residue one if translated in the cytoplasm. Both forms are active and the last three residues appear not to matter [1] (verified, in cells). The nuclear genome also carries several near-copies of the same sequence, remnants of mitochondrial DNA that migrated into the chromosomes long ago. Which source actually produces the peptide measured in blood, and from which tissue, has not been resolved [1] (verified as open).
54. So the familiar line, a peptide encoded by mitochondrial DNA, is the leading hypothesis rather than a settled fact. It also means two different peptide lengths share one name, and listings rarely say which is present (inferred).

### One small peptide, five reported partners [cells]
55. The card names Bax and two receptor systems. The fuller picture is that humanin has been reported to bind at least five different things, which is unusual for a peptide this size and worth sitting with.
56. Inside the cell it binds inactive Bax and prevents the shape change that sends Bax to the mitochondria; it also binds Bid and its cleaved form tBid, which normally help Bax assemble into pores; and it binds IGFBP-3, a growth-factor binding protein that can itself trigger cell death [1] (verified, in cells). Outside the cell it acts on the formyl peptide receptor FPRL1, and on a three-part cytokine-type receptor assembled from CNTFR, WSX-1 and gp130 that switches on JAK2 and STAT3 [1] (verified, in cells).
57. One detail undercuts the tidy version of this story: neuroprotection persisted when FPRL1 was knocked down, so the receptor account is incomplete [1] (verified). Many binding partners can mean a genuinely central signal, or a sticky peptide that registers activity in whatever assay it is dropped into. The literature has not separated those readings (inferred), which is one reason an effect shown in one tissue does not reliably carry to another.

### Most of the animal evidence was not collected with humanin [animals]
58. This is the single most important thing the card cannot fit, and it changes how every quoted animal result should be read. A single-residue variant, serine 14 swapped for glycine, known as S14G or HNG, is reported to be more than a thousand times more potent at protecting cells than the natural sequence [1] (verified as claimed, though the review attributes that figure to a citation that does not support it, which is worth noting). Because it is so much stronger, HNG became the workhorse of the field.
59. The consequence is that the headline mouse experiments used the analogue. In the 2020 lifespan study, middle-aged female mice received HNG at 4 milligrams per kilogram by abdominal injection twice weekly from 18 months of age for 14 months, in a survival cohort of 100 animals [2] (verified, in animals). The metabolic work in rats that produced the insulin-sensitivity finding infused peptide directly into the brain ventricles during a clamp procedure, with the effect traced to hypothalamic STAT3 [1] (verified, in animals). Neither design resembles a peripheral injection of the natural peptide, and neither tested the natural peptide.
60. Half-life is given as roughly 30 minutes [2] (reported). No proper pharmacokinetic programme exists for either form, in any species: no bioavailability, no tissue distribution, no clearance route (verified as absent).

### The longevity claim, read against its own best study [animals]
61. The 2020 paper from the USC gerontology group is the most complete test of the longevity idea, and it is more mixed than its title suggests [2].
62. In worms, extra humanin lengthened mean life from 17.7 to 19.0 days, a small but statistically significant gain, and the gain disappeared in animals lacking daf-16, the worm version of FOXO [2] (verified, in animals). So in the one species where lifespan was extended, the effect required a separate longevity pathway to work through.
63. In mice, HNG from mid-life did not significantly change lifespan [2] (verified). What it did change was body composition at 28 months, in a small subgroup of five per group: less visceral fat, more lean mass, unchanged subcutaneous fat, with significantly lower circulating IGF-I and food intake no different from controls [2]. The authors suggest the dose may have been too low or started too late. Either way, the mammalian lifespan result is negative, and it is rarely quoted that way.
64. The cost side appeared in both species. Worms overexpressing humanin were smaller, leaner and less fertile; transgenic mice were about 12% shorter, 10% lighter, and had litters 46.5% smaller at 28 days [2] (verified). The authors read this as the familiar trade-off between reproduction and longevity. It is also a reminder that this is a growth-suppressing signal, not a tonic.

### The human record, which is association only [people]
65. No person has ever been given humanin or any analogue of it in a published study. There is no clinical trial, no case series, no human pharmacokinetic measurement, and no safety dataset of any kind (verified as absent; the review itself states that pharmacokinetic and safety data for long-term use are still needed [1]).
66. The human data that exist are measurements of peptide already in people. Blood levels fall with age in humans and mice, and growth hormone or IGF-1 treatment lowers them [1] (verified). Children of centenarians in an Ashkenazi Jewish cohort had higher circulating levels than age-matched controls, with 18 offspring against 19 controls [2] (verified). In newborns, cord-blood levels tracked mitochondrial DNA copy number across 73 samples [2]. Spinal fluid levels were lower in Alzheimer's disease than in controls, in a comparison of three or four people per group depending on whether the text or the figure legend is read [2] (verified, including the inconsistency).
67. Those are the numbers behind the longevity reputation: a handful of small cohorts, measuring a natural peptide, in people who were not taking anything. A correlation between high natural levels and long-lived families does not establish that injecting the peptide reproduces whatever the correlation reflects (inferred). It is just as consistent with high levels being a marker of healthier mitochondria rather than a cause of them.

### Safety: unexamined, with one specific question that will not go away [mixed]
68. There are no toxicology studies: no acute, no repeat-dose, no genotoxicity, no reproductive, no carcinogenicity work, for either the natural peptide or the analogue (verified as absent). The 14-month mouse dosing study reported no safety outcomes beyond survival and body composition [2].
69. The question the biology raises is cancer, and it cuts both ways, which is why it stays open. A peptide whose core action is blocking programmed cell death is, in principle, doing for a tumour cell what it does for a neuron. Some authors have proposed it as an oncopeptide, pointing to upregulation of the mitochondrial 16S rRNA region in non-Hodgkin lymphoma, detection in serum from some people with cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, and higher isoform messenger RNA in chemotherapy-resistant stomach tumour tissue [1] (reported). Against that, the analogue delayed tumour growth in medulloblastoma and neuroblastoma models [1] (reported). The reviewers' own conclusion was that the evidence is not conclusive [1] (verified).
70. The reproductive findings deserve to be read as safety data rather than curiosities. Smaller litters in transgenic mice and reduced reproductive output in worms are the clearest biological effects in the whole file [2]. Nothing is known about what a growth- and IGF-suppressing signal does in a person, at any exposure.

### Where the law stands, and open questions [mixed]
71. No approved product anywhere, no registered clinical programme found, and it was not among the peptides nominated for or reviewed under the US compounding lists in 2026 (verified as of October 2026). It is not named on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List, which leaves it inside the catch-all class for pharmacological substances with no government approval for human use, prohibited at all times [3] (inferred from the class wording). Note that the related mitochondrial peptide MOTS-c is named explicitly on that list as an AMPK activator [3], so the family has regulators' attention.
72. Which gene actually makes the circulating peptide, and in which tissue? Until that is answered, the claim that this is a mitochondrial hormone rests on sequence location rather than demonstrated biology.
73. Is the 21-residue or the 24-residue form the relevant molecule, and which is in a given vial? No identity testing in general circulation distinguishes them.
74. Does the natural sequence do anything the analogue does? Nearly all the potent results used the analogue, and it is more than a thousandfold stronger in cells.
75. If high natural levels mark long-lived families, is the peptide a cause or a readout? Nothing distinguishes these yet, and the mouse lifespan result argues against the simple causal reading.
76. Does blocking apoptosis for weeks at a time carry an oncological cost? Unstudied, arguable in both directions, and the one question this mechanism cannot avoid.
