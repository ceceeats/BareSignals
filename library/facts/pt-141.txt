# PT-141
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/pt-141
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: bremelanotide, Vyleesi
2. Origin: Lab-designed
3. Sequence: LDHFRWK (7 amino acids). Ring-closed (cyclic); Nle shown as L, D-Phe shown as F; acetylated start
4. Shown with an FDA approved badge
5. Legal and approval status: FDA-approved (Vyleesi) for female HSDD. Male and grey-market use is off-label.

## Summary
6. PT-141 is bremelanotide, a cyclic melanocortin peptide spun off from Melanotan II that keeps the arousal effect without strong tanning. It raises sexual desire through brain melanocortin pathways rather than blood flow, and is FDA-approved as Vyleesi for low desire in premenopausal women. Trial effects were modest, nausea is common, and male and grey-market use is off-label.
7. Punch line: Melanotan II's spin-off, approved as Vyleesi. Raises desire through the brain, not blood flow; nausea is common.
8. Used for: Low sexual desire, both sexes

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)

10. Tanning: Some skin darkening

## How it works
11. Raises desire through the brain, not blood flow · MC4R, MC3R · role Connection & desire
   Bremelanotide activates MC4R and MC3R in the brain, especially in the medial preoptic area, a hub for sexual desire. It works centrally rather than on blood flow, so it can act where PDE5 drugs do nothing. Phase 3 human trials in women led to approval.
12. Lifts dopamine in desire centres · MPOA dopamine release · role Connection & desire
   Activating these receptors raises dopamine release in the medial preoptic area, the circuit that drives sexual motivation. This step comes from animal work. In people it shows up as modest gains in desire in phase 3 trials, with nausea common.
13. Can darken skin and gums with repeat use · MC1R · role Tanning
   Bremelanotide still activates MC1R on pigment cells, a leftover from its parent melanotan II. With repeated dosing this can darken skin and gums, a known effect listed for the approved drug.
14. Briefly raises blood pressure after a dose · Sympathetic MC4R tone · role Heart & vessels
   MC4R activation raises sympathetic nerve tone, which briefly lifts blood pressure after a dose. Blood pressure concerns halted an earlier nasal programme, and the approved injection carries a blood pressure warning.
15. Mechanism overview: Bremelanotide is a cyclic peptide derived from Melanotan II, tuned to keep the arousal effect with less tanning. It activates MC4R and MC3R in the brain, especially in the medial preoptic area, where it raises dopamine release in circuits that drive sexual desire. Because it works centrally rather than on penile or clitoral blood flow, it can act where PDE5 drugs do nothing. Sympathetic MC4R tone briefly raises blood pressure, and residual MC1R activity darkens skin and gums with repeat dosing.

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### The part of the origin story that explains the side effects [lab]
48. Bremelanotide is not a designed-from-scratch molecule. It is the active breakdown product of melanotan II, differing from it by one chemical group: where melanotan II ends in an amide, this ends in an acid [1] (verified). Palatin stopped work on melanotan II in 2000 and carried the metabolite forward instead [1].
49. That lineage matters because the parent was a tanning peptide, and the family it belongs to acts on all the melanocortin receptors except the one that drives cortisol. Bremelanotide is described as a non-selective agonist across MC1, MC3, MC4 and MC5, acting mainly at MC3 and MC4 [1] (verified). The arousal effect and the pigment effect are not two separate things that happen to coexist; they are two receptors in one family being hit by one molecule. Nothing about the design removed the pigment arm, it was reduced relative to the parent.
50. The development path is also the clearest safety signal in the file, and it is usually left out. A nasal spray was taken into mid-stage trials in both women and men, including four trials in erectile dysfunction. The FDA halted that programme in 2007 over blood pressure increases, the commercial partner ended its agreement, and the nasal formulation was abandoned in 2008 [1] (verified). The injection that was eventually approved in June 2019 exists because the nasal route failed on cardiovascular grounds, not because injection was preferred.

### What the approved trials actually measured, and what they found [people]
51. Two identical 24-week randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials supported approval, with 635 women on drug and 632 on placebo, followed by a 52-week open-label extension [2] (verified).
52. There were two co-primary endpoints, both questionnaires. On the desire domain of the Female Sexual Function Index, the change from baseline was +0.5 against +0.2 on placebo in the first trial and +0.6 against +0.2 in the second. On the single question measuring distress about low desire, the change was minus 0.7 against minus 0.4 in both [2] (verified). The p-values are small, the differences are not: roughly a third of a point on one scale and three tenths on another, against placebo.
53. The endpoint that is easiest for a non-specialist to interpret was negative. The number of satisfying sexual events did not differ from placebo, with p-values of 0.76 and 0.70 [2] (verified). So the approved claim rests on self-reported desire and distress, not on a change in what actually happened.
54. The dropout figures belong next to the efficacy figures. Premature discontinuation during the blinded period was 40% against 13% on placebo in the first trial and 39% against 25% in the second, and 18% of women on drug stopped specifically for adverse reactions against 2% on placebo [2] (verified). A treatment that four in ten participants stop inside six months is being described accurately only when that is said out loud.

### The tolerability profile in plain numbers [people]
55. Nausea affected 40% of women on drug against 1.3% on placebo, usually starting within an hour and lasting about two hours. It was heaviest on the first dose, 21%, and fell to around 3% with later doses. Thirteen percent needed anti-sickness medication and 8% stopped treatment because of it [2] (verified). Flushing reached 20.3% against 0.3%, headache 11.3% against 1.9%, vomiting 4.8%, and injection-site reactions 13.2% against 8.4% [2].
56. Pigment change is the effect most often treated as cosmetic trivia, and the label's own numbers argue otherwise. Focal darkening of the face, gums and breasts occurred in 1% with as-needed use, but in a study using daily dosing 38% developed it, with a further 14% developing new changes on continued use. Risk was higher with darker skin and with frequent dosing, and resolution was not confirmed in everyone [2] (verified). Frequent or daily use is exactly the pattern reported outside the label, and it is the pattern associated with the pigment change that may not reverse (inferred).
57. One case of acute hepatitis occurred in the open-label extension and the drug's role could not be definitively excluded, though liver test outliers were not imbalanced in the blinded trials [2] (verified).

### The cardiovascular arm, measured rather than described [people]
58. Every dose raises blood pressure and lowers heart rate, typically resolving within 12 hours [2] (verified). In a 127-patient ambulatory monitoring study the mean daytime rise was 1.9 mmHg systolic and 1.7 mmHg diastolic, with a peak mean systolic rise of 2.8 mmHg and a mean heart rate fall of 0.5 beats per minute [2] (verified).
59. Those averages look small, and the label's response to them is not small: it is contraindicated in uncontrolled high blood pressure and in known cardiovascular disease, not recommended in people at high cardiovascular risk, and requires cardiovascular risk assessment before starting and periodically afterwards [2] (verified). The averages are averages; the regulator priced in the individuals at the tail, and the 2007 halt of the nasal programme shows what the tail can look like with higher systemic exposure [1].
60. This is the one section where off-label male use diverges sharply from the evidence base. The trials that generated every number above enrolled premenopausal women. No approved indication, and no completed late-stage programme, exists for men; the male trials in erectile dysfunction were part of the nasal programme the FDA stopped [1] (verified). The cardiovascular screening the label requires has no counterpart when the compound is obtained outside that system (inferred).

### What the body does with it, including the interactions nobody expects [people]
61. Subcutaneous bioavailability is essentially complete, around 100%. Peak concentration averages 72.8 nanograms per millilitre at a median of about one hour, plasma protein binding is 21%, volume of distribution about 25 litres, clearance about 6.5 litres per hour, and terminal half-life about 2.7 hours. It is broken down by hydrolysis of the ring's amide bonds, with about 65% of a radiolabelled dose recovered in urine and 23% in faeces [2] (verified).
62. Impairment matters more than the short half-life suggests. Exposure rose 1.2-fold in mild, 1.5-fold in moderate and 2-fold in severe kidney impairment, and 1.2-fold in mild and 1.7-fold in moderate liver impairment, with severe liver impairment never studied [2] (verified).
63. The interaction section is the part most likely to catch someone out, because it has nothing to do with sexual medicine. The drug slows stomach emptying, so it can reduce absorption of swallowed medicines. The label says to avoid oral naltrexone products used for alcohol or opioid dependence, because naltrexone exposure may be significantly reduced, and to avoid oral drugs that depend on reaching a threshold concentration, such as antibiotics; it also flags drugs needed to act fast, such as indomethacin [2] (verified).
64. Use is not recommended in pregnancy, with contraception advised. Human data are limited to a handful of outcomes in the trials. In animals, fetal harm appeared in dogs at exposures at least 16 times the maximum human dose and developmental delays in mice at roughly 125 times [2] (verified).

### Who owns it now, and where the law stands [mixed]
65. The commercial history is a string of partners handing it on: King Pharmaceuticals in 2004 and out after the 2007 halt, Gedeon Richter for Europe in 2014 and terminated in 2016, AMAG for North America in 2017, approval in 2019 [1] (verified). Palatin then sold the product outright to Cosette Pharmaceuticals in 2023 in a deal worth up to $171 million [3] (verified). Four partners and an outright sale inside twenty years is a commercial signal, not a scientific one, but it tells you how the owners read the market.
66. It is approved in the United States for premenopausal women with acquired, generalised hypoactive sexual desire disorder, with the label explicitly excluding postmenopausal women, men, and use to enhance performance [2] (verified). It is not approved in Europe or the UK [1] (verified as of the source; EMA pages could not be opened directly for this review).
67. For sport: bremelanotide is not named on the 2026 Prohibited List, and because it holds government approval for human therapeutic use it does not fall into the catch-all class for non-approved substances either, so on the published text it is not prohibited [4] (inferred from the class definitions). That is a different position from most compounds in this library and should not be generalised to melanotan II.

### Open questions [mixed]
68. Is a third of a point on a desire questionnaire, with no change in satisfying events, a difference people can feel? The approval says the endpoint was met; it does not settle what the effect is worth, and the 40% dropout rate suggests many participants answered that question for themselves.
69. Does the pigment change reverse? The label says resolution was not confirmed in all patients, and the highest rates came from daily dosing [2]. Nobody has followed frequent users long enough to answer it.
70. What does repeated use do in men, at the doses actually used? The entire safety dataset is female, the male programme was stopped on blood pressure grounds, and no one has repeated it [1].
71. Why does the brain effect need a molecule that also hits pigment and vascular receptors? A genuinely MC4-selective agonist would answer whether the side effects are separable from the effect.
72. How much of the reported benefit is anticipation? With an as-needed drug taken before a planned event, a short-onset side effect profile that is obvious to the user makes effective blinding difficult, which the 40% versus 13% dropout gap hints at (inferred).
