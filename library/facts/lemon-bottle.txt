# Lemon Bottle
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/lemon-bottle
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: Lemon Bottle fat dissolving, Lemon Bottle lipolysis solution, Lemon Bottle Fat Dissolve, 레몬보틀
2. Origin: Cosmetic solution: vitamin + enzyme + phospholipid
3. Legal and approval status: Switzerland: illegal unauthorised medicine (Swissmedic warning, 26 Mar 2024). Finland: Fimea and Tukes warning (28 Mar 2024); cosmetics may not be injected. UK: MHRA treats it as a cosmetic, not a medicine; the GPhC warned pharmacies off selling it (Apr 2025). US: not FDA-approved; FDA warned spas in 2010 that injectable fat dissolvers are unapproved. Korea: sold as a cosmetic; no regulator action found. Australia: no TGA statement found.

## Summary
4. Lemon Bottle is a Korean cosmetic solution sold as a fat dissolver and injected under the skin. Its label lists riboflavin, bromelain (a pineapple enzyme) and lecithin, and it is claimed to break down fat cells, though no data back this. No human or animal study exists, regulators say no effect is proven, and lab tests found mislabelled contents. Reports include swelling, lumps, infection and skin damage.
5. Punch line: Korean riboflavin, bromelain and lecithin mix, injected to melt spot fat. Its maker says not for injection.
6. Used for: Spot fat reduction: chin, belly, flanks, thighs

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Fat metabolism: Sold to dissolve spot fat
8. Skin & aesthetics: Used as a body-contouring cosmetic
9. Inflammatory signaling: Bromelain claimed to cut swelling
10. Cellular energy: Riboflavin is a fat-burning coenzyme, in theory

## How it works
11. Claimed to break fat cells down so they leave in urine · Maker's claim, no data · role Fat metabolism
   The maker says the product breaks down fat cells so their contents leave in urine. No study describes how it would do this, and the product contains no deoxycholic acid, the detergent that licensed fat dissolving injections use. Swissmedic found tested packs did not match their label.
12. Phospholipid similar to the 'PC' in older fat dissolvers · Lecithin / phosphatidylcholine · role Fat metabolism
   Lecithin supplies phosphatidylcholine, the phospholipid used in older off-label fat dissolving mixes. In those mixes the fat cell damage came mostly from the deoxycholate added to dissolve it, not from phosphatidylcholine itself. Lecithin alone is not an established fat cell destroying agent.
13. Pineapple enzyme claimed to reduce swelling · Bromelain, protease · role Inflammatory signaling
   Bromelain is a group of protein-cutting enzymes (proteases) from pineapple stem. It is marketed here to reduce swelling and downtime after injection. There is no study of injected bromelain in this product, so any anti-swelling effect is a claim, not a finding.
14. Vitamin that helps enzymes burn fat · Riboflavin → FAD, β-oxidation · role Cellular energy
   Riboflavin, vitamin B2, becomes FAD, a cofactor the enzymes of beta-oxidation need to burn fatty acids. Extra riboflavin corrects a deficiency but is not known to speed fat burning in people with normal levels. No study tests it as a fat loss injection.
15. Mechanism overview: Licensed injection lipolysis relies on deoxycholic acid, a bile-acid detergent that lyses adipocyte membranes and triggers an inflammatory clearance response. Older off-label 'lipodissolve' mixes combined phosphatidylcholine with deoxycholate as solubiliser. Lemon Bottle declares lecithin (a phosphatidylcholine source), bromelain (cysteine proteases from pineapple stem) and riboflavin (precursor of FAD and FMN, cofactors in fatty-acid beta-oxidation), and its maker states it contains no deoxycholic acid. None of these is an established adipocytolytic agent on its own, and no mechanism study exists for the product. Some clinic consent forms nonetheless list deoxycholic acid, and Swissmedic found declared ingredients absent or replaced in tested samples.

## What is inside the blend
16. Riboflavin (vitamin B2): null not disclosed · Coenzyme in fat burning; gives the yellow colour
17. Bromelain: null not disclosed · Pineapple enzyme, claimed to cut swelling
18. Lecithin: null not disclosed · Phospholipid, the 'PC' of older fat dissolvers
19. Most common ratio: Riboflavin · Bromelain · Lecithin, amounts not disclosed = Not disclosed (n/a (single branded product))
20. Version Maker's declaration: Riboflavin · Bromelain · Lecithin, amounts not disclosed; no deoxycholic acid = Not disclosed
21. Version Swissmedic lab samples (2024): One sample caffeine only; another none of the declared ingredients = Varied by pack
22. Version Some clinic consent forms: Deoxycholic acid · Riboflavin · Bromelain = Not disclosed
23. Why Lecithin is in it: The only part related to known injectable fat dissolvers
24. Why Bromelain is in it: Marketed to reduce swelling and downtime
25. Why Riboflavin is in it: Marketed to speed fat metabolism
26. Mixing chemistry: Sold as a ready solution labelled as a cosmetic, not made or tested to sterile injectable standards.
27. Mixing chemistry: Riboflavin breaks down in light; colour fading is a sign of exposure.
28. Mixing chemistry: Bromelain is a protein enzyme that loses activity in heat and can trigger allergy.
29. Mixing chemistry: Lab tests showed contents can differ from the label and between packs.
30. Self-mixing: Nothing to mix; it comes ready. Some injectors combine it with other fat-dissolving solutions, which stacks unknowns.

## Dosage (community reported unless tagged)
31. Typical: No standard dose · Several sessions, spacing not standardised · cycle Results claimed over 4–6 weeks · route Sub-Q injection (maker says external use only) (reported)
32. Half-life: per component
33. Most people take: No standard dose. Injectors size it by treatment area and number of vials per session, repeated over several sessions. Amounts per vial are not disclosed, and lab tests found contents that don't match the label.

## What people report
34. Injected in several sessions into chin, belly, flanks or thighs; results claimed over 4 to 6 weeks. Common: pain, swelling, redness, bruising, numbness, hard lumps. Rare but serious: infection, abscess, skin necrosis, nerve injury, allergy. The maker says the product is for external use only and does not endorse injecting it.
35. Liked / worked: One clinic reports ~200 tolerated treatments
36. Liked / worked: Cheap and widely available
37. Liked / worked: Bromelain and B2 are low-risk when eaten
38. What worked: One UK doctor reported treating about 200 patients with good tolerance and visible results on chins, bellies, flanks and thighs. These are clinic anecdotes, not measured outcomes.
39. Went wrong / did not work: Lab tests found contents not matching the label
40. Went wrong / did not work: No published trial; regulators say no effect proven
41. Went wrong / did not work: Infections, abscesses and skin death reported
42. What went wrong: Regulators say no effect is proven. A UK complaints register logged 90 complaints in 2023, 50 of them in the last three months, including no effect, long bruising and swelling, infection and claimed tissue death. A published case describes nodules, discharge and skin death after ten vials from a non-medical injector.
43. Most reported: Several sessions, results by 4 to 6 weeks
44. Most reported: Swelling, bruising, lumps, numbness
45. Most reported: Often injected by non-medical staff
46. Most reported: Maker says not for injection

## What has been tested
47. Fat metabolism: Swissmedic lab analysis of retail samples (lab)
48. Fat metabolism: Lemon Bottle in any clinical trial (none)
49. Skin & aesthetics: Clinic treatments, chin and body (users)
50. Never in a published human trial. Swissmedic's lab tested samples in 2024: contents did not match the label and varied between packs; one held only caffeine, another none of the declared ingredients. No clinical trial or safety study is published.
51. What it helps: No published human or animal study. The evidence is clinic anecdote on one side and regulator warnings, a lab analysis showing mislabelled contents, complaint data and a case report of skin damage on the other.

## Who shouldn't take it
52. People allergic to pineapple, papaya or latex: Contains bromelain, a pineapple enzyme that cross-reacts. (reported)
53. People on blood thinners: Bromelain has antiplatelet effects and injections bruise. (reported)
54. People with soy or egg allergy: Contains lecithin, usually soy or egg derived. (inferred)
55. Anyone using salon (non-medical) injectors: Swissmedic and Nordic agencies warned of infections from unauthorised injections. (reported)

## Synergies (paired with)
56. L-Carnitine: L-carnitine carries fatty acids into mitochondria to be burned, and oral trials show a small extra weight loss. Clinics pair it with Lemon Bottle to burn whatever fat is released. The pair has never been tested together.
57. Lipo-C: Lipo-C supplies liver fat export nutrients and carnitine, while Lemon Bottle is injected locally into a fat pocket. Clinics combine them as whole-body plus spot treatment. Neither has a fat loss trial, and the pair has not been tested.
58. MIC + B12: MIC + B12 gives lipotropes for liver fat export and B12, while Lemon Bottle targets one area. Clinics offer both in fat loss packages. Neither product has a weight loss trial, so the pairing is user logic, not trial data.
59. AOD-9604: AOD-9604, a growth hormone fragment, was shown to block new fat storage in rodents. Users add it to Lemon Bottle for spot fat work. The pairing is user logic, not trial data.
60. HGH frag 176–191: HGH frag 176–191 raised fat breakdown in obese mice and is often injected locally, the same way Lemon Bottle is used. People pair them for stubborn areas. The pair has not been tested together.
61. Adipotide: Adipotide cuts the blood supply to white fat and shrank fat in obese monkeys, but injured kidneys at higher doses. It is a far stronger and riskier agent than Lemon Bottle. The pairing has no data at all.

## Where it comes from
62. A Korean 'fat-dissolving' solution sold as a cosmetic and injected under the skin in salons and clinics. The maker lists riboflavin (vitamin B2), bromelain (a pineapple enzyme) and lecithin (a phospholipid) as its core ingredients, and says it contains no deoxycholic acid.
63. First studied for: Went viral on TikTok in 2023 (over 80 million views by year end) as a cheap, 'no downtime' fat dissolver for chins, bellies and thighs.
64. Name story: A brand name: 'Lemon' for the fresh, natural image and the yellow riboflavin colour, 'Bottle' for the vial. It contains no lemon as a declared active. [S1] (inferred)
65. 2010: FDA warns US spas: injectable fat dissolvers are unapproved
66. 2015: Deoxycholic acid approved in the US for double chin, the licensed alternative
67. 2023: Lemon Bottle goes viral on TikTok; UK complaints jump to 90
68. Mar 2024: Swissmedic declares it illegal; lab finds mislabelled contents. Finland warns too
69. Apr 2025: UK pharmacy regulator warns pharmacies off selling it
70. Today: Still sold as a cosmetic and injected in salons
