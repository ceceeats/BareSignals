# Collagen peptides
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/collagen-peptides
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: hydrolysed collagen, hydrolyzed collagen, collagen hydrolysate, gelatin hydrolysate, bioactive collagen peptides, marine collagen, bovine collagen, porcine collagen, Pro-Hyp, Hyp-Gly
2. Origin: Digest of a natural protein: collagen from cow hide, pig skin, fish skin or scales, cut with enzymes
3. Legal and approval status: Sold worldwide as a food ingredient and dietary supplement; gelatin is GRAS in the US. Not a medicine anywhere.

## Summary
4. Collagen peptides are short protein fragments made by enzyme-cutting collagen, usually from cow hide, pig skin or fish skin. People take them for skin, joints, tendon and bone, since fragments like Pro-Hyp reach the blood intact and may prompt skin and cartilage cells to build matrix. Pooled trials show modest gains in skin hydration, but a 2025 meta-analysis found no skin benefit in independent or high-quality trials.
5. Punch line: Enzyme-cut collagen from cow, pig or fish, a daily scoop. Pro-Hyp fragments reach the blood intact.
6. Used for: Skin hydration and elasticity, joint pain, tendon and bone

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Skin & aesthetics: Hydration and elasticity in pooled trials
8. Bone & cartilage: Bone density after menopause, one trial
9. Tissue repair: Tendon size and pain with training
10. Pain & sensory: Knee arthritis pain, modestly
11. Muscle & performance (backed only by animal or user reports): More lean mass with strength training
12. Hair (backed only by animal or user reports): Users report stronger nails and hair
13. Digestive & gut (backed only by animal or user reports): Users report gut comfort

## How it works
14. Digestion cuts it into short fragments and free amino acids · Digestion · role Digestive & gut
   In the gut, protease enzymes cut collagen peptides into free amino acids and short chains. Most of the dose ends up as ordinary amino acids, the same fate as any dietary protein. Only a small fraction survives as intact fragments, and that fraction is where the specific claims rest.
15. Pro-Hyp and Hyp-Gly reach the blood intact · Hydroxyproline dipeptides · role Skin & aesthetics
   Dipeptides containing hydroxyproline, chiefly Pro-Hyp and Hyp-Gly, resist digestion and appear in blood within one to two hours in human feeding studies. After four weeks of daily intake the share of Hyp-Gly rose while Pro-Hyp stayed most abundant. These are the closest thing collagen has to an active ingredient.
16. Fragments prompt skin fibroblasts to make collagen and hyaluronan · Fibroblast signal, cell studies · role Skin & aesthetics
   In cell studies, these fragments signal skin fibroblasts, the cells that build the dermis, to divide and make hyaluronan and collagen. Pooled human trials show modest gains in skin hydration and elasticity, but independent and high quality trials found no benefit.
17. Supplies glycine, proline and hydroxyproline for new collagen · Building blocks · role Tissue repair
   The freed amino acids supply glycine, proline and hydroxyproline, the raw materials collagen is built from. Whether this beats an equal dose of other protein is largely untested. In eight men, vitamin C enriched gelatin before skipping roughly doubled a collagen synthesis marker.
18. May push cartilage and bone cells toward building matrix · Chondrocyte and osteoblast signal · role Bone & cartilage
   In cell studies, collagen fragments push chondrocytes and osteoblasts, the cartilage and bone building cells, toward making matrix. Human trials are small: knee arthritis pain fell modestly in high risk of bias trials, and one industry linked year long trial reported higher spine and hip density after menopause.
19. Mechanism overview: Collagen peptides are collagen cut by enzymes into a mixture of fragments, mostly a few to a few dozen amino acids long. There is no single chain: like Cerebrolysin, the product is defined by its source and process. After a dose, most is broken down to free amino acids, but hydroxyproline-containing dipeptides, chiefly Pro-Hyp and Hyp-Gly, resist digestion and appear in blood within an hour or two. In cell studies these fragments prompt skin fibroblasts to divide and make hyaluronan, and cartilage and bone cells to build matrix. The rest supplies glycine, proline and hydroxyproline, the raw material of new collagen.

## Dosage (community reported unless tagged)

22. Most people take: Trials use 2.5–5 g a day for skin, 5 g for bone and tendon, about 10 g for knee arthritis and 15 g with strength training for muscle. Any time of day; tendon studies give it with vitamin C about an hour before loading.

## What has been tested
39. Skin & aesthetics: Skin hydration and elasticity (trial)
40. Pain & sensory: Knee arthritis pain (trial)
41. Bone & cartilage: Bone density after menopause (trial)
42. Tissue repair: Achilles and patellar tendon (trial)
43. Muscle & performance: Lean mass with strength training (trial)
44. Dozens of small randomised trials, mostly 2.5 to 15 g a day for 8 weeks to a year: skin hydration and elasticity, knee arthritis pain, bone density after menopause, tendon and muscle with training.
45. What it helps: Pooled randomised trials show modest improvements in skin hydration and elasticity, but wrinkles do not reliably improve, and a 2025 meta-analysis found no skin benefit in independent or high-quality trials. Knee arthritis pain falls modestly in small, high-risk-of-bias trials. Single industry-linked trials report higher bone density after menopause, larger tendons and more lean mass with training.
46. Trial: active · Supplement trial · telomeres, inflammation and strength over 24 weeks · NCT07456449 · University of Vienna. One of many ongoing collagen trials; this one is co-sponsored by an industry collagen research institute. (checked 2026-10-24) [S8] (reported)

## Who shouldn't take it
47. People with fish allergy (marine collagen): Fish collagen is a registered fish allergen; one in five fish-allergic patients in one study had IgE to it, some with a negative standard fish test. Jellyfish and shellfish-sourced powders exist too. (verified)
48. People with alpha-gal syndrome: Bovine and porcine collagen carry the alpha-gal sugar from tick-bite meat allergy; reactions by mouth are uncommon but can be delayed and severe. (reported)
49. Kidney stone formers: Collagen is rich in hydroxyproline, which the body partly turns into oxalate; 5–10 g gelatin loads raised urinary oxalate and glycolate. (verified)
50. People with histamine intolerance: Bone broth and some collagen powders are reported to trigger flushing or headaches; slow-cooked and fermented sources can carry more histamine. Thin data. (reported)
51. Anyone counting it as their protein: It has no tryptophan and little leucine, so it is not a complete protein; matters on low-protein or kidney diets where every gram is budgeted. (verified)
52. People avoiding pork or beef: Most powders are bovine or porcine and labels often just say collagen. (verified)
53. Pregnant or breastfeeding: No supplement-dose trials; heavy metals found in some powders matter more here. (inferred)

## Synergies (paired with)
54. Vitamin C: Vitamin C is the cofactor the enzymes need to finish new collagen, while collagen peptides supply the raw amino acids and signalling dipeptides. In eight men, vitamin C enriched gelatin before exercise roughly doubled a collagen synthesis marker, but larger trials of the pair are lacking.
55. GHK-Cu: GHK-Cu signals fibroblasts to make collagen and elastin and remodels the matrix, while collagen peptides supply building blocks and fragments that prompt fibroblasts in cell studies. People pair them for skin from the signal and supply sides, but the pair has not been tested together.
56. BPC-157: BPC-157 grows blood vessels and moves repair cells into injured tissue in animals, while collagen peptides supply the amino acids new tendon needs and grew tendons in small training trials. People stack them for tendon recovery, but the pair has not been tested together.

## Where it comes from
57. Gelatin, boiled-down collagen, has been eaten for centuries. Enzyme-cut collagen hydrolysate became a commercial ingredient in the late 20th century; in 2005 a Japanese team showed short collagen fragments such as Pro-Hyp reach human blood intact.
58. First studied for: A by-product of the meat and gelatin industry, sold first for joints and bone, later as a skin supplement.
59. Name story: Collagen comes from the Greek kolla, glue, plus -gen, producing: boiling hide and bone made glue. Hydrolysate means split with water and enzymes into short pieces; peptides is the marketing name for the same thing. [S9] (reported)
60. 2005: Pro-Hyp found in human blood after eating collagen hydrolysate
61. 2014: 2.5 g a day lifts skin elasticity in an 8-week trial
62. 2018: 5 g a day raises spine and hip bone density in a one-year trial
63. 2025: Meta-analysis: no skin benefit in independent, high-quality trials
64. Today: A cheap daily supplement; modest, contested benefits

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### What is in the scoop [lab]
65. Collagen peptides are not one peptide. They are collagen, mostly type I from cow hide, pig skin or fish skin and scales, boiled to gelatin and then cut with enzymes into a mixture of fragments [11] (verified). Like Cerebrolysin, the product is defined by its source and its process rather than by a sequence, so two brands with the same label can differ in fragment size, source animal and the share of small peptides (inferred).
66. What matters biologically is the small fraction that survives digestion. In 2005 researchers fed volunteers gelatin hydrolysate and found prolyl-hydroxyproline (Pro-Hyp) as the dominant collagen peptide in blood, peaking within one to two hours, with smaller amounts of Ala-Hyp, Pro-Hyp-Gly and others [11] (verified). After four weeks of daily intake the blood profile shifted, with a rising share of hydroxyprolyl-glycine (Hyp-Gly), while Pro-Hyp stayed the most abundant [12] (verified). These two dipeptides are the closest thing collagen has to an active ingredient.

### The skin evidence, and the funding problem [people]
67. Early trials of specific peptides were positive: 2.5 g or 5 g a day for eight weeks improved skin elasticity against placebo in 69 women, with hydration trending better but not significant [4] (verified). Pooled analyses since then report modest gains in hydration and elasticity [3] (reported), and an updated 2026 meta-analysis put the effects at standardised mean differences of about 0.44 for hydration and 0.62 for elasticity, with no reliable effect on wrinkles and signs of publication bias [2] (reported).
68. The 2025 meta-analysis of 23 trials and 1,474 people cut the other way. Pooled, collagen looked effective; split by funding, trials without industry money showed no benefit, and high-quality trials showed no effect on hydration, elasticity or wrinkles [1] (reported). Industry groups disputed how funding and quality were classified [1] (reported). The honest reading is a small effect at best, measured mostly by instruments on the skin surface, in short trials, from a field where most studies are commercially linked (inferred).

### Joints, bone, tendon and muscle [people]
69. Knee osteoarthritis: a 2023 meta-analysis of four trials and 507 patients found less pain on collagen peptides (standardised mean difference about −0.58), but every trial was rated high risk of bias and the result was heterogeneous [5] (verified).
70. Bone: in a one-year trial of 102 postmenopausal women with low bone density, 5 g a day raised spine and femoral neck density against placebo, with a rise in a bone formation marker [6] (reported; summarised by the manufacturer, single trial).
71. Tendon: with calf strengthening, 5 g a day sped recovery in 20 people with Achilles tendinopathy in a crossover trial [7] (verified), and in young men doing heavy training, 5 g a day grew the patellar tendon more than placebo without changing stiffness or strength [8] (verified). In eight men, 15 g of vitamin C enriched gelatin an hour before skipping roughly doubled a collagen synthesis marker [9] (verified).
72. Muscle: older men on 15 g a day plus resistance training gained more lean mass than training alone over 12 weeks [10] (reported). Collagen is a poor protein for muscle on paper, so the result is surprising and has not been widely replicated (inferred).

### Safety, quality and who should think twice [mixed]
73. Side effects in trials are rare and mild: fullness, a taste, mild stomach upset (reported across trials). The real issues are source and quality. Hydroxyproline, abundant in collagen, is partly converted to oxalate; in a controlled feeding study gelatin raised urinary oxalate by 43% and glycolate fivefold, and 5 to 10 g loads moved them while 1 to 2 g did not [13] (verified). That matters for calcium oxalate stone formers.
74. Allergy depends on the animal. Fish collagen is a registered fish allergen, and some fish-allergic patients react to it without reacting to the usual fish test [14] (verified). Bovine and porcine collagen carry alpha-gal, the sugar behind tick-bite meat allergy [15] (reported).
75. Heavy metals: a non-profit test of 30 collagen products found measurable lead in 37% and cadmium in 17%, with 4 of 30 over its limits; industry called the report sensational and not peer reviewed [16] (reported). Look for third-party testing and a named source animal (inferred).

### Open questions [mixed]
76. Does the benefit come from specific dipeptides, or from protein and glycine any food could supply? Few trials compare collagen with an equal dose of another protein.
77. Why do independent trials of skin outcomes look null while sponsored ones look positive?
78. Do bone density gains hold and reduce fractures? No fracture trial exists.
79. What dose matters? Skin trials found no clear dose-response, and duration seemed to matter more [2] (reported).
