# Semaglutide
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/semaglutide
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus, NN9535, NNC 0113-0217
2. Origin: Lab-modified natural chain
3. Sequence: HAEGTFTSDVSSYLEGQAAKEFIAWLVRGRG (31 amino acids). Position 2 is Aib, shown as A; fatty acid chain on K20 not shown
4. Shown with an FDA approved badge
5. Legal and approval status: FDA-approved (Ozempic, Rybelsus, Wegovy), plus an oral Wegovy pill for obesity approved in December 2025 (reported). Widely approved worldwide. The grey-market vials in this catalog are not the approved product. WADA does not ban it.

## Summary
6. Semaglutide is Novo Nordisk's lab-modified GLP-1 analogue, with two amino acid swaps and a fatty-acid tail that stretch the natural two-minute hormone into a once-weekly drug. It is approved for type 2 diabetes and obesity, working mainly by suppressing appetite. Trials show about 15% weight loss and fewer heart attacks and strokes in some patients; users report GI upset, muscle loss without resistance training and regain after stopping.
7. Punch line: Ozempic and Wegovy. The weekly GLP-1 shot that quiets food noise; about 15% weight loss, fewer heart attacks.
8. Used for: Weight loss, type 2 diabetes

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
9. Appetite & satiety: Quiets hunger and food noise
10. Glucose & insulin: Lowers blood sugar
11. Heart & vessels: Fewer heart attacks and strokes
12. Reward & urges: Fewer cravings, including alcohol
13. Organ function (backed only by animal or user reports): Protects the kidneys
14. Mood (backed only by animal or user reports): Mood shifts reported

## How it works
15. Quiets hunger in the brain · GLP-1 receptor · role Appetite & satiety
   Semaglutide activates GLP-1 receptors in the hypothalamus and brainstem, the brain's appetite centres. Hunger falls and fullness comes sooner, so people eat less. This is the main way weight falls, confirmed in the large STEP trials with about 15% weight loss in obesity.
16. Slows stomach emptying · GLP-1 receptor, gut · role Appetite & satiety
   Through GLP-1 receptors in the gut and its nerve signalling, semaglutide slows how fast the stomach empties. Food sits longer, fullness lasts, and sugar reaches the blood more gradually. This also drives the nausea and GI upset common during dose increases.
17. Raises insulin and cuts glucagon when glucose is high · GLP-1R, beta and alpha cells · role Glucose & insulin
   On pancreatic beta cells it boosts insulin, and on alpha cells it cuts glucagon, but only when glucose is high. Blood sugar falls with little risk of hypoglycemia on its own. The SUSTAIN trials confirmed better glucose control in type 2 diabetes.
18. Fatty tail rides albumin for weekly dosing · Albumin binding · role Glucose & insulin
   A C18 fatty diacid chain lets semaglutide bind albumin, the main blood protein, which shields it from clearance. A swap at position 8 also blocks the enzyme DPP-4. Together these stretch its action to a full week.
19. Cuts heart attack and stroke risk in trials · CV outcomes, SELECT · role Heart & vessels
   In SELECT, adults with obesity and established heart disease but no diabetes had 20% fewer heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths than on placebo. All three outcomes moved the same way. The result applies to that population, not to healthy people.
20. Dampens reward-driven cravings · Mesolimbic GLP-1R · role Reward & urges
   GLP-1 receptors in mesolimbic reward circuits, the brain's craving system, turn down the pull of food reward when activated. Users report fewer cravings. The circuit detail comes mainly from animal studies; in people it shows up as reported changes in craving.
21. Mechanism overview: Semaglutide is a GLP-1 analogue with 94 percent sequence match to the native hormone. Two changes make it last a week: a swap at position 8 blocks DPP-4, and a C18 fatty diacid chain binds albumin, slowing clearance. It activates GLP-1 receptors in the hypothalamus and brainstem to cut hunger, in mesolimbic circuits to dampen food reward, in the gut to slow emptying, and on beta and alpha cells to raise insulin and cut glucagon only when glucose is high. Weight falls mostly through eating less.

## Dosage (community reported unless tagged)
22. Typical: 0.25–2.4 mg (HD 7.2 mg) · once weekly · cycle Ongoing · route Sub-Q (verified)
23. Half-life: ~7 days
24. Dose note: Label maximum is 2.4 mg weekly, 7.2 mg for the high-dose version approved in 2026.
25. Standard: 1–2.4 mg · 1× weekly · ongoing; 0.25 mg start, step up every 4 wk · Sub-Q · for most patients and compounded users; many hold at 1–1.7 mg [S1] [S2] (reported)
26. Microdose: 0.125–0.5 mg · 1× weekly or split 2× weekly; some every 2–4 wk for maintenance · ongoing · Sub-Q · for low-side-effect weight maintenance, 'longevity' [S1] [S2] [S3] (reported)
27. High (label HD): 7.2 mg · 1× weekly · after ≥4 wk tolerating 2.4 mg · Sub-Q · for obesity needing more loss (Wegovy HD) [S4] (reported)

## What people report
28. Grey-market and compounded use is enormous. Users report strong appetite suppression, GI upset on titration, and the same regain when they stop.
29. Liked / worked: About 15% weight loss in obesity trials

31. Liked / worked: Fewer heart attacks and strokes without diabetes

37. What went wrong: Nausea, vomiting, constipation; rare pancreatitis and gallbladder issues; muscle loss without resistance training; weight regain on stopping. A boxed warning covers thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodents.

## What has been tested
42. Fat metabolism: Obesity, STEP program (trial)
43. Glucose & insulin: Type 2 diabetes, SUSTAIN (trial)
44. Heart & vessels: Heart events in obesity, SELECT (trial)
45. Among the most heavily trialled peptides in existence: large cardiovascular-outcome and weight trials (STEP, SUSTAIN, SELECT). SELECT showed a reduction in major cardiovascular events in people with obesity and established heart disease.
46. What it helps: STEP trials showed about 15% weight loss in obesity, and SUSTAIN showed better glucose control in type 2 diabetes. SELECT showed fewer heart attacks and strokes in people with obesity and heart disease without diabetes. Users report strong appetite suppression and fewer cravings. GI upset on titration, muscle loss without resistance training and regain after stopping are common themes.
47. Trial: active · Phase 3 · fatty liver disease (MASH) outcomes · NCT04822181 · Novo Nordisk. Approved as Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus, with dozens of trials still open from liver disease to addiction. (checked 2026-10-23) [S5] (inferred)

## Who shouldn't take it
48. Personal or family history of MTC or MEN2: Causes thyroid C-cell tumours in rodents; boxed warning and contraindication on the label. (verified)
49. Anyone booked for surgery, endoscopy or sedation: Food can stay in the stomach for days and be inhaled into the lungs under anaesthesia. (verified)
50. People with diabetic retinopathy: Fast glucose drops worsened retinopathy in the SUSTAIN-6 trial; the label flags it. (verified)
51. People with optic nerve disease or one good eye: EMA lists sudden optic nerve stroke (NAION) as a very rare side effect. (verified)

## Synergies (paired with)
52. Cagrilintide: Semaglutide works through the GLP-1 receptor, while cagrilintide adds the amylin signal for fullness and slower stomach emptying from the brainstem. The two hormones normally act together after meals. The pair has been tested together in obesity trials, where cagrilintide adds to semaglutide's weight effect.

## Where it comes from
53. Novo Nordisk's GLP-1 analogue: two amino acid swaps and a fatty-acid tail that rides albumin, stretching the two-minute natural hormone into a once-weekly drug. It began as a diabetes medicine; the weight loss became the headline.
54. First studied for: Type 2 diabetes (Ozempic/Rybelsus), then obesity (Wegovy).
55. Name story: The "-glutide" ending is the INN stem for GLP-1 analogues. Novo built it in 2012 as a longer-acting liraglutide. [S6] (verified)
56. 2017: Approved for type 2 diabetes as Ozempic
57. 2021: Approved for obesity as Wegovy
58. 2023: SELECT: fewer heart attacks and strokes in obesity
59. Dec 2025: Oral Wegovy pill approved for obesity (reported)
60. Today: The default weight-loss drug; huge compounded and grey-market use

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### What the cardiovascular trial actually measured [people]
61. This is the trial that changed the drug's standing, so it is worth knowing its shape rather than its headline. It enrolled 17,604 adults across 41 countries, aged 45 or older, with a body mass index of 27 or above, with established cardiovascular disease and no history of diabetes. They received 2.4 mg once weekly by injection or placebo, added on top of standard care, for up to five years [1] (verified).
62. The endpoint was a composite: first occurrence of cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack or non-fatal stroke. There were 1,270 first events across the trial, and the drug was associated with a statistically significant 20% reduction, with all three components of the composite contributing to the effect [1] (verified).
63. Two things follow that are often lost. First, a composite endpoint with all components moving in the same direction is a stronger result than a composite carried by one soft component, so this is a genuinely robust finding. Second, the comparison was against placebo on top of standard cardiovascular care, in people who already had heart disease. It is not a result about healthy people, and it was not designed to be.
64. The population also explains the most common misreading. This was a trial in people with obesity or overweight and established cardiovascular disease, deliberately excluding diabetes, because the question was whether the benefit existed outside diabetes. It did. That does not extend to people without cardiovascular disease, in whom the absolute benefit would be much smaller even if the relative effect were identical (inferred).

### The gap between the trials and how it is actually used [people]
65. The trial programme tested a specific thing: a stepped titration to a defined maintenance dose, taken continuously, in defined populations, with weight or cardiovascular events as the measured outcome. A large share of current use does not resemble that.
66. Sub-label dosing for appetite control, metabolic tidiness or longevity has no trial behind it. There is no study measuring outcomes at a fraction of the maintenance dose, no study of intermittent or every-few-weeks schedules, and no study in people without obesity or diabetes. The absence is not a hint that it does not work; it is simply unmeasured territory, and the honest label for it is unstudied rather than reported (verified as absent).
67. The long half-life, around a week, has a consequence that matters more at the edges of use than in the middle. Changes take weeks to express fully and weeks to wash out, so self-adjusting dose on the basis of how a given week felt is working with a signal that lags the input substantially (inferred). The trials sidestepped this by using fixed four-week titration steps.
68. The ceiling moved in March 2026, when a higher-dose 7.2 mg weekly version was approved for obesity in the US [2] (verified). Worth noting the direction of travel: the approved range has been extending upward on trial evidence, while a good deal of community practice has been moving downward on no evidence at all. Both trends are real and they are not in conversation with each other.

### Product reality: where this one differs from every other peptide here [people]
69. Unlike the repair peptides, an approved, characterised version of this molecule exists. That makes the grey-market and compounded supply a different kind of problem: not an unstudied substance, but a studied substance of uncertain identity, concentration and sterility, used without the labelling and titration support the evidence was generated with.
70. The documented failure mode is dosing error, and it is specific. Regulators described reports of patients receiving 5 to 20 times the intended dose from multi-dose vials; clinicians miscalculating when converting milligrams to units or millilitres, including one prescription written for 20 units instead of 2, which delivered ten times the intended dose to three patients; a patient who received no clear instructions from a telemedicine prescriber, searched online and took five times the intended dose; a clinician who recalculated his own dose and gave himself ten times the intended amount; and patients supplied with syringes far larger than the prescribed volume [3] (verified). Reported consequences included gastrointestinal effects, fainting, dehydration, headache, gallstones and acute pancreatitis, with some hospitalisations [3].
71. That list is worth reading closely because none of those events are pharmacology. They are unit-conversion and packaging failures, and they are the predictable result of separating a titrated microgram-scale injectable from the pen that doses it.
72. A 2025 analysis covering 2018 to 2024 linked compounded versions of this drug class to a higher rate of adverse effects than the non-compounded products, and it was cited in the US advisory debate on peptide compounding as a caution about pharmacy standards [4] (reported). Enforcement has followed the same line: warning letters went to online peptide vendors in August 2026, and to a compounder over its products in this class and its telehealth prescribing practices in September 2026 [5] (verified).
73. The practical reading for anyone comparing sources: with this molecule, the concentration stated on a vial and the volume in a syringe are where the risk lives, far more than the molecule itself.

### The generic wave, and why it changes the picture [people]
74. The patent position began unwinding in several large markets during 2026, and this is the development most likely to reshape grey-market demand.
75. In India, a generic launched around March 2026. In the US, a first tentative generic approval was granted in April 2026. In Canada, generics of the diabetes product were approved on 29 April 2026 and launched in mid-May, Apotex on 14 May and a second company two days later, both as pre-filled pens for type 2 diabetes in adults, making Canada the first G7 market with a generic version on pharmacy shelves. In China, a marketing application for a generic of the obesity product was accepted for review in February 2026. Health Canada was reviewing at least four further submissions [6] (verified).
76. Why an encyclopedia entry should care: the main driver of grey-market use of this molecule has been price and access, not the absence of an approved option. Characterised, pen-delivered generics at generic prices remove much of that rationale in the markets where they land. The dosing-error failure mode described above is almost entirely a property of vial-and-syringe supply, and it largely disappears with a pen (inferred).
77. The uneven part is that approvals so far are mostly for the diabetes indication and the diabetes dose range, not the obesity product, so access for weight management remains patchier than the headlines imply [6] (verified).

### Open questions [mixed]
78. What happens below the tested dose range? Sub-label dosing is now widespread and has never been measured for any outcome, including the appetite effect people are actually chasing.
79. Who benefits cardiovascularly outside the trial population? The 20% relative reduction was measured in people with established heart disease. The absolute benefit in lower-risk people is unknown and would necessarily be smaller.
80. What does very long-term use look like? The pivotal cardiovascular trial ran up to five years, which is long by trial standards and short relative to a treatment many people expect to continue indefinitely.
81. Does the muscle-loss question have a dose-dependent answer? It is well recognised as a risk and poorly quantified across the dose range, and it interacts with the sub-label dosing question above.
82. Will generic availability actually displace grey-market vials, or will price simply fall on both sides? The dosing-error evidence suggests the public-health answer hinges on delivery format rather than on the molecule.
83. For compounded and grey-market supply specifically: what is the real distribution of concentration accuracy? The adverse-event signal points at it, and unlike most questions on this page, it is answerable by testing rather than by trials.
