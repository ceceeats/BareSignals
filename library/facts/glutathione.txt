# Glutathione
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/glutathione
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: GSH, reduced glutathione, L-glutathione, gamma-L-glutamyl-L-cysteinylglycine, philothion (historic)
2. Origin: Made by the body, by enzymes
3. Sequence: ECG (3 amino acids). E is linked through its side chain (gamma bond), not the usual backbone
4. Shown with an FDA approved badge
5. Legal and approval status: NAC is approved; glutathione itself is compounded/supplement-grade, not an approved anti-aging drug.

## Summary
6. Glutathione is the body's main antioxidant, a tripeptide built by two enzymes and present in every cell. It is given IV or by injection to raise antioxidant capacity, support the liver and, popularly, lighten skin. Its precursor NAC is an approved drug, but skin-lightening trials are small and mixed, and oral glutathione is barely absorbed.
7. Punch line: The body's master antioxidant. Given IV or injected for skin brightening and liver support; oral barely absorbs.
8. Used for: Skin brightening, antioxidant support

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
9. Cellular protection: The body's main antioxidant

14. Cellular energy (backed only by animal or user reports): More energy reported

## How it works
15. Neutralises peroxides and free radicals · Glutathione peroxidase · role Cellular protection
   Glutathione peroxidase uses glutathione's cysteine thiol to donate electrons, turning hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides into water and harmless alcohols. This is a core antioxidant defence inside nearly every cell. It is textbook biochemistry, though whether glutathione given from outside adds to it in people is still open.
16. Tags toxins for removal · Glutathione S-transferase · role Organ function
   Glutathione S-transferases attach glutathione to toxins and drug breakdown products, making them water soluble so they leave in bile or urine. This conjugation is a main liver detox route. It is why its precursor NAC is the approved antidote for acetaminophen overdose.
17. Tilts melanin toward lighter pigment · Tyrosinase, pheomelanin · role Skin & aesthetics
   In melanocytes, glutathione pushes tyrosinase output away from dark eumelanin toward lighter pheomelanin. That is the basis for skin brightening claims, shown in cell work. Human trials are small and mixed, and any lightening seen faded once dosing stopped.
18. Recycles vitamins C and E · GSH/GSSG redox cycling · role Cellular protection
   After donating electrons, glutathione pairs up as oxidised GSSG, and glutathione reductase converts it back to active GSH. In that cycle it also regenerates vitamin C, which in turn helps restore vitamin E. This keeps the cell's wider antioxidant network running.
19. Main antioxidant pool in brain cells · Neuronal/astrocyte GSH · role Neural protection
   Neurons and astrocytes rely on glutathione as their main antioxidant pool, with astrocytes supplying building blocks to neurons. Low glutathione is found in the brain region that degenerates in Parkinson's disease. IV and nasal trials have not beaten placebo so far.
20. Supports T-cell growth and function · Lymphocyte redox control · role Immunity
   Lymphocytes need a well reduced redox balance to multiply, and glutathione sets that balance. In a six-month human trial, daily oral glutathione raised lymphocyte stores, and natural killer cell activity rose on the high dose as a secondary outcome. Disease outcomes were not measured.
21. Mechanism overview: Glutathione is a tripeptide of glutamate, cysteine and glycine, the glutamate linked by its side chain, so cells build it with two enzymes rather than ribosomes. Its cysteine thiol donates electrons. Glutathione peroxidase uses it to neutralise peroxides, and glutathione S-transferases attach it to toxins for excretion. It recycles vitamins C and E and supports T-cell proliferation. In melanocytes it pushes tyrosinase output toward lighter pheomelanin. It is the main antioxidant pool in neurons and astrocytes.

## Who shouldn't take it
46. People with asthma (inhaled form): Nebulised glutathione triggered bronchospasm. (reported)
47. People in chemotherapy or radiation: Antioxidant may shield tumour cells. (inferred)

## Synergies (paired with)
48. Vitamin C: Vitamin C and glutathione recycle each other in the cell's antioxidant network, and both are taken for skin brightening. People pair them to keep both antioxidants topped up. The pair has not been tested together in controlled trials.
49. NAC: NAC supplies cysteine, the slowest building block in the cell's own glutathione production, while glutathione adds the finished molecule from outside. People pair them to raise stores from two directions. NAC alone is the proven route, in acetaminophen overdose, and the pair has not been tested together.

## Where it comes from
50. The body's main antioxidant, a tripeptide whose glutamate is joined by its side chain, a link ribosomes cannot make, so two enzymes build it. Present in every cell.
51. First studied for: Given IV and by injection to raise antioxidant capacity, support the liver, and (popularly) lighten skin.
52. Name story: First spotted in yeast in 1888 and called "philothion", Greek for "love of sulfur". Hopkins renamed it in 1921 (glutamate plus thio, sulfur) believing it a two-amino-acid peptide; in 1929 he corrected himself and added glycine. [S7] (verified)
53. 1920s: Identified as a cellular tripeptide
54. 1970s to 1980s: Precursor NAC becomes the acetaminophen overdose antidote
55. Today: IV skin-brightening and antioxidant trend that outruns its data

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### The core problem: the body makes it inside cells, and it is hard to deliver there [people]
56. Glutathione is made inside nearly every cell from three amino acids, and it does its work there. Blood levels are low by comparison: about 6 micromoles per litre in plasma in one human study [1] (verified), against concentrations inside cells that are orders of magnitude higher. So the question for any supplement is not whether glutathione matters, which is settled, but whether glutathione given from outside reaches the inside of the cells that need it.
57. The gut and liver are built to break it down. They carry an enzyme, gamma-glutamyl transferase, that splits it into its component amino acids [1] (verified). The cells then rebuild glutathione from those parts, and the slowest step in that rebuild is usually the supply of cysteine. That is why the precursor N-acetylcysteine became the approved antidote for paracetamol overdose rather than glutathione itself (inferred from the mechanism; NAC's approval is on the card).

### Oral: a negative result, then a positive one, and how they fit [people]
58. In 1992 a Swiss group gave seven healthy volunteers a single dose of about 3 g by mouth and followed blood for four and a half hours. Plasma glutathione, cysteine and glutamate did not rise significantly, and the authors concluded that oral glutathione's systemic availability was negligible [1] (verified).
59. In 2015 a US group ran a six-month randomised placebo-controlled trial in 54 healthy non-smokers on 250 or 1,000 mg a day. At six months the high dose had raised glutathione in red cells, plasma and lymphocytes by about 30 to 35%, and in cheek cells by about 260%; the low dose raised whole-blood levels 17%. Levels returned to baseline within a month of stopping. Natural killer cell activity more than doubled on the high dose at three months, a secondary outcome measured in a subset [2] (verified).
60. The two are less contradictory than they look. One measured a single dose over hours; the other measured daily dosing over months and found the rise built slowly. The likely reading is that oral glutathione does very little acutely but can shift body stores with sustained use, possibly by supplying its building blocks (inferred). The 2015 trial used a branded ingredient whose maker publicised the result [2], which is worth knowing though it does not undermine a randomised design.

### Skin lightening: what the trials actually tested [people]
61. The pigment mechanism is plausible and comes from cell work: glutathione shifts melanin production from dark eumelanin toward lighter pheomelanin. The human evidence is thinner than the market for it. A 2018 review identified two oral or sublingual studies and one topical trial, which together showed a good safety profile and appreciable but reversible effects on skin tone [3] (verified).
62. For intravenous use, the route most associated with lightening, the same review found a single study, and described its design as dubious and its analysis as apparently flawed, which casts doubt on both the efficacy and the adverse effects it reported [3] (verified). The authors called parenteral use for lightening unjustified. Injectable glutathione's approved uses, where it has them, are in severe liver disorders and in preventing chemotherapy nerve damage, not in skin [3].
63. Reversibility is the practically important word. Every positive result faded once dosing stopped [2,3], so lightening, where it happens, depends on continued use (verified).

### The brain: a reasonable idea that has not cleared a placebo [people]
64. Parkinson's disease involves low glutathione in the brain region that degenerates, which made it an obvious candidate. An open-label 1996 study of nine people reported improvement; a 2009 randomised placebo-controlled pilot in about 20 people found the infusions safe and well tolerated, with a possible symptomatic benefit that did not separate clearly from placebo [4,5] (verified).
65. The intranasal route was then tried, because the nose offers a partial path to the brain. A phase 2b trial randomised 45 people to placebo or two doses three times daily for 12 weeks. The high dose improved scores from baseline, but neither dose beat placebo, because the placebo group improved too, and everyone worsened in the washout. One high-dose participant developed a fast heart rate and cardiomyopathy and withdrew, with causation unclear [6] (verified). The authors concluded the data did not show superiority over placebo.

### Safety: the risk is mostly in the vial, not the molecule [people]
66. Glutathione itself has a long record of tolerability by mouth and on the skin [2,3]. The serious harms in the record come from contaminated injectable product, and they have recurred.
67. In 2019 the FDA reported seven clinic patients who, within minutes of a 1,400 mg infusion of compounded glutathione, developed nausea, vomiting, chills, body aches and in one case low blood pressure and breathing difficulty requiring hospital care; a separate patient was admitted after a 2,400 mg dose. The agency's testing found bacterial endotoxin in every sample, some at five times the appropriate limit, and traced the source to powder labelled for dietary supplement use that had been repackaged for sterile compounding [7] (verified).
68. It happened again in 2026. In August the FDA reported at least 30 patients with fever, chills, pain, dizziness and sepsis-like symptoms after intravenous glutathione, some hospitalised, again traced to supplement-grade powder from a single lot, again consistent with endotoxin, with recalls by two pharmacies [8] (verified). In September a further compounder recalled glutathione and two other IV products shipped to patients' homes, after nine adverse event reports [9] (reported).
69. The pattern is the point. The molecule is cheap and widely sold as a supplement ingredient, the same powder can end up in an injectable, and nothing in a finished vial tells the person receiving it which grade was used. For injectable use, ingredient grade and endotoxin testing matter more than dose (inferred from the two episodes).
70. What has not been studied: long-term high-dose intravenous use, which is how lightening clinics use it, has no controlled safety data at all [3].

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
71. In the US, glutathione is sold legally as an oral dietary supplement. Injectable glutathione is not an FDA-approved drug; it is made by compounding pharmacies, and the agency has twice warned them, in 2019 and August 2026, not to use supplement-grade ingredient for injectables [7,8] (verified). Some countries approve injectable glutathione for liver disease and chemotherapy neurotoxicity; none approves it for skin lightening [3]. A Philippine health authority advisory against off-label IV use for lightening dates from 2011 [3].
72. In sport the substance is not prohibited, but the method can be. WADA prohibits intravenous infusions or injections of more than 100 mL in total per 12 hours outside hospital treatment, surgery or clinical investigation [10] (verified). A typical IV drip exceeds that; a small push may not.

### Open questions [mixed]
73. Does raising blood glutathione by 30% change any outcome that matters to health? The 2015 trial measured stores and immune markers, not disease.
74. Is IV lightening real, and at what cost? One flawed study is the entire controlled record.
75. Would a precursor do the same job more cheaply and safely? Plausible by mechanism, rarely tested head to head.
76. Why did placebo do so well in the Parkinson's trial? The authors flagged it as worth studying in its own right.
