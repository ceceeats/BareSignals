# TB-500 frag 17–23
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/tb-500-frag-17-23
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: TB-500 (strict sense), Ac-LKKTETQ, Tβ4 (17-23)
2. Origin: Fragment of a natural protein
3. Sequence: LKKTETQ (7 amino acids). Acetylated start (Ac-), not shown
4. Legal and approval status: Unapproved everywhere and banned in sport at all times. FDA 503A Category 2 in 2023, removed April 2026 after the nomination was withdrawn; the July 2026 advisory committee gave a favorable non-binding recommendation; no final rule found. WADA S2.3 (growth factors), prohibited at all times, no exemption. Australia/New Zealand treat it as a prescription medicine.

## Summary
5. TB-500 fragment 17-23 is a synthetic seven-amino-acid peptide copying the actin-binding region of thymosin beta-4, meant to carry its repair action in a smaller molecule. People use it for tendon, wound and soft-tissue recovery, often stacked with BPC-157. There is no human trial of the fragment, and many vials are actually full thymosin beta-4, so reports mix molecules; it is banned in sport at all times.
6. Punch line: The strict TB-500: seven-residue repair core of thymosin beta-4. Weekly shots, usually stacked with daily BPC-157.

## How it works
14. Holds actin so cells can crawl into wounds · G-actin binding · role Tissue repair
   The fragment carries the LKKTETQ motif that lets thymosin beta-4 bind single actin units (G-actin) and hold them in reserve. With that reserve, cells can rebuild their skeleton fast and crawl into a wound. This is shown for the full protein; the one direct cell test of the fragment found no significant wound closure.
15. Grows new vessels at the injury · Angiogenesis · role Tissue repair
   In the full thymosin beta-4 protein, this region promotes angiogenesis, the growth of new blood vessels into healing tissue. Better blood supply carries oxygen and repair cells to the injury. This is shown in animals with the full protein, not with the fragment.
16. Lowers inflammatory signals · TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 down · role Inflammatory signaling
   With full thymosin beta-4, animal studies show lower TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, the main inflammatory messengers at an injury. A calmer site can heal with less ongoing damage. Whether the seven-residue fragment does the same has not been tested.
17. Data mostly from the full 43-mer, assumed shared · Thymosin beta-4 · role Cellular protection
   Nearly all the evidence comes from the full 43 amino acid thymosin beta-4, and the fragment is assumed to share it. It lacks the other domains, including the end that releases anti-scarring Ac-SDKP, and regulators state the two are different substances. No human trial of the fragment exists.
18. Mechanism overview: The 17 to 23 fragment (LKKTETQ) is the actin-binding motif of thymosin beta-4. It binds monomeric G-actin and holds it in reserve, letting cells rapidly rebuild their cytoskeleton and crawl into a wound. Most data come from the full 43 amino acid Tβ4, where this region drives cell migration and new vessel growth, and TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 fall. The fragment is assumed to carry these repair effects, but it lacks the other domains, including the anti-scarring Ac-SDKP end.

## What has been tested
45. Tissue repair: Wound and tendon repair, full Tβ4 (animal)
46. Heart & vessels: Heart cell survival after injury (mouse)
47. Inflammatory signaling: Inflammatory cytokines (animal)
48. Cellular protection: Full Tβ4 safety, 40 volunteers IV (human)
49. Tissue repair: Rotator cuff, elbow, knee recovery (users)
50. Almost all mechanism and outcome data come from full-length thymosin beta-4, not the 7-mer, which is assumed to share them. In animals: G-actin binding that keeps cells mobile, faster cell migration into wounds, new vessel growth, lower TNF-alpha/IL-1beta/IL-6, and a heart-cell survival signal (Bock-Marquette 2004, Nature). Full-length human trials exist for safety (Ruff 2010, 40 volunteers IV, no dose-limiting toxicity) and for eye disease (RGN-259 drops). No controlled human trial of the TB-500 fragment itself exists.
51. What it helps: There is no human trial of the fragment. Animal work, mostly with full Tβ4, shows wound and tendon repair and heart cell survival. In forum accounts, 79% of clear outcomes were better, most often for rotator cuff, elbow, pec and knee injuries and post-surgery recovery. Many vials are actually full Tβ4, so reports mix molecules. Users often stack it with BPC-157.
52. Trial: none. The 17 to 23 fragment sold as TB-500 has never been tested in humans; trials used full thymosin beta-4. (checked 2026-10-23) [S8] (inferred)

## Who shouldn't take it
53. Drug-tested athletes: TB-500 is named on the WADA list as a thymosin beta-4 derivative. (verified)
54. People with active or recent cancer: The actin-binding 17-23 region is the part that drives cell migration, a step in tumour spread. (inferred)

## Where it comes from
66. A synthetic, acetylated 7-amino-acid peptide (Ac-LKKTETQ) copying the actin-binding region, positions 17-23, of thymosin beta-4, a 43-amino-acid protein made in most human cells. It first surfaced as a designer doping agent in racehorses; equine detection methods were published in 2012.
67. First studied for: Meant to carry thymosin beta-4's repair and anti-inflammatory action in a smaller, cheaper molecule, mainly for soft-tissue and tendon injury.
68. Name story: Strictly speaking this is the original TB-500: the doping literature uses that name for this fragment, not for full thymosin beta-4. 'TB-500' is a trade code, not a medical name. [S9] (verified)
69. 1980s to 1990s: Thymosin beta-4 mapped; its actin-binding stretch is positions 17 to 23
70. 2004: Full-length Tβ4 protects heart cells in mice (Nature)
71. 2012: Found in racehorses; equine doping tests published
72. 2023 to 2026: FDA Category 2, then removed; July 2026 panel favourable
73. Today: The weekly recovery shot, usually paired with BPC-157

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### What was actually nominated, and under which name [lab]
74. In 2026 US regulators evaluated this fragment for the compounding list, and the paperwork is instructive. The nominated product was a 3 mg per millilitre lyophilised powder for subcutaneous and intramuscular injection, for wound healing [1] (verified). Not tendon repair, not recovery, not injury. Wound healing is the single claim that was formally assessed.
75. The nomination described the active entity as the LKKTETQ site within the parent protein, and regulators evaluated two substances under the name: a free base at 889.01 g/mol under one CAS number, and an acetate salt at 949.1 under another [1]. The nomination's own stated alternate identifier matched neither of them [1] (verified). The named substance was the free base while the submitted certificate of analysis was for the acetate, listing the acetate's CAS number, formula and molecular weight.
76. Regulators noted that this is a common name rather than an approved generic name, and treated the inconsistent naming as a patient safety issue in itself: with no reference standard, there is nothing against which a batch can be checked [1] (verified). The doping literature's use of the name for this fragment, rather than for the full protein, is the older and more precise usage, but it is not a regulatory identity.

### What the certificates do and do not cover [lab]
77. For the free base, no certificate of analysis was submitted with the nomination. A publicly available one tested appearance, identity by mass spectrometry and HPLC, and peptide purity, and did not test assay, impurities, bacterial endotoxins or aggregates [1] (verified).
78. For the acetate, the certificate was more complete: appearance, solubility, amino acid composition, water content, acetate content, impurity limits of 2.0% total and 1.0% for the largest single impurity, and purity. It did not report identification, assay, aggregates or bacterial endotoxins [1] (verified).
79. The impurity limits are the quiet problem. A limit caps how much unidentified material is present without saying what it is. Regulators' concern is that unidentified peptide-related impurities and aggregates are a known driver of immune responses to injected peptides, and they judged injectable routes to carry significant immunogenicity risk on that basis, with no immunogenicity studies available for either form [1] (verified). Both were concluded to be not physically and chemically well characterised.
80. Storage is colder than most handling assumes: below minus 20 degrees Celsius for the free base, below minus 15 for the acetate [1].

### What a seven-residue fragment can and cannot inherit [cells]
81. The inheritance argument is that this stretch is the actin-binding motif, so it should carry the parent protein's repair effects. There is a specific reason to be careful with that reasoning, and it is not the one usually given.
82. The front end of this fragment carries an acetyl group. The wound-healing data most often invoked for it came from a 2003 study that used the non-acetylated seven-residue sequence, applied topically, in aged mice. Regulators' position is that this cannot be extrapolated to the acetylated injected form, because both the molecule and the route differ [1] (verified). So the foundational positive result is for a chemically different peptide delivered a different way.
83. That matters more than it sounds. Acetylation changes charge at the front end of a very short peptide, and in a seven-residue molecule the front end is a large fraction of the whole. It is not a cosmetic modification.
84. Regulators also stated plainly that this fragment and the 43-residue parent protein are not the same substance [1] (verified). The practical consequence runs in both directions: data from the parent cannot be assumed to transfer down, and a vial containing the parent under this name does not contain what the label says.

### The one direct test of the marketed molecule [cells]
85. This is the most important and least circulated finding about this compound.
86. In a 2024 in vitro fibroblast scratch assay, the free base at 50 micrograms per millilitre did not significantly promote wound closure. A metabolite, the N-acetylated five-residue fragment, showed a small but statistically significant effect at the same concentration [1] (verified, cells).
87. A single negative assay does not settle a question. But this is the only direct test of the marketed fragment against its own nominated indication, it used a high concentration, and it did not support the claim. The accompanying observation that a shorter breakdown product did something is more interesting than the negative itself: if activity sits with a metabolite rather than the administered peptide, then the whole logic of what to give and when changes (inferred).
88. Set against that, there is no cell or animal study showing the acetylated fragment reproducing the parent protein's wound, cornea or heart results. The assumption of shared activity has not been tested and, where tested at all, was not confirmed.

### What the body does with it [animals]
89. Pharmacokinetics amount to one study in a species nobody is dosing. Horses given 10 mg subcutaneously reached plasma peaks of 0.05 and 0.08 nanograms per millilitre [1] (verified). There is no intramuscular pharmacokinetic data for either form, and none in humans by any route [1].
90. Those plasma figures are strikingly low for a 10 mg dose, which is consistent with a short peptide being cleared and broken down fast. It also means there is no exposure number from any species to anchor a human expectation to, and nothing to connect a given amount injected to a concentration at a tendon.
91. The detection side is better developed than the therapeutic side, because the compound's first scientific attention came from drug testing rather than from medicine. That asymmetry is worth noticing: the methods for proving someone used it are more mature than the methods for showing it works.

### Safety: unopened rather than open [lab]
92. No acute toxicity studies, no repeat-dose studies, no genotoxicity studies, no developmental or reproductive studies and no carcinogenicity studies were submitted or identified for either form [1] (verified).
93. So the standard questions have not been asked: what dose causes harm, which organ is affected first, what happens over months, whether it damages DNA, whether it affects pregnancy, whether it causes tumours. Regulators noted the absence of carcinogenicity data without drawing a conclusion from it [1].
94. Human surveillance is similarly empty. The adverse event database returned nothing for this substance. A separate complaint system returned two cases involving a blended product combining it with another repair peptide, with no safety assessment attached, and causality could not be assigned to either component [1] (verified). That is a direct cost of blending, not a finding about this molecule.
95. The commonly repeated claim of an excellent safety profile with no reported side effects is, against this record, a statement about absent studies and absent surveillance. The reported pattern of headache and flu-like fatigue in community use has no counterpart anywhere in the formal literature and no mechanism has been investigated for it (reported).

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
96. Regulators proposed not adding either form to the 503A compounding bulks list, citing poor characterisation, no history of use in compounding, no evidence of effectiveness for wound healing, no human safety data, immunogenicity risk, and the existence of approved wound therapies [1] (verified).
97. The advisory committee recommended adding it anyway, voting 8 to 6 with one abstention on 23 July 2026, for wound healing [2,3]. Agency staff had recommended against all seven peptides considered that week.
98. That recommendation is advisory. Addition requires formal notice-and-comment rulemaking, which has not begun and carries no deadline [2] (verified). An "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" went to the White House budget office on 14 October 2026 and was unpublished as of mid-October, with its covered substances and conditions not public [2]. Removal from the do-not-compound category in April 2026 followed withdrawn nominations rather than a revised safety finding, and left the substance outside any recognised category rather than cleared [4] (verified).

### Open questions [mixed]
99. Does the acetylated fragment do anything at all? The one direct test against its own nominated indication was negative, and the positive wound data belongs to a chemically different peptide applied topically. This is the field's central unexamined assumption, and it is testable cheaply.
100. If activity sits with a metabolite, what follows? The 2024 assay's only positive result was for a breakdown product, which would change the logic of what is worth administering.
101. Which molecule is in the vial? Regulators state the fragment and the parent protein are different substances, and no routine identity test in grey-market supply distinguishes them, so pooled user reports describe a mixture of molecules.
102. What exposure does any dose produce in a human? One horse study is the entire pharmacokinetic record.
103. Everything about toxicology. There is none, in any species, by any route, which makes long-term safety an unopened question rather than an open one.
104. Why the divergence between forum reports and app-logged favourability? Both populations are self-selected, and the gap may be as much about which molecule people actually received as about effect.
