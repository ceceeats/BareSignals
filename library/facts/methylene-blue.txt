# Methylene blue
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/methylene-blue
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: methylthioninium mb, methylthioninium chloride, ProvayBlue, Basic Blue 9, C.I. 52015, Urolene Blue
2. Origin: Not a peptide
3. Legal and approval status: Approved (methemoglobinemia); low-dose wellness use is off-label.

## Summary
4. Methylene blue is a synthetic dye from 1876 and one of the first fully synthetic drugs; it is not a peptide. It is approved as the antidote for methemoglobinemia, and low-dose off-label users take it for focus and energy, based on its role shuttling electrons in mitochondria. Evidence for cognitive and longevity use is thin, and high doses are toxic and dangerous alongside serotonergic drugs.
5. Punch line: The 1876 blue dye and approved blood antidote. Low oral doses for focus and mitochondrial energy; stains urine.
6. Used for: Focus and mitochondrial energy

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Cellular protection: Antidote for blocked blood oxygen
8. Cognition: Small studies on memory
9. Cellular energy: Passes electrons in mitochondria

## How it works
10. Restores blood's oxygen carrying · Methemoglobin reduction · role Cellular protection
   In red cells, an NADPH reductase converts methylene blue to its reduced form, leucomethylene blue. That form reduces methemoglobin back to working haemoglobin, which can carry oxygen again. This is its approved medical use, as the antidote for methemoglobinemia in people.
11. Shuttles electrons in mitochondria · Electron transport chain · role Cellular energy
   Methylene blue can take electrons from NADH and pass them straight to cytochrome c, skipping blocked steps of the electron transport chain. In theory that keeps ATP production going when mitochondria are damaged. This energy claim rests mostly on cell data, with only small human studies.
12. Blocks serotonin breakdown · MAO-A inhibition · role Cognition
   Methylene blue is a potent inhibitor of MAO-A, the enzyme that breaks down serotonin. Less breakdown means more serotonin, which may explain small mood findings in human studies. It also makes the dye dangerous with serotonergic drugs such as SSRIs, due to the risk of serotonin syndrome.
13. Cuts nitric oxide signalling · NOS, guanylate cyclase · role Cellular protection
   It blocks nitric oxide synthase and guanylate cyclase, the enzymes that make and respond to nitric oxide. Nitric oxide relaxes blood vessels, so blocking it raises blood pressure. That is why it is used in some cases of shock, and why high doses carry real toxicity.
14. Mechanism overview: Methylene blue is a redox dye that cycles between oxidised and reduced forms. In red cells, NADPH reductase converts it to leucomethylene blue, which reduces methemoglobin back to working haemoglobin and restores oxygen transport. In mitochondria it can take electrons from NADH and pass them to cytochrome c, bypassing blocked complexes, which is the basis of the energy claims. It is also a potent MAO-A inhibitor, raising serotonin, and it inhibits nitric oxide synthase and guanylate cyclase, which is why it raises blood pressure in shock.

36. What it helps: Its proven use is as the antidote for methemoglobinemia. Small low-dose human studies hint at better memory and attention, and some at mood support. Users take low oral doses for focus and energy, and note blue urine. Mitochondrial and longevity claims rest on cell data. High doses are toxic, and the MAO-A effect makes it dangerous with serotonergic drugs.
37. Trial: active · Phase 1 · cytokine storm and neurotoxicity after CAR T immunotherapy · NCT07169487 · Institute of Hematology & Blood Diseases Hospital, China. ## Who shouldn't take it
38. People on SSRIs, SNRIs or MAOIs: Strong MAO-A inhibitor; boxed warning for serotonin syndrome. (verified)
39. People with G6PD deficiency: Causes haemolysis; contraindicated on the label. (verified)
40. People relying on a pulse oximeter: The blue dye absorbs the oximeter light, so oxygen readings show falsely low. (verified)
41. Breastfeeding parents: Label says stop breastfeeding for 8 days after a dose. (verified)

## Where it comes from
42. A synthetic dye from 1876, the first fully synthetic drug ever used in medicine, later found to shuttle electrons in mitochondria. Not a peptide.
43. First studied for: A medical dye and antidote (for methemoglobinemia); malaria historically.
44. Name story: Made in 1876 by Heinrich Caro at BASF as a cotton dye. Paul Ehrlich then noticed it stained malaria parasites and used it as a treatment, the first time an infectious disease was cured with a synthetic substance. [S8] (verified)
45. 1876: Synthetic dye; the first fully synthetic drug used in medicine
46. 1890s: Used against malaria
47. 20th century: Became the antidote for methemoglobinemia
48. Today: Approved antidote; low-dose use for focus and energy is off-label
