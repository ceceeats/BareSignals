# Tesofensine
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/tesofensine
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: NS2330, NS-2330, Teso
2. Origin: Not a peptide: small molecule
3. Legal and approval status: Not FDA or EMA approved. Mexico: COFEPRIS committee favoured it Feb 2023, refused Nov 2024, Medix resubmitted Feb 2025; no approval found as of Oct 2026. Not named on the WADA list (may fall under S6 stimulants, inferred).

## Summary
4. Tesofensine is a Danish triple reuptake inhibitor (serotonin, noradrenaline, dopamine) from NeuroSearch, not a peptide. Originally aimed at Parkinson's and Alzheimer's, it was dropped for weak efficacy but caused weight loss, so it became an appetite-suppressant obesity drug. A 2008 Phase 2 showed about 11 to 13 kg loss in 24 weeks. Insomnia, dry mouth, faster pulse and anxiety recur, and weight returns after stopping.
5. Punch line: Failed Alzheimer's drug turned appetite killer. Oral triple reuptake inhibitor; insomnia and racing heart common.
6. Used for: Appetite and weight loss

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Appetite & satiety: Strong appetite suppression
8. Fat metabolism: About 10% weight loss
9. Drive & mental stamina: Dopamine lift, more focus
10. Cognition: Tested in Alzheimer's, failed
11. Sleep & rhythms (backed only by animal or user reports): Insomnia common
12. Heart & vessels (backed only by animal or user reports): Raises heart rate
13. Mood (backed only by animal or user reports): Anxiety and mood shifts

## How it works
14. Keeps noradrenaline, serotonin and dopamine around longer · NET, SERT, DAT reuptake inhibition · role Appetite & satiety
   Tesofensine blocks the reuptake transporters that clear noradrenaline, serotonin and dopamine from the synapse, most strongly the noradrenaline one. All three signals last longer. This brain-wide boost is the root of both its appetite effect and its side effects, from insomnia to a faster pulse.
15. Quiets hunger circuits · Lateral hypothalamus GABA neurons · role Appetite & satiety
   A 2024 animal study linked its appetite effect to silencing GABA neurons in the lateral hypothalamus, cells that drive feeding. With them quieter, animals ate less. In the human Phase 2 trial this showed up as large weight loss over 24 weeks.
16. Nudges energy burn and fat oxidation · Sympathetic tone, night-time EE · role Fat metabolism
   More noradrenaline raises sympathetic tone, the body's activating nervous system. That nudges up energy expenditure, notably at night, and fat oxidation. This adds to the appetite effect but is a smaller part of the weight loss.
17. Dopamine lift for focus and drive · DAT inhibition · role Drive & mental stamina
   Blocking the dopamine transporter lets dopamine linger in reward and focus circuits. Users report more focus and drive. How strong this is remains unclear, since two published potency datasets disagree on the dopamine transporter.
18. Raises heart rate · Noradrenergic effect · role Heart & vessels
   The same noradrenaline push speeds the heart and raises blood pressure. In human trials heart rate rose noticeably. The developer built Tesomet, a combination with the beta-blocker metoprolol, specifically to blunt this.
19. Mechanism overview: Tesofensine blocks the reuptake transporters for noradrenaline (NET), serotonin (SERT) and dopamine (DAT), strongest at NET, so all three stay longer in the synapse. In the hypothalamus this quiets hunger, and a 2024 study tied it to silencing GABA neurons in the lateral hypothalamus that drive feeding. Higher noradrenaline also nudges energy expenditure and fat oxidation, and the dopamine lift explains the focus and drive users report. The same noradrenergic push raises heart rate and blood pressure, the reason a combination with the beta-blocker metoprolol (Tesomet) was built.

## Who shouldn't take it
50. People with hypertension or heart disease: Raises heart rate and blood pressure in trials. (reported)
51. People on SSRIs, SNRIs or MAOIs: Triple reuptake inhibitor; serotonin syndrome risk. (inferred)
52. People with bipolar disorder or anxiety: Dopamine and noradrenaline lift may trigger mania. (inferred)

## Where it comes from
53. A Danish triple reuptake inhibitor (serotonin, noradrenaline, dopamine) from NeuroSearch, first aimed at Parkinson's and Alzheimer's; patients lost weight, so it became an obesity pill.
54. First studied for: Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease, dropped for weak efficacy.
55. Name story: Born at Danish NeuroSearch as code NS2330 and given the INN tesofensine; the name has no published story. Rights later passed to Saniona, which licensed Mexico to Medix. [S5] (reported)

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### It was never meant to be a diet drug [people]
62. Tesofensine began as NS2330, a Danish candidate for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. Development for both was dropped for lack of efficacy [1] (verified). What got noticed in those trials was an adverse event: participants, especially heavier ones, kept losing weight. The obesity programme exists because of a side effect in a failed neurology drug [1].
63. That history explains the pharmacology that follows. This is a triple monoamine reuptake inhibitor built for the brain, not a gut hormone analogue, and it sits in the same pharmacological family as the appetite suppressants that were withdrawn over cardiovascular and psychiatric effects. Nothing about its chemistry is peptide-like; it belongs in this library only because it is traded alongside peptides (inferred).
64. Potency figures depend on which dataset is quoted, which is itself worth knowing. One set of inhibition values gives noradrenaline transporter 3.2 nanomolar, dopamine transporter 8.0 and serotonin transporter 11.0; a revised set gives noradrenaline 1.7, serotonin 11 and dopamine 65 [1] (verified as both reported). Those two readings tell different stories about how dopaminergic the drug is, and the difference matters for what to expect of its drive and abuse profile.

### The famous trial, and the formal cloud over it [people]
65. The 2008 phase 2 trial is the source of nearly every number quoted for this compound. Over 24 weeks, with all participants on a 300 kcal deficit diet and rising exercise, mean weight loss was 6.7 kg at 0.25 mg, 11.3 kg at 0.5 mg and 12.8 kg at 1 mg, against 2.2 kg on placebo [1] (verified). A 48-week open-label extension re-enrolled 140 completers and reported total mean loss of 13 to 14 kg in those previously on 0.5 mg [1].
66. In 2013 the journal that published it issued an expression of concern, and the detail matters more than the label. The Danish Health and Medicines Authority inspected the trial in November 2011 as part of a random selection of completed studies. At one of five sites, informed consent, which Danish rules require a medical practitioner to take, had been delegated to non-medical staff. Blinding integrity had been questioned in an earlier 2007 inspection and rebutted by the investigators. And the contract research organisation had recorded adverse events incompletely: recurring conditions such as headache, migraine, stress and depression were not reliably captured in participants who had them before enrolling [2] (verified).
67. The authority's conclusion is the sentence to carry away: the overall side-effect profile as published was not in accordance with the actual course of the trial [2] (verified). The endpoint data and serious adverse events were found to match the source records [2]. The corresponding author himself raised the concerns with the journal, agreed that under-reporting of some adverse events was the relevant finding, and drafted an erratum [2] (verified).
68. So the efficacy numbers above are probably sound, and the tolerability numbers from that trial are specifically the ones a regulator said were understated. Anyone quoting the 12.8 kg figure without that qualification is quoting half a finding.

### The pharmacokinetics are the real practical problem [people]
69. Half-life is about 220 hours, roughly nine days, with 90% oral bioavailability, hepatic metabolism mainly via CYP3A4 and renal clearance of about 15 to 20%. The main metabolite has a half-life of about 374 hours, roughly sixteen days, reaching 31 to 34% of parent exposure at steady state while contributing about 6% of the activity [1] (verified).
70. A nine-day half-life has consequences that a daily tablet hides. Steady state takes weeks, so what someone feels in the first days is not what the drug will do at plateau. A dose change takes a month or more to express itself. If an adverse effect appears, stopping does not clear it quickly; exposure decays over weeks. And any interaction that affects CYP3A4 acts on a compound that accumulates (inferred).
71. That profile is a poor match for a self-adjusted regimen of any kind, because the feedback loop between a change and its effect is longer than most people's attention to it (inferred).

### The cardiovascular arm, and the drug built to cancel it [people]
72. At 0.25 and 0.5 mg, blood pressure rose 1 to 3 mmHg and heart rate rose by up to 8 beats per minute [1] (verified). Discontinuation for adverse events in the obesity trials was 13% against 6% on placebo, with dry mouth and insomnia dose-dependent alongside headache, nausea, diarrhoea and constipation [1].
73. The clearest evidence that the developers took the cardiovascular effect seriously is that they built a second product around it: Tesomet, a fixed combination of tesofensine with the beta-blocker metoprolol, specifically intended to blunt the heart-related effects [3] (verified). A drug that needs a beta-blocker co-formulated into it is a drug with a known cardiovascular signal, by its own sponsor's design.
74. That programme ran in hypothalamic obesity and Prader-Willi syndrome, obtained orphan drug designation in 2021, and was paused in April 2022. The company stated the pause was entirely due to funding limitations and not related to safety or efficacy, alongside a restructuring that cut about 30% of staff [3] (verified, as the company's own account).
75. The psychiatric side has never been properly characterised, and the 2013 finding is the reason. Depression and stress were among the pre-existing conditions the inspectors found were not reliably recorded [2]. For a drug that raises dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin availability, mood and sleep effects are the expected risks, and the main trial's record of them was held to be incomplete (inferred).

### The regulatory record, which is a refusal and not a pending formality [people]
76. A Mexican phase 3 trial run by the licensee enrolled 372 patients and reported about 10% mean weight loss over 24 weeks, with more than half of participants losing over 10%, against a safety database of roughly 1,600 patients [4] (verified as the sponsor's account).
77. In November 2024 the regulator did not approve it. The sponsor's own statement said it appeared the decision had not been based on the full data package as submitted, and that the partner was in dialogue with the agency about a path forward [4] (verified). A resubmission followed in February 2025, and no approval has been found as of October 2026 (verified as of this review; the agency's own register could not be opened directly).
78. No approval exists in the US or EU, and no filing has been reported there. That is twenty-five years from first-in-human work to no approved product in any major market, across two therapeutic areas and two corporate owners (inferred).

### Where it sits in sport, and open questions [mixed]
79. Unlike most entries in this library, this one is named explicitly on the 2026 Prohibited List. It appears under non-specified stimulants, which is prohibited in competition rather than at all times [5] (verified). The non-specified classification matters: it removes the reduced-sanction route available for specified substances. A nine-day half-life also means in-competition prohibition is effectively a long out-of-competition constraint, since a dose taken weeks earlier is still present (inferred).
80. What is the real adverse event profile at 0.5 mg? The dataset a regulator said understated it is still the main published source.
81. Does the mood and sleep risk scale with duration? Insomnia was dose-dependent over 24 weeks, and nobody has characterised a longer course with complete psychiatric reporting.
82. Is it dopaminergic enough to matter? The two published transporter datasets disagree eightfold on the dopamine transporter, which is exactly the parameter that governs reinforcement.
83. Why has it failed to reach approval anywhere? The refusal in the one market that reviewed it has not been explained publicly beyond the sponsor's account, which is the weakest kind of source for a regulator's reasoning.
84. What happens on stopping, given the kinetics? Weight regain after discontinuation has not been characterised for this compound, and the slow washout makes the question unusual rather than routine.
