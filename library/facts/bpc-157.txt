# BPC-157
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/bpc-157
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: bepecin, PL 14736, PL-10, PLD-116, Body Protection Compound 157
2. Origin: Fragment of a natural protein
3. Sequence: GEPPPGKPADDAGLV (15 amino acids)
4. Legal and approval status: Unapproved everywhere. FDA placed it in 503A Category 2 in 2023, then moved it to 'previously in Category 2' by April 2026 after the nomination was withdrawn; staff proposed not adding it; the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8-6 in July 2026 to recommend adding it, with no proposed rule as of early October 2026. WADA lists it S0, banned in and out of competition.

## Summary
5. BPC-157 is a 15-amino-acid chain based on a protective fragment of human gastric juice, now treated as a stable synthetic peptide. People use it for tendon, ligament and gut repair. Animal work is strong, human data are small, and it is unapproved and WADA-banned; users report help with elbow, shoulder and knee pain, often paired with TB-500.
6. Punch line: The Wolverine peptide. Most used for tendon, joint and gut healing, injected near the injury, often with TB-500.

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Tissue repair: Faster tendon and ligament healing in rats
8. Digestive & gut: Protects the stomach and gut lining
9. Inflammatory signaling: Calms injury inflammation
10. Pain & sensory: Less joint and tendon pain reported
11. Heart & vessels (backed only by animal or user reports): Grows new blood vessels
12. Neural protection (backed only by animal or user reports): Nerve protection in rats
13. Bone & cartilage (backed only by animal or user reports): Bone and tendon-to-bone repair in rats

## How it works
14. Grows new blood vessels into injured tissue · VEGFR2, Akt, eNOS · role Tissue repair
   In rat models BPC-157 raises VEGFR2, a receptor for blood vessel growth signals, and activates Akt and eNOS downstream. Cell and rat studies show more vessel formation and faster blood flow recovery, without more VEGF itself. No receptor for BPC-157 has been identified.
15. Moves repair cells into the wound · FAK, paxillin · role Tissue repair
   BPC-157 activates FAK and paxillin, proteins that anchor cells and steer their movement. Fibroblasts, the cells that lay down new tissue, then migrate into the injury faster. This comes from rat and cell work, mostly from one research group.
16. Makes tendon cells more responsive to growth hormone · GH receptor up · role Tissue repair
   In fibroblasts from rat Achilles tendon, BPC-157 raised growth hormone receptor levels. Treated cells then grew more and activated JAK2 when growth hormone was added. It does not act like growth hormone; it makes tendon cells respond more to what is present. Shown only in rat cells.
17. Shields gut lining from NSAID damage, in rats · Gastric cytoprotection · role Digestive & gut
   BPC-157 protects the stomach and gut lining from damage caused by NSAIDs, a property called gastric cytoprotection. In rats this means fewer lesions and better healing of colitis and surgical gut joins. Human gut data are limited to a small enema trial in ulcerative colitis.
18. Lowers inflammatory signals in injury models · TNF-α, IL-6 down · role Inflammatory signaling
   In injury models BPC-157 lowers the inflammatory messengers TNF-α and IL-6. Less of these signals means a calmer injury site during repair. This is shown in animals, but the studies lack clear dose-response relationships, which weakens the mechanism case.
19. Steadies nitric oxide signalling · NO system · role Heart & vessels
   In isolated rat arteries and human endothelial cells, BPC-157 activated Src and eNOS and freed eNOS from caveolin-1, raising nitric oxide output. The vessels relaxed, mostly through their lining. This is shown in cells and isolated tissue, not in people.
20. Mechanism overview: BPC-157 is a stable 15 amino acid peptide from a fragment of a gastric juice protein. No receptor has been identified, and most mechanism data come from one Zagreb group. In rat models it upregulates VEGFR2 and activates Akt and eNOS, driving new vessel growth into injured tendon, ligament and muscle. It activates FAK and paxillin, helping fibroblasts migrate into the wound, and increases growth hormone receptors on tendon cells, making them more responsive to GH. It also steadies nitric oxide signalling, lowers TNF-α and IL-6, and protects the gut lining from NSAID damage.

## What has been tested
46. Tissue repair: Achilles and ligament tears (rat)
47. Muscle & performance: Crushed and torn muscle (rat)
48. Digestive & gut: Colitis and gut joins after surgery (rat)
49. Digestive & gut: Ulcerative colitis, enema (trial)
50. Pain & sensory: Knee pain, injected into the joint (human)
51. Organ function: Bladder pain, 12 women (human)
52. Animal studies are extensive and almost all from one group: rat Achilles and medial-collateral-ligament tears, muscle crush, colitis, and gut-join (anastomosis) healing. Human data are thin. PL 14736, the enema form, ran a Phase I in 32 healthy volunteers and a Phase II in 53 people with ulcerative colitis (published only as a 2005 abstract). An oral Phase I was cancelled in 2016. A small retrospective knee series (Lee 2021, ~12 people, 4 mg into the joint) reported 11 of 12 improved. A bladder pilot in 12 women and a 2-person IV pilot reported no adverse events. A hamstring Phase 2 (NCT07437547, SC daily 14 days vs placebo) is recruiting.
53. What it helps: Animal work is strong: faster Achilles and ligament healing, muscle repair and gut lining protection in rats. Human data are small: 11 of 12 knee pain patients improved after joint injection, and an enema form was trialled in ulcerative colitis. User reports centre on elbow, shoulder and knee tendon pain and recovery after surgery, often paired with TB-500. Oral use for gut symptoms gives split results.
54. Trial: active · Phase 1 · recovery after rotator cuff surgery · NCT07803250 · University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences. First US placebo controlled BPC-157 trial, not yet recruiting; the only earlier registered trial (2015) was cancelled. (checked 2026-10-23) [S7] (reported)

## Who shouldn't take it
55. People with active or recent cancer: Raises VEGFR2 signalling and new vessel growth in animal work; tumours use the same pathway. (inferred)
56. Drug-tested athletes: Banned since 2022 under S0 (non-approved substances); positives have led to bans. (verified)
57. People with proliferative retinopathy or vascular malformations: Pro-angiogenic peptides could feed abnormal vessel growth. (inferred)
58. People with known peptide or injection allergies: FDA reviewers flagged immunogenicity and impurity risk; no human safety dataset exists. (reported)

## Synergies (paired with)
59. TB-500 frag 17–23: BPC-157 drives vessel growth and growth hormone sensitivity at the injury, while the TB-500 fragment is meant to help repair cells crawl in through actin binding. People stack them for tendon and soft tissue injuries, but the fragment's own data are thin and the pair has not been tested together.
60. GHK-Cu: BPC-157 brings blood supply and repair cell movement, while GHK-Cu delivers copper for collagen cross-linking and remodels scar matrix. They are sold pre-mixed as GHK-Cu + BPC-157 and inside the GLOW and KLOW blends, but the pairing is user logic, not trial data.
61. KPV: BPC-157 protects and repairs the gut lining, while KPV calms inflammation inside gut and immune cells by blocking NF-κB. People pair them for colitis and gut flares, and they come pre-mixed in KLOW and Tβ4 + BPC + KPV. The pair has not been tested together.
62. NAD+: BPC-157 acts on local tissue repair, while NAD+ supplies the coenzyme that cell energy and DNA repair enzymes run on. People add NAD+ as a general recovery and energy layer alongside an injury protocol. The pairing is user logic, not trial data.
63. Somatropin (HGH): BPC-157 raises growth hormone receptors on tendon cells in rat work, and somatropin supplies the growth hormone itself, which drives IGF-1 and collagen synthesis. The logic is a more receptive tissue meeting more signal. The pair has not been tested together.
64. MOTS-c: BPC-157 targets the injured tissue, while MOTS-c works on whole-body metabolism through AMPK, the cell's energy sensor, in mice. People combine them for recovery alongside training and fat loss goals. The pairing is user logic, not trial data.
65. Ipamorelin: Ipamorelin triggers growth hormone pulses through the ghrelin receptor, while BPC-157 makes tendon cells more responsive to growth hormone in rat cells. People pair them for injury recovery and sleep. The pair itself has not been tested together.
66. Retatrutide: These serve different goals: retatrutide drives weight loss through GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors, while BPC-157 is used for tendon and gut repair. People on a weight protocol sometimes run BPC-157 for an injury or gut complaint at the same time. There is no data on the combination.
67. ARA-290: ARA-290 acts on the innate repair receptor to calm nerve inflammation and regrow small nerve fibres in small trials, while BPC-157 supports blood supply and repair in injured tissue. People pair them for injuries with a nerve pain side. The pair has not been tested together.
68. Cartalax: Cartalax is a short peptide claimed by the Khavinson group to tune connective tissue genes in cartilage, while BPC-157 brings vessel growth and repair cell movement. They are sold pre-mixed as Cartalax + Tβ4 + BPC-157 for joints, but the pairing is user logic, not trial data.
69. PEA: PEA is usually added to BPC-157 for pain and inflammation, while BPC-157 works on tissue repair itself. This library holds no data on PEA yet, so the logic is cautious, and the pair has not been tested together.
70. Thymosin Beta-4: BPC-157 works on growth signalling and blood flow at the injury, while Tβ4 moves repair cells in and supplies the actin they rebuild with. People stack them for tendon and muscle injuries and they are sold pre-mixed as BPC-157 + TB-500 and in several wider blends, but the pair has not been tested together in people.

## Where it comes from
71. BPC stands for Body Protection Compound. A Zagreb team led by Predrag Sikiric reported the 15-amino-acid chain in the early 1990s, isolated from a protective fragment of human gastric juice. The parent protein it is cut from has never been published, so BPC-157 is treated as a stable synthetic peptide rather than a known natural one.
72. First studied for: Studied first for the gut: protecting the stomach lining and healing ulcers, which is where the stomach-juice origin and the name come from. The tendon, ligament and muscle uses came later, almost entirely from rat work.
73. Name story: BPC means Body Protection Compound. The '157' is never explained; it is not the residue count, which is 15. Gym slang calls it the 'Wolverine' peptide. [S8] (verified)
74. Early 1990s: Found in human stomach juice in Zagreb; studied for ulcers
75. 2000s to 2010s: Rat work spreads to tendon, ligament, muscle and gut healing
76. 2005: Enema form tested in ulcerative colitis, Phase II
77. 2016: Oral Phase I cancelled without results
78. Today: The best-known recovery peptide; FDA panel backed compounding in 2026

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### Where it came from, and what is still missing from that story [lab]
79. The compound has been described in the published literature since 1993, out of a single laboratory in Zagreb [1,2]. The part of the origin story that never arrived is the parent protein. A fragment is, by definition, cut from something larger, but the protein it was supposedly cut from has never been identified in print. That means the usual chain of evidence for a natural peptide, find the protein, find the cleavage, show the body makes the fragment on purpose, was never completed (verified). In practice it is treated as a synthetic 15-residue peptide whose sequence happens to have been written down in 1993.
80. The naming problem is not cosmetic. BPC-157 is a common name, not an approved generic name, and the FDA flagged inconsistent naming as a safety risk in its own right, because without a reference standard there is nothing to check a batch against [2]. There are also two different substances sharing the name: the free base (CAS 137525-51-0) and the acetate salt (CAS 216441-37-1). When two compounding pharmacies nominated it for the US compounding list, they themselves were inconsistent about which of the two they meant, so the agency ended up evaluating both separately [2] (verified).
81. Earlier development ran under other names, PL-10, PLD-116, PL 14736 and Bepecin, through a Croatian pharmaceutical programme [1] (verified). Those names matter when reading old trial records, because the human work was done on a rectal formulation under those codes, not on anything resembling what circulates now.

### What is actually in a vial, as far as anyone has documented [lab]
82. This is the least discussed and most practically important part of the record. Reviewing the certificates of analysis submitted with the US nominations, the FDA found: no certificate at all for the free base; for the acetate, one certificate reporting 97.8% purity with related substances at 1.1% maximum individual and 2.2% total, and a second reporting only a purity figure of 98.34% [2]. In both cases the impurities were not identified, only counted. Neither certificate included endotoxin testing, bioburden, residual solvent or aggregate testing [2] (verified).
83. Those gaps are the reason the agency concluded the substance is not well characterised, and they feed directly into its immunogenicity concern. Solid-phase peptide synthesis can leave peptide-related impurities and aggregates behind, and aggregates are a known trigger for immune responses to peptide drugs. No immunogenicity studies of this compound exist, so the risk could not be ruled out either way [2] (verified). For a 15-residue peptide given by injection or nasal spray, the agency considered that risk significant rather than theoretical.
84. Stability is tighter than most handling assumes. The lyophilised powder is recommended for storage below minus 18 degrees Celsius, and once reconstituted the solution is documented as stable for only two to three weeks at refrigerator temperature [2] (verified). Nothing in the record supports the longer shelf lives often quoted.
85. Independent batch testing exists outside the official record and is worth reading for what it shows about spread rather than for any single verdict. One public testing service tracks 149 vendors for this compound; its default standard scores purity, dose accuracy and label quality together, and a representative vendor record shows 11 tests with 7 passing and 4 failing, including one result with a fill-volume deviation of +30% [3] (reported). The same service offers endotoxin testing but does not publish those results, so sterility and endotoxin remain the least visible part of product quality.

### Mechanism, one step at a time, with the model each step came from [mixed]
86. No receptor has been identified, which shapes everything below. What exists is a set of signalling observations, mostly from two groups, each tied to a specific preparation.
87. Step one, vessel growth. In a 2017 study from a Taiwanese group, the compound raised VEGFR2 at both mRNA and protein level in endothelial cells, increased vessel density in the chick chorioallantoic membrane assay and in endothelial tube formation, and sped blood-flow recovery in rat hind-limb ischaemia measured by laser Doppler [4]. The informative detail is what did not change: VEGF-A itself was not increased [4]. So the proposal is not more growth factor but a more responsive receptor. The same work showed the receptor being pulled into the cell, and that blocking endocytosis with dynasore abolished both the downstream signalling and the extra tube formation [4] (verified, in cells and rats).
88. Step two, how that receptor gets switched on without its usual ligand. The same group's 2020 follow-up worked in isolated rat aortic rings and human umbilical vein endothelial cells. Relaxation was concentration-dependent and reached about 48% in rings with intact endothelium but only about 19% with the endothelium stripped away, so the effect runs through the vessel lining rather than the muscle [5]. Blocking nitric oxide cut relaxation from about 38% to 10 to 12%. In cells, phosphorylation of Src, caveolin-1 and eNOS all rose, a Src inhibitor blunted them, and the binding between eNOS and caveolin-1 fell to about half of control, which is the step that frees the enzyme to make nitric oxide [5]. Nitric oxide signal rose about 1.35-fold (verified, in cells and isolated tissue).
89. Step three, tendon cells specifically. In fibroblasts taken from rat Achilles tendon, a gene expression screen put the growth hormone receptor among the most strongly upregulated genes after treatment, confirmed at mRNA and protein level, dose- and time-dependently. Adding growth hormone to treated cells then raised proliferation and activated JAK2, the receptor's normal downstream kinase [6] (verified, in rat cells). The proposal is sensitisation: the peptide does not act like growth hormone, it makes the cell listen harder to whatever growth hormone is present.
90. What that chain does not include is a dose-response relationship. Reviewing the rodent pharmacology across colonic fistula, NSAID-lesion and liver-injury models, the FDA's assessment was that the studies lacked established dose-response relationships and mechanistic understanding [2] (verified). That is the weakest joint in the mechanism story: effects are reported across a very wide dose span without the orderly increase that normally distinguishes a drug effect from an artefact.

### What the body does with it [animals]
91. One proper pharmacokinetic programme exists, published in 2022 by a group at Air Force Medical University in Xi'an, funded by Chinese national and provincial science foundations [7]. It is the source for nearly every number quoted elsewhere, so it is worth knowing what it actually did.
92. Rats received 20 micrograms per kilogram intravenously and 20, 100 or 500 micrograms per kilogram intramuscularly, plus a seven-day repeat arm and a tritium-labelled arm. Dogs received 6 micrograms per kilogram intravenously and 6, 30 or 150 intramuscularly, plus a seven-day repeat arm [7]. Elimination half-life was under 30 minutes in both species, and the FDA's reading of the data put it at 15.2 minutes in rats and 5.3 minutes in dogs [2,7]. Kinetics were linear across the tested range.
93. Bioavailability after intramuscular injection was about 14 to 19% in rats but 45 to 51% in dogs [7] (verified). That three-fold species gap is rarely mentioned and makes any confident extrapolation to humans unwise.
94. Distribution in rats peaked around one hour, highest in kidney, then liver, stomach wall, spleen and thymus; intestine, lung and skin tracked plasma; gonads, heart and skeletal muscle sat lower; brain and body fat were lowest, which suggests little crosses into the brain [7]. Over 72 hours, recovery was 15.9% in urine, 9.1% in bile, 2.3% in faeces, with about 54% still in the carcass [7]. The peptide breaks down quickly to smaller fragments and then to free amino acids that enter ordinary metabolism, with proline the main one [7] (verified).
95. No human pharmacokinetic data exist for oral, subcutaneous, nasal or transdermal use. In the rectal studies where plasma was measured at all, levels were undetectable or mostly below the limit of quantification [2] (verified). So there is no human exposure figure to anchor anything to.
96. For anyone subject to testing: metabolites are detectable in urine for four to five days by mass spectrometry, at limits of detection well below the threshold used for peptides in sport [8] (verified).

### The human record, in full, because it is short [people]
97. A 2025 systematic review in a peer-reviewed orthopaedic journal screened the literature and found 36 studies: 35 preclinical and one clinical [8]. That ratio is the single most honest summary of the evidence base.
98. One controlled trial exists. Reported only as a 2005 meeting abstract, it randomised 53 people with mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis one-to-one to a daily 80 mg enema or placebo for two weeks; 46 completed, with three withdrawals for adverse events on drug and two on placebo. Mean disease activity index change was minus 3.2 (95% CI minus 5.58 to minus 0.82) on drug against minus 1.6 (95% CI minus 3.86 to 0.67) on placebo, and the reported between-group difference was 1.6 with a confidence interval from minus 4.84 to 1.62, which crosses zero [2]. Reviewing it, the FDA found the abstract inadequate: no endpoint definition, no inclusion criteria, no statistical methods [2] (verified). This is the trial that gets cited as human evidence of benefit. It did not show one.
99. Everything else is uncontrolled. A 2021 retrospective chart review from a private wellness clinic in Orlando enrolled 17 people with varied chronic knee pain and reached 16 by telephone; 12 received the peptide alone, of whom 11 reported improvement, and 4 received it with thymosin beta-4, of whom 3 did. Follow-up was a phone survey administered by a college student volunteer, all outcomes were self-reported with no objective testing, only 4 of 16 had a knee MRI, and patients paid out of pocket [9] (verified). The authors declared no conflicts and reported no adverse events.
100. A 2024 case series gave 12 women with interstitial cystitis a single 10 mg intravesical injection during cystoscopy after they had failed standard therapy; 10 reported complete symptom resolution and 2 reported about 80% improvement, with no adverse events and no dropouts [10] (reported). Uncontrolled, self-reported, single-arm.
101. The remaining human exposure is small and mostly safety-oriented: a rectal enema up to 2 mg/kg daily for eight days in 24 healthy subjects, and an intravenous sequence of 10 mg then 20 mg in 2 healthy subjects [2]. No serious adverse events were reported in any of these, but the FDA noted the monitoring was unclear and the studies small and short [2]. A Phase 1 safety and pharmacokinetic trial registered in Mexico in 2015 for 42 healthy volunteers has never posted results and was reportedly withdrawn from submission in 2016 [2,8] (verified).
102. Put plainly: there is no established human dose. Nothing in the record establishes an effective dose, a dose-response relationship, or a safe long-term dose by any route people actually use. Every figure in circulation is either an animal dose converted by body weight, a single-procedure dose from one of the uncontrolled series above, or a community convention.

### Two trials that could change this, and how to read them [people]
103. A Phase 2 trial in acute hamstring strain is recruiting: randomised, placebo-controlled, quadruple-blind, one-to-one, 120 participants aged 18 to 45, daily subcutaneous dosing for 14 days against matching placebo, both arms on standardised rehabilitation, with assessments at days 3, 7, 14, 28 and 56 and recurrence follow-up three months after return to play. The co-primary endpoints are time to unrestricted sport and change in MRI-assessed injury volume at day 14 [11] (verified). That is a real design, and MRI volume is a hard endpoint.
104. One caveat belongs with it. Trial registration is self-submitted and is not peer review. The sponsor's peptide studies share a single site and identical listed start and primary completion dates across a cluster of records, and one record in that cluster carries "Mock Study" in its registered title [12] (reported). That is not evidence of wrongdoing by anyone, and it is not a reason to dismiss the trial. It is a reason to treat the registration as an announcement rather than a result, and to wait for reported data.
105. A Phase 1 trial at a US university medical centre is registered for recovery after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair: randomised, parallel, quadruple-masked, self-administered daily subcutaneous dosing for 90 days after surgery, against both matched saline and a no-treatment arm, with isometric shoulder strength by handheld dynamometer at baseline, 12 weeks and 5 months as the primary outcome. Enrolment is listed as 30 in the structured field and twenty in the plain-language summary; listed start is January 2027 [12,13] (verified). Neither trial has results. Until one does, the human efficacy question stays open.

### Safety: what has been looked at, and the shape of what has not [mixed]
106. Acute animal toxicity is reassuring as far as it goes. No lethal or toxic dose has been identified. Single intramuscular dosing gave no-observed-adverse-effect levels of 20 mg/kg in rats and 10 mg/kg in dogs, and gross and histological examination of major organs found no changes over six weeks in rats and dogs [2,8]. The genotoxicity battery was negative: Ames at 5 to 2000 micrograms per plate, chromosomal aberration in CHL cells at 100 to 400 micrograms per millilitre, and a mouse micronucleus assay at 25 to 100 mg/kg intramuscularly [2]. A rat teratogenicity study gave a no-effect level of 4 mg/kg [2] (verified).
107. The 28-day repeat-dose study is where the picture gets more textured. Rats received 0.2, 1.0 or 4.0 mg/kg daily and dogs 0.1, 0.5 or 2.0 mg/kg daily, all intramuscular [2,14]. Clotting time moved in opposite directions in the two species: shortened in rats, prolonged in dogs [2]. Rats showed raised ALT, glucose and triglycerides, and significant changes in mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration, platelet indices and red cell distribution width; the histopathology for that study was not included in the published manuscript [2]. In dogs, serum creatinine fell significantly in females at the top dose and in males at the middle dose and above [2] (verified). None of this is alarming on its own. It is also not a clean bill of health, and it is the opposite of the "no side effects in any study" claim that circulates.
108. The absences matter more than any finding. There are no carcinogenicity studies. There are no repeat-dose studies longer than 28 days, in any species. There are no acute or repeat-dose studies by the routes people actually use, oral, subcutaneous, nasal or transdermal. There are no peri- or post-natal developmental studies [2] (verified).
109. Human adverse event reporting is thin and hard to attribute. Three reports reached the FDA's surveillance system, all involving injectable products: injection-site redness and swelling in someone also injecting thymosin, shortness of breath with minimal detail, and hyperpigmentation with darkening of the gums that recurred on re-exposure to a combination product labelled for research purposes only. None could be attributed to this peptide alone [2] (verified).
110. Two theoretical concerns deserve to be named as theoretical. First, a mechanism that works by promoting new vessel growth is, in principle, a mechanism that could support tumour growth; no study has tested this, and one cancer-cachexia study found no significant effect on tumour size [1] (speculated). Second, an independent review noted that proline, the main metabolite, can generate reactive oxygen species through proline oxidase, and that sustained excess nitric oxide has its own downstream problems [1] (speculated). Both are reasoned arguments from known biology, not observations.

### Who produced the evidence, and why that is part of the evidence [lab]
111. Most of the animal literature traces to one group in Zagreb across three decades [1,2]. That is not an accusation; founding groups often dominate early literature on a compound they discovered. It does mean the usual safeguard of independent replication is largely absent, and that reading five papers does not necessarily mean reading five independent assessments.
112. The useful development is that independent groups have now entered. The angiogenesis and vasomotor mechanism work came from a Taiwanese university group with no connection to the original laboratory [4,5]. The pharmacokinetics came from a Chinese military medical university [7]. A 2025 literature and patent review came from an independent Polish academic group, and it drew the cancer and metabolite concerns above, which the original group then contested in a published comment-and-reply exchange in the same journal [1] (verified). Disagreement in print is a sign of a field becoming normal.
113. Independent does not mean disinterested. The authors of the 2025 orthopaedic systematic review disclosed relationships with several orthopaedic device companies, and the review itself contains internal inconsistencies in its study counts between text and tables [8] (verified). Worth knowing when quoting its numbers.
114. The patent landscape is scattered across many unrelated private parties, covering uses from acute respiratory distress to scar dressings to a sublingual combination with semaglutide [1]. No single company holds a development programme that would plausibly fund the trials needed for approval, which is part of why the evidence has stayed where it is.

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
115. No approved product exists in any country, and the substance appears in no major pharmacopoeia [2] (verified).
116. The US picture changed twice in 2026 and is easy to misread. In April, the FDA removed twelve peptide bulk substances from the compounding Category 2 list, effective 22 April 2026. That happened because the original nominators withdrew their nominations, not because the agency revised a safety judgement [15]. Removal from Category 2 did not place the substance in Category 1 or on the approved bulks list, so it sits in a gap: still an unapproved new drug, without even the Category 1 enforcement-discretion policy to rely on [15] (verified).
117. In July, the agency's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee met on the 23rd and 24th and voted 8 to 6 with one abstention to recommend adding it to the 503A bulks list, for ulcerative colitis, the indication the agency had reviewed [16,17]. The agency's own scientists had recommended against, finding the evaluation criteria weighed against both the free base and the acetate [2]. Their stated difficulty was identity before efficacy: these peptides have no universally accepted chemical definition, which makes evaluation very hard [16]. The committee also recommended KPV and TB-500 by the same margin [17].
118. What that vote is not: approval, a finding of benefit, or permission to compound. It is advisory. Adding a substance to the bulks list requires formal notice-and-comment rulemaking, which the agency has not begun and faces no deadline to begin; one attorney quoted at the time estimated eight to twelve months before pharmacies would have unambiguous authority [16,18] (verified).
119. The live development is an interim route. On 14 October 2026, the agency and its department sent an "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" to the White House budget office for regulatory review. As of mid-October it had not been issued or published, and its terms, including which peptides it covers and what conditions attach, are not public [18] (verified). The expected shape is enforcement discretion for prescription compounding by state-licensed 503A pharmacies, possibly with route restrictions, patient disclosures and adverse-event reporting attached, and it could be withdrawn without rulemaking [18] (reported). Pressure runs both ways: a drug-safety organisation filed a citizen petition in October 2026 asking the agency to stop compounding of untested peptides [19] (reported).
120. Elsewhere: prohibited in sport at all times under the non-approved substances class [2,20]. On the US Department of Defense prohibited ingredients list, so service members are told to avoid it by any route [20]. Not a controlled substance under US drug scheduling, which is why it is sold as a research chemical with not-for-human-consumption labelling [8,20]. A New Zealand committee proposed prescription classification in May 2023 [2]. The agency has separately cautioned against compounded products containing it, citing safety risks and possible contamination with other substances [20] (verified).

### Open questions [mixed]
121. What protein is it a fragment of? Until that is published, the claim that this is a natural human peptide rests on a 1993 assertion rather than a demonstrated biosynthetic pathway.
122. Is there a receptor? Every mechanism above is a downstream signalling observation. Without a binding target, there is no way to predict tissue selectivity, no structure-activity work, and no rational basis for dose.
123. Does any of it survive a controlled human trial? The hamstring trial's MRI endpoint is the first design capable of answering that for soft tissue. One negative result there would matter a great deal.
124. Why does the species gap in bioavailability exist, and which way do humans fall? Three-fold between rats and dogs, with no human data, is a wide gap to guess across.
125. What happens past 28 days? The entire long-term safety question is unexamined, in any species, by any route. That is the single largest hole, given that reported use runs in multi-week courses.
126. Does the pro-angiogenic mechanism carry an oncological risk? Unstudied. It cannot be ruled in or out from current data.
127. Is the material people obtain the material in the studies? Given unidentified impurities, absent endotoxin and aggregate testing, and a documented spread in independent batch results, this is arguably the most answerable open question, and the one most within reach of better testing rather than better trials.
