# KLOW
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/klow
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: KLOW blend, KLOW stack, KLOW 80, GLOW + KPV, GHK-Cu + BPC-157 + TB-500 + KPV
2. Origin: Blend of 4 peptides
3. Legal and approval status: Not approved anywhere. US: BPC-157, TB-500, KPV and injectable GHK-Cu left FDA Category 2 in April 2026 when nominations were withdrawn; non-injectable GHK-Cu sits in Category 1; the July 2026 panel voted for BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV compounding, no rule yet. WADA: BPC-157 and TB-500 banned at all times; KPV under S0.

## Summary
4. KLOW combines GHK-Cu (a copper skin peptide), BPC-157, TB-500 and KPV, the anti-inflammatory tail of alpha-MSH. It is used for skin, repair and gut inflammation: GHK-Cu signals collagen, BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4 support healing, and KPV calms inflammation inside cells. Evidence comes from the separate components and user reports of calmer gut and skin. No study of the blend exists, and GHK-Cu can cause itching welts.
5. Punch line: GLOW plus KPV. Copper peptide for skin, BPC-157 and TB-500 for repair, KPV to calm gut inflammation.
6. Used for: Skin, gut and inflammation, plus repair

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
7. Skin & aesthetics: Copper peptide for collagen; KPV calms inflamed skin
8. Inflammatory signaling: KPV switches off NF-κB inside cells
9. Tissue repair: BPC-157 and TB-500 for wound and tendon repair
10. Digestive & gut: KPV and BPC-157 aimed at gut lining and colitis
11. Hair (backed only by animal or user reports): Hair and nail gains reported
12. Immunity (backed only by animal or user reports): KPV steadies mast cells and immune cells
13. Pain & sensory (backed only by animal or user reports): Joint comfort reported

## How it works
14. Signals skin to build collagen · GHK-Cu: copper delivery · role Skin & aesthetics
   GHK-Cu binds copper and carries it into tissue that is rebuilding, where it signals fibroblasts, the skin's collagen-making cells. That shifts the balance toward new collagen and elastin and matrix remodelling. The best data are topical skin studies in people; injected use is not studied in humans.
15. Blocks a key inflammation switch inside gut and immune cells · KPV: PepT1 uptake, NF-κB down · role Inflammatory signaling
   KPV, the last three amino acids of alpha-MSH, enters gut and immune cells through PepT1, a peptide transporter. Inside, it blocks activation of NF-κB, a master switch for inflammatory genes, without needing the tanning receptor. Colitis improved in mouse studies; there are no human data.
16. Helps blood vessels grow into damage · BPC-157: VEGFR2, eNOS · role Tissue repair
   BPC-157 acts on VEGFR2 and eNOS, the receptor and enzyme that drive new blood vessel growth and nitric oxide release. Better blood supply brings oxygen and repair cells into damaged tendon, muscle or gut. This is shown in rat injury models, not in human trials.
17. Moves repair cells into wounds · Tβ4: G-actin sequestering · role Tissue repair
   Thymosin beta-4 binds single actin units (G-actin) and holds them in reserve. When a repair cell needs to move, that stock is released to build filaments fast, so cells migrate into the wound. Shown in cell and animal work; human wound trials used the full protein.
18. Protects the gut lining · BPC-157 and KPV, colitis models · role Digestive & gut
   BPC-157 protects the stomach and gut lining from damage, and KPV calms inflammation in the colon wall. In mouse and rat colitis models, the lining healed faster with each one alone. The two have never been tested together for gut effects.
19. Mechanism overview: KLOW keeps GLOW's logic (GHK-Cu for matrix and collagen gene expression, BPC-157 for angiogenesis, thymosin beta-4 for actin-driven migration) and adds KPV, alpha-MSH 11 to 13. KPV enters gut and immune cells through PepT1 and blocks NF-κB activation independent of MC1R. Chemically KPV is the quietest addition: no histidine, methionine or asparagine, so no copper binding or oxidation hot spot. The 5:1:1:1 ratio puts GHK-Cu at 62.5% of the powder; how the four behave together in liquid has not been studied.

## What has been tested
53. Digestive & gut: KPV in DSS and TNBS colitis (mouse)
54. Skin & aesthetics: GHK-Cu topical skin studies (human)
55. Tissue repair: BPC-157 and Tβ4 tissue repair (rat)
56. Skin & aesthetics: Rosacea, eczema, bloating (users)
57. Never tested as a blend; one aggregator states no preclinical or clinical study examines the four together. Component evidence: KPV in mouse colitis, GHK-Cu in skin studies, BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4 in animal repair. Powder COAs from two labs (Jul to Aug 2026) show each component close to label, with GHK-Cu up to 120%.
58. What it helps: Evidence is component-level and user-reported. KPV eases colitis in mice; GHK-Cu has skin data; BPC-157 and thymosin beta-4 have animal repair data. No study of the blend exists. Users report calmer gut, skin and rosacea plus recovery benefits, often within the first 2 weeks.

## Who shouldn't take it
59. Wilson's disease or copper overload: Each dose delivers copper; people who cannot clear copper accumulate it in liver and brain. (inferred)
60. People with active or recent cancer: Raises VEGFR2 signalling and new vessel growth in animal work; tumours use the same pathway. (inferred)
61. Drug-tested athletes: TB-500 is named on the WADA list as a thymosin beta-4 derivative. (verified)
62. People with IBD on biologics or immunosuppressants: KPV damps NF-kB signalling; stacking it onto immune drugs is untested. (inferred)

## Synergies (paired with)
63. GHK-Cu: GHK-Cu is already the largest part of KLOW by mass, so adding more mainly raises the skin and collagen share. People do it to push the skin side. The extra dosing has not been studied.
64. KPV: KPV is already in KLOW. Adding standalone KPV raises the gut and skin anti-inflammatory share over the repair parts, which some users want for colitis-type symptoms. KPV has mouse data only, and the pairing is user logic, not trial data.
65. BPC-157: BPC-157 is already in KLOW. Extra BPC-157 tilts the blend toward gut protection and blood vessel growth for injury repair. No study tests the blend with added BPC-157, so the pairing is user logic.
66. Thymosin Beta-4: Thymosin beta-4 is already in KLOW. Extra Tβ4 brings the cell migration share closer to standalone use for injury repair. A 2026 rat Achilles study found BPC-157 plus Tβ4 no better than either alone.
67. Larazotide: Larazotide tightens the seals between gut lining cells through the zonulin pathway, while KLOW's KPV and BPC-157 calm inflammation and protect the lining. People pair them for leaky gut symptoms. The pair has not been tested together.

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### What "never tested as a blend" actually means [mixed]
77. The phrase gets used as a mild caveat. It carries more than that, and it is worth unpacking into the three separate things that have not been examined.
78. First, pharmacology. Nobody has measured whether the four do the same things together that they do apart. Two of them act partly through the same pathway, nitric oxide signalling in the case of the gastric peptide, and matrix remodelling in the case of the copper tripeptide, so additive, redundant and opposing outcomes are all possible and none has been tested (verified as absent).
79. Second, chemistry in solution. The four sit in one vial, dissolved together, for the life of that vial. No stability study of the combination exists. What is known is that each component has its own documented degradation behaviour and its own storage requirement, and those requirements do not match (see below).
80. Third, safety attribution. Each injected component separately drew the same concern from US regulators in 2026: that peptide-related impurities and aggregates can drive immune responses, and that no immunogenicity studies exist for any of them [1,2,3] (verified). Combining four peptides in one solution multiplies the impurity and aggregate surface without adding any data about it (inferred).
81. None of that says the blend is dangerous. It says the blend is, in evidence terms, a fifth product with no file of its own.

### The four components do not have comparable evidence [mixed]
82. A blend tends to inherit the reputation of its best-regarded ingredient. Read component by component, the evidence here is not just thin, it is unevenly thin in ways that matter.
83. The copper tripeptide has the most human data of the four, and the most discouraging large result. Its pivotal programme in diabetic foot ulcers enrolled 511 patients across 30 centres at two strengths against placebo, and the drug was no better than placebo [4,5] (verified). Its positive human evidence is topical, small and short. The injected route, which is what a blend uses, has never been studied in humans at all.
84. The gastric peptide has exactly one controlled human trial, reported as a 2005 meeting abstract: 53 people with ulcerative colitis, a daily 80 mg enema against placebo for two weeks, with a between-group difference whose confidence interval crossed zero. Regulators found the abstract inadequate for lacking endpoint definitions, inclusion criteria and statistics [1] (verified). Everything else human is uncontrolled.
85. The thymosin fragment is the weakest of the four and the one most often assumed strongest. Regulators found no clinical studies, no human exposure data and no human pharmacokinetic data by any route, and no toxicology studies of any kind: no acute, no repeat-dose, no genotoxicity, no reproductive, no carcinogenicity [2] (verified). When the marketed fragment was tested directly in a fibroblast wound-closure assay at 50 micrograms per millilitre, it did not significantly promote closure [2].
86. The tripeptide from alpha-MSH has no human studies, no pharmacokinetic data in any species and no toxicology studies [3]. Its nomination cited nine references, eight animal studies of related compounds and one cadaver-skin study, none testing it in a living human [3] (verified). It was nominated only as a 0.1% topical cream, not for injection.
87. So the blend combines one component with a failed pivotal trial, one with a single inadequate and non-significant controlled trial, and two with no human data whatsoever.

### The identity problem, multiplied by four [lab]
88. Each of these substances exists in at least two forms, a free base and an acetate salt, with different molecular weights and, in two cases, different solubility and storage requirements. For each one, regulators found that nominators had been inconsistent about which form they meant, in some cases submitting a certificate of analysis for one form under the name of the other [1,2,3] (verified). None of the four has an approved generic name, so there is no reference standard to test a batch against, which regulators flagged as a patient safety issue in its own right.
89. One mismatch is specific to this blend and worth stating plainly. Regulators stated that the marketed seven-residue fragment and the full 43-residue protein are not the same substance [2] (verified). Retail versions of this blend commonly contain the full protein under the fragment's name. That is not a minor labelling quirk: it means the vial contains a molecule with a different evidence base, different stability and a different regulatory record from the one on the label, and that the component list cannot be used to look up what is actually present.
90. Independent batch testing of the blend is better than for most, and still mixed. Across one public testing service's classifiable results from May to September 2026, 9 of 15 passed. Powder certificates from two laboratories in July and August 2026 showed each component near its label figure, with the copper tripeptide running as high as 120% of label. One certificate from an unnamed laboratory failed its own stated specifications while recording the conclusion "complies" (verified, as logged in the card record).
91. The copper component being the one that runs over label is the least convenient result, since it is the largest share of the powder by a wide margin and the component carrying a metal.

### The chemistry one vial has to satisfy at once [lab]
92. This is where documented facts about the separate components say something concrete about the combination.
93. Storage requirements conflict. The gastric peptide's lyophilised powder is recommended below minus 18 degrees Celsius, with reconstituted solution documented as stable only two to three weeks refrigerated [1]. The thymosin fragment is stored below minus 20 as free base and below minus 15 as acetate [2]. The alpha-MSH tripeptide is reported stable up to three years at minus 20 as free base, while its acetate's stated storage is ordinary refrigeration at 2 to 8 degrees [3] (verified). A single blended vial can only be stored one way, so at least one component is being held outside its documented condition, and after reconstitution the shortest-lived component sets the real shelf life of the whole vial (inferred).
94. Solubility sets a constraint that can be checked with arithmetic. At the common composition, 10 mg of the alpha-MSH tripeptide in a 3 mL reconstitution is about 3.3 mg per millilitre. The documented water solubility of its free base is about 0.70 mg per millilitre; its acetate dissolves to about 5 mg per millilitre [3]. On those figures the free base could not fully dissolve at that concentration and the acetate could, which means a blend that reconstitutes clearly at 3 mL is almost certainly built on the acetate salt (inferred, assuming the cited water-solubility figures carry over into the blended solution).
95. Degradation is predictable for at least one component: under forced conditions the alpha-MSH tripeptide acetate cyclises to a diketopiperazine [3] (verified). A degraded vial therefore does not simply contain less of that component, it contains a different molecule, and nothing in the record describes what that does alongside three other peptides and a copper complex.
96. The copper itself is the variable with no file. An injected copper complex raises questions a plain peptide does not: how much copper is delivered, where it goes once the peptide is degraded, and what repeated dosing does to copper handling. Nothing in the available record addresses this for the single agent, let alone in a mixture (verified as absent).

### What a blend costs you in information [mixed]
97. There is a practical argument for blends, which is convenience, and a practical cost, which is attribution. Both deserve stating honestly.
98. If something improves, four components could be responsible, and the two with no human data at all are as likely candidates as the two with some. If something goes wrong, the same ambiguity applies in reverse. The reported adverse effects of this blend map neatly onto single components, stinging and welts to the copper share, nausea to the gastric peptide, with first-week tiredness and headache unattributed (reported), which suggests the ingredients are not masking each other, but it also means nobody using the blend learns which component they actually respond to.
99. That matters more here than it would for a fixed-dose approved combination, because the ratio is fixed by the vendor rather than by evidence. At the common composition the copper tripeptide is roughly five parts to one of each other component, which makes this, in mass terms, mostly a copper peptide product with three additions. Nothing in the literature establishes that this ratio is the right one for any purpose; no study of any ratio exists (verified as absent).
100. A regulator's framing of the general problem fits here: for each of these substances individually, the agency's difficulty was identity before efficacy, because these peptides lack a universally accepted chemical definition, which makes evaluation very hard [6]. A four-component mixture of four such substances inherits all four of those problems simultaneously.

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
101. No approved product contains this combination anywhere, and the four components sit in three different regulatory positions, which is unusual and makes blanket statements wrong.
102. The gastric peptide, the thymosin fragment and the alpha-MSH tripeptide were each removed from the US do-not-compound category in April 2026, because nominations were withdrawn rather than because of any revised safety finding, which left them outside any recognised category rather than cleared [7] (verified). On 23 July 2026 the advisory committee recommended all three for the 503A bulks list by 8 to 6 with one abstention each, against the agency's own staff, who had proposed not adding any of them [1,2,3,8]. Those recommendations are advisory, require formal rulemaking that has not begun, and carry no deadline [9].
103. The copper tripeptide is in a different position again. Its non-injectable form was restored to Category 1 on 5 May 2026 after a nominator clarified it had meant to withdraw only the injectable nomination, and it sits there under temporary enforcement discretion. The injectable route was left unclassified, and has not yet been reviewed by the committee at all; that review is expected before the end of February 2027 [7,9] (verified).
104. So an injectable blend of all four draws on three substances recommended but not yet permitted, plus one whose injectable form has never been assessed. An "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" went to the White House budget office on 14 October 2026 and was unpublished as of mid-October, with its covered substances and conditions not public [9] (verified). Until it appears, nobody can say whether a blend of this kind would fall inside it.
105. In sport, two of the four are prohibited at all times, and the other two fall under catch-all provisions rather than outside the rules.

### Open questions [mixed]
106. Does the combination do anything the components do not? No study of any kind has addressed this, which makes the central premise of the product untested.
107. Which salt forms are present, and does the vial contain the fragment or the full protein? Regulators state those are different substances. Without identity testing that distinguishes them, the label cannot answer this.
108. What is the real shelf life after reconstitution? The components' documented stability windows differ and the shortest one governs, but nobody has measured the mixture.
109. What happens to the copper over repeated dosing? Unstudied for the single agent and unexamined in combination, and it is the largest component by mass.
110. Why is the copper component the one that runs over label in independent testing, and is that a weighing, a filling or a measurement artefact? This is answerable by testing.
111. If a user responds, to what? The fixed ratio and four simultaneous components make individual attribution impossible by design, which is the quiet cost of every blend.
112. Would a blend fall within the interim compounding policy once published? Unknowable until it is published, and the four components' differing positions make it genuinely uncertain.
