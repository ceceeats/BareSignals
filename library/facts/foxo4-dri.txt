# FOXO4-DRI
Page: https://baresignals.co/library/#/p/foxo4-dri
Checked against Library v68, exported 2026-10-25

## Identity
1. Also called: foxo4 senolytic proxofim, FOXO4 D-retro-inverso peptide, FOXO4-p53 interfering peptide
2. Origin: Lab-designed
3. Sequence: LTLRKEPASEIAQSILEAYSQNGWANRRSGGKRPPPRRRQRRKKRG (46 amino acids). Built from D amino acids in reverse order (retro-inverso), so the body’s enzymes can’t cut it. Sequence reported; verify before release
4. Legal and approval status: Research chemical; no clinical program. Experimental and unproven in humans.

## Summary
5. FOXO4-DRI is a lab-designed peptide built from D-amino acids in reverse order, so enzymes cannot cut it. It is meant to clear aged senescent cells by blocking the FOXO4 and p53 interaction that keeps them alive. In aged mice it restored fur, fitness and kidney function, but there is no human data at all, and the few grey-market users report only anecdotal, inconsistent effects.
6. Punch line: The senolytic peptide. Breaks the FOXO4 and p53 bond keeping aged cells alive; old mice regained fur, fitness.
7. Used for: Senescent cell clearance

## Roles (what it is used for, strongest first)
8. Aging & senescence: Clears senescent cells in old mice
9. Organ function: Restored kidney function in mice
10. Muscle & performance: Better fitness in treated mice

## How it works
11. Frees p53 to kill senescent cells · Blocks FOXO4 and p53 binding · role Aging & senescence
   In senescent cells, the transcription factor FOXO4 holds p53 in the nucleus and keeps the damaged cell alive. FOXO4-DRI is a decoy of the FOXO4 region that grips p53, so it competes for p53 and frees it. p53 then moves to the mitochondria and triggers apoptosis, shown in cell studies and mice.
12. Built so enzymes cannot cut it · Retro-inverso D-peptide · role Aging & senescence
   It is a retro-inverso peptide: mirror-image D-amino acids in reversed order. That keeps the shape that binds p53 while making it unreadable to the protease enzymes that digest normal peptides. A stretch of charged residues helps carry it into cells. No pharmacokinetic study in any species has been published.
13. Restored kidney function in aged mice · Senolysis, renal · role Organ function
   In aged and fast-ageing mice, clearing senescent cells with this peptide restored kidney function in the original 2017 study. Removing these cells also removes the inflammatory signals they secrete into nearby tissue. The effect sizes were not reported in the abstract, and there is no human data at all.
14. Restored fur and fitness in aged mice · Senescent cell clearance · role Muscle & performance
   The same mouse study reported restored fur density and running fitness in naturally aged and fast-ageing mice. A later human cartilage cell study found clearance worked, but the surviving cells did not regain their function. No large independent replication and no human trial exist.
15. Mechanism overview: In senescent cells, the transcription factor FOXO4 binds p53 and holds it in the nucleus, which keeps these damaged cells alive instead of letting them die. FOXO4-DRI is a decoy built from the FOXO4 region that contacts p53. It competes for p53 and frees it, and p53 moves to the mitochondria, where it triggers apoptosis. Healthy cells rely less on FOXO4 and are largely spared. The peptide is retro-inverso: D-amino acids in reversed order, so proteases cannot cut it. Clearing senescent cells removes the inflammatory signals they secrete.

## Where it comes from
42. A designed 'senolytic' peptide: built from D-amino acids in reverse order (retro-inverso) so enzymes cannot cut it. It blocks the FOXO4-p53 interaction that keeps aged 'senescent' cells alive.
43. First studied for: Clearing senescent cells, to reverse aspects of aging; from a 2017 Dutch study.
44. Name story: DRI means D-retro-inverso: a FOXO4 fragment rebuilt from mirror-image D-amino acids in reversed order, so it keeps the original's shape but enzymes can't digest it. Debuted in Cell in 2017 as a decoy that frees p53 to kill senescent cells. [S6] (verified)
45. 2017: Dutch mouse study: senolytic peptide restores aged mice
46. Today: Experimental senolytic; no human trials or clinical program

## Deep read (as of 2026-10)

### Where it came from, and what the original paper actually claimed [animals]
47. It came out of one 2017 paper from a Dutch group, which identified FOXO4 as what they called a pivot in senescent cell viability, designed a peptide to break the FOXO4 to p53 interaction, and showed that this pushes p53 out of the nucleus in senescent cells and triggers their self-destruction [1] (verified).
48. The paper's three in-vivo claims are worth stating precisely, because they are usually compressed into "it rejuvenated old mice". It neutralised the tissue damage caused by doxorubicin, a chemotherapy drug; and in both fast-ageing XpdTTD mice and naturally aged mice it restored fitness, fur density and kidney function [1] (verified). The abstract reports no effect sizes, and the full paper could not be opened for this review, so the magnitudes behind those three words are not quoted here.
49. The design is the cleverest part. It is a retro-inverso peptide: mirror-image D-amino acids in reversed order, which keeps the shape that binds p53 while making it unreadable to the enzymes that would otherwise digest it, plus a stretch of positively charged residues to carry it into cells [2] (verified from the published sequence). That is why it is injected rather than infused as a protein, and why it is not comparable to ordinary peptides on stability.

### The selectivity argument, and the one study that tested it carefully [cells]
50. The claim that healthy cells are spared rests on them depending less on FOXO4 to keep p53 in check. A 2021 study from a Pittsburgh group is the most careful public test of that in human cells, and it is worth reading because it both supports and limits the claim.
51. Human chondrocytes from eight donors were expanded in culture until over 40% were senescent by beta-galactosidase staining, against under 17% in minimally expanded controls. Treatment at 25 micromolar for five days removed more than half the heavily expanded cells while leaving the minimally expanded population essentially unchanged, with caspase staining confirming apoptosis. Senescent cells fell to under 5%, and p16, p21 and p53 protein dropped [3] (verified, human cells).
52. Then the part that is rarely quoted. The surviving cells did not regain their ability to make cartilage: in three-dimensional pellet culture, pretreatment did not increase matrix deposition or cartilage gene expression, and it reduced SOX9 and COL2, two central cartilage genes. p21 protein rose in pretreated groups, which the authors read as possible stress on the remaining cells. They concluded that removing senescent cells was not enough to restore function and that other strategies would be needed [3] (verified).
53. That is the most informative result in the whole literature for anyone thinking about repair: clearance worked, and clearance alone did not restore the tissue's job.

### What else has been published since [animals]
54. The peptide has been used as a research tool across a scattered set of animal and cell models: senescence in kidney transplantation, Leydig cells and testosterone in aged mice, radiosensitisation and radiation-induced lung fibrosis, bleomycin lung fibrosis, spermatogenesis in aged mice, keloid fibroblasts, and the p53 transactivation domain as a target [2] (verified that these studies exist; their results are not summarised here because the primary texts were not opened).
55. A 2025 study reported that injections suppressed aortic ageing and improved aortic function in naturally aged and progeroid mice, and reduced senescence in endothelial cells deprived of oxygen and glucose, with the proposed chain running from blocked FOXO4 binding to nuclear exclusion of phosphorylated p53 to BAX and caspase-3 activation [4] (reported; dose and route were not stated in the accessible summary).
56. What is missing from this list is a large independent replication of the 2017 ageing result by a group with no connection to the original, and any human study of any kind.

### Safety: what is known, and the structural reason to be careful [animals]
57. The 2017 paper described the peptide as well tolerated in vivo under the conditions tested [1] (verified as stated, with those conditions unspecified in the abstract). That is the extent of the published safety record. No repeat-dose toxicology, genotoxicity, reproductive or carcinogenicity studies have been published, and no pharmacokinetic study in any species was found for this review.
58. The mechanism itself sets the main caution, and it is worth being explicit rather than vague. The peptide works by freeing p53 to kill a cell. p53 is the body's main brake on cancer and also a central damage-response switch in healthy tissue. The selectivity argument is that healthy cells do not need FOXO4 to restrain p53, which the chondrocyte work supports in one cell type at one concentration [3]. Whether that holds across tissues, over repeated courses, and in people with existing damage has not been tested (inferred).
59. The chondrocyte study's incidental finding points the same way: the cells that survived treatment showed raised p21, a stress marker [3] (verified, cells). Not a harm, but not nothing either.
60. There is no established human dose. Figures circulating in self-experimentation circles are extrapolations from a mouse dose, and nothing in the published record supports any of them.

### Who is developing it, and why that has stalled [lab]
61. A company was formed to commercialise the Dutch group's work and is still described as a preclinical operation, with its focus reported as the FOXO4 to p53 interaction more broadly rather than this particular peptide, and candidate indications under selection across chronic diseases, a rare disease and resistant tumours [4,5] (reported).
62. The commentary around it makes an economic point that explains the plateau better than any scientific one: a peptide whose sequence is public and hard to protect struggles to attract the money a proper trial costs, so it stays a tool compound while a patentable successor is sought [4] (reported). Nine years after the original paper, that is still where it sits.

### Where the law stands, October 2026 [people]
63. Not approved anywhere, no investigational new drug programme announced, no registered clinical trial found. Material sold under this name is a research chemical.
64. It is not named on the WADA Prohibited List; as a substance with no approval for human therapeutic use it falls under the S0 class, prohibited at all times (inferred from the class definition).

### Open questions [mixed]
65. Does clearance help, if the cells left behind do not work better? The chondrocyte result says removal and restoration are different problems.
66. Does selectivity hold outside a dish and outside one cell type? This is the question the entire safety case depends on, and it has one careful test behind it.
67. What happens with repeated courses? No repeat-dose study in any species has been published, in a mechanism that deliberately promotes cell death.
68. What are the pharmacokinetics? A D-amino acid retro-inverso peptide should be unusually long-lived, which cuts both ways, and nobody has published the numbers.
69. Why has nobody replicated the 2017 ageing result independently? Nine years is a long time for a widely cited finding to stand on one paper.
