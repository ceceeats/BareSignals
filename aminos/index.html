Twenty-one bare amino acids. Chained in the right order, they become peptides and proteins: bare signals that tell the body what to do. A walk in three parts: the letters, the build, the fold.

Part one · The letters

The 21 amino acids tap one to open its card, tap again to close

Think of this as the periodic table of life. Every peptide and protein in your body is spelled from these 21 letters, in different orders and lengths. Each tile is one amino acid: tap it to see its structure, its weight and what it does in the body.

How to read a tile
AlanineAla · 89A 12345
  1. Alanine is the full name. Say it AL-uh-neen. Every card below shows how to say its name.
  2. Ala is the three-letter code, the short name scientists write. 89 is its weight: 89 g/mol. Glycine is the lightest at 75, tryptophan the heaviest at 204.
  3. The squares count the codons that spell it. Alanine has four; methionine and tryptophan have only one.
  4. The big faded A is the one-letter code. It is the letter used to write every sequence on this page.
  5. The stripe shows what it is known for in the body, in Bare Signals colours. Small marks in the top corner show essential or not, charge, rings and sulfur; the key is under the grid.

When each letter was found

Asparagine came first, from asparagus juice in 1806. It took 129 years to find all twenty; the twenty-first only turned up in 1974.

The peptide. Each link is a peptide bond.

One continuous chain. N is the amino end, where the first amino acid still has its free nitrogen group (NH₂). C is the carboxyl end, where the last one still has its free acid group (COOH). The ribosome always builds from N to C, adding each new link at the C end. Long chains fold into shapes; short ones often stay loose.

Part three · The fold

Then it folds

As chains grow longer, most become proteins. Stretches of it coil into helices like this one, others pleat into sheets, and the whole chain packs into a working shape.

An alpha helix turns every 3.6 amino acids.

Proteins you already know

Glossary · 33 terms
Amino acid
One of the 21 building blocks of every peptide and protein. All share the same backbone; only the side chain changes. That one small difference decides whether it loves water, carries a charge or bends the chain.
Peptide
A short chain of amino acids, usually under about 50. Many work as messengers, carrying a signal from one tissue to another. Small enough to be built in a lab one link at a time.
Protein
A long chain that folds into a working 3D shape. The shape is the job: enzymes, muscle fibres, antibodies, hormones. Your body runs on tens of thousands of different ones.
Residue
One amino acid once it sits inside a chain. It lost a little water when it joined, so it weighs slightly less than on its own. Chains are measured in residues.
Peptide bond
The link between two amino acids. A water molecule leaves each time one forms. Flat and stiff, which is part of why chains fold the way they do.
Backbone
The repeating N, C, C spine every amino acid shares. Navy in the drawings. It is the same in every chain on earth; only the side chains hanging off it change.
Side chain (R)
The part that makes each amino acid different. Orange in the drawings. It can be a single hydrogen, as in glycine, or a double ring, as in tryptophan.
N end, C end
The two ends of a chain. N keeps a free nitrogen group, C a free acid group. Chains are always built and written from N to C.
DNA
The gene, written in four letters: A, T, C, G. Every cell carries the full set of about 20,000 protein recipes. It stays in the nucleus and is never used directly.
mRNA
The working copy of a gene, sent out to be read. T becomes U. Short-lived on purpose, so the cell can switch a recipe off quickly.
Codon
Three mRNA letters that call for one amino acid. Four letters in threes give 64 codons for 21 amino acids, so most amino acids have several spellings.
Stop codon
UAA, UAG or UGA. Calls for no amino acid; it tells the ribosome the chain is done. Without one, the ribosome keeps reading.
Ribosome
The cell’s machine that reads mRNA and links amino acids. Its core is made of RNA, not protein. It adds a few links per second, one at the C end.
tRNA
The carrier that brings each amino acid to the ribosome. One end reads a codon, the other holds the matching amino acid. The cell’s translator in a single molecule.
Essential
Your body can’t make it, so it has to come from food. Nine amino acids are essential for adults. Leucine, the muscle-building switch, is one.
Conditionally essential
Your body makes it, but not always enough under stress, growth or illness. Arginine and glutamine are classic examples. Need rises when repair work rises.
BCAA
Branched-chain amino acids: leucine, isoleucine, valine. Their side chains fork like a Y. Unusually, they are burned mainly in muscle rather than the liver.
g/mol
Grams per mole, the standard unit for molecular weight. A mole is a fixed count of molecules, so the number tells you how heavy one molecule is compared with another.
Monoisotopic mass
Weight counting only the most common form of each atom. Labs measuring peptides by mass spectrometry often report this one. It reads slightly lower than average weight.
D amino acid
The mirror image of the natural form, like a left and right hand. The body’s enzymes struggle to cut it. Swapping one in makes a peptide last longer.
Aib
A lab-made amino acid with two methyl groups where a hydrogen would be. Enzymes can’t grip it, so the chain survives. Semaglutide uses one.
Acetylated start
A small cap on the N end. Protects the chain from enzymes that chew from the front. Thymosin Beta-4 carries one naturally.
Amide end
A cap on the C end. Same job from the other side: more stable, longer lasting. Many natural hormones end this way, oxytocin included.
Cyclic
A chain closed into a ring. Stiffer and harder to break down, with no loose ends for enzymes to start on. Some of the strongest peptides are rings.
Bioregulator
Very short peptides, 2 to 4 amino acids, studied mainly in Russia for tissue-specific effects. Each is named for the tissue it was modelled on. Most evidence is early.
Sequence
The order of amino acids in a chain, written N to C in one-letter codes. Change one letter and the shape, and the job, can change with it.
Folding
How a chain twists into its working shape: helices and sheets first, then the full 3D form. It happens in milliseconds to seconds. A misfolded protein can stop working or clump.
Lipidation
A fatty acid tail added so the peptide rides on albumin, the blood’s main carrier protein. Hidden there, it escapes the kidneys and lasts days instead of minutes.
Pyroglutamate
A starting E that curls into a ring. A natural cap on some peptides. It shields the N end the way an acetyl cap does.
Recoded codon
UGA usually means stop. With a special signal further along the mRNA, it calls for selenocysteine instead. The genetic code’s one widely used exception.
Nucleotide
The unit DNA and RNA are made of: a base, a sugar and a phosphate. The base is the letter, A, T, C, G or U. Two of them joined make NAD+.
Small molecule
A compound far smaller than a peptide, built from rings and atoms rather than links. Most pills are small molecules. Bromantane and 5-Amino-1MQ are two.
Coenzyme
A helper molecule an enzyme needs to do its job. It is reused over and over rather than used up. NAD+ is one of the busiest in the body.
