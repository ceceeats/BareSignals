FAQFrequently asked questions

Why does a Luer lock give fewer doses?

A Luer lock needle twists on, and its hub holds a little liquid. When you draw, the barrel fills to your line and the hub fills too, so you pull more from the vial than you read. That extra never comes out. An insulin syringe has the needle moulded in, so almost nothing is stuck.

Example: 10 mg in 2 mL, a 1,000 mcg (1 mg) dose. Hubs hold roughly 0.02 to 0.08 mL or more; this uses 0.05 mL.

Why is one dose 10 units on one syringe and 0.10 mL on another?

Insulin syringes are marked in units, where 100 units is 1 mL. Luer locks are marked in mL. So 10 units and 0.10 mL are the same amount of liquid, and the same dose of peptide. Read the scale printed on your own syringe.

Why does more liquid mean a bigger draw?

More liquid makes a weaker mix. 10 mg in 2 mL is 5,000 mcg per mL; in 4 mL it is 2,500 mcg per mL. A weaker mix needs a bigger draw for the same dose, which is easier to read on the syringe. The dose itself does not change.

Are there other syringe types than insulin and Luer lock?

Yes. For injecting, the common ones are:

Insulin syringe. Needle moulded in, marked in units, 0.3, 0.5 or 1 mL.
Luer lock. Needle twists on and locks, marked in mL.
Luer slip. Needle pushes on without a thread, so it can pop off. Marked in mL, same dead space issue as a Luer lock.
Tuberculin syringe. 1 mL marked in 0.01 mL steps, usually with a Luer slip or Luer lock tip, so it reads like a 1 mL Luer lock.
Low dead space syringes. The plunger tip reaches into the hub, so much less liquid is stuck. Made in both fixed needle and Luer styles.

Oral and catheter tip syringes exist too, but they are not for injecting. Match the printed scale on your own syringe to the math.

Which angle does the needle go in?

The angle follows the needle length and how much fat you have under the skin. It does not follow the syringe volume (0.3, 0.5 or 1 mL). Insulin syringes come with different needle lengths, printed on the pack.

Commonly taught: 4 to 6 mm straight in, 8 mm straight in or at 45° with a pinch, 12.7 mm at 45° with a pinch. General information, not medical advice. Check with whoever prescribed or taught you.

What is the gauge of your needle?

Bigger number, thinner needle. It changes comfort, not your math.

Glossary · 22 terms
Nanogram ng
One thousandth of a microgram. You see it on lab results, not on vials.
Microgram mcg · µg
One thousandth of a milligram. Said "MY-kro-gram". Also written µg: µ is the Greek letter mu, so it looks like "ug", but it is still read "microgram". Labels favour mcg because µg is easy to misread as mg.
Milligram mg
One thousandth of a gram. Vial sizes are in mg.
Gram g
A unit of weight. About one paperclip.
Milliliter mL
A unit of liquid volume. A 1 mL syringe holds 1 mL.
Unit
A mark on an insulin syringe. 100 units is 1 mL. Units measure volume, not strength.
Vial
The small glass bottle with a rubber stopper.
Compound
The dry powder in the vial. The label gives its total mg.
Diluent liquid · BAC water
The liquid you add. Bacteriostatic water is sterile water with 0.9% benzyl alcohol.
Solution
The compound dissolved in the diluent. This is what you draw.
Reconstitution
Adding the diluent to the powder so it dissolves into a solution.
Strength
How much compound sits in each mL of the solution, written as mcg per mL.
Dose
The amount of compound you want, in mcg. You choose it.
Draw
Pulling the solution into the syringe up to a line.
Insulin syringe
A thin syringe marked in units. The needle is fixed in.
Luer lock
A syringe marked in mL. The needle twists on and off.
Luer slip
Like a Luer lock, but the needle pushes on without a thread.
Dead space
Liquid stuck in the needle hub that never gets pushed out.
Gauge G
Needle width. Bigger number, thinner needle.
Subcutaneous
Under the skin, into the fat layer.
Pen
A prefilled injector. You dial the dose, screw on a short pen needle, prime, and press. No drawing up.
Pen needle 4 to 8 mm
A short, fine needle that screws onto a pen, thinner and shorter than most syringe needles.
